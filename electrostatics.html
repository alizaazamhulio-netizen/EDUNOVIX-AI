Topic 01

Electric Charge & Fundamental Properties

DEFINITION
Electric Charge
"Electric charge is the fundamental intrinsic property of matter by virtue of which it produces and experiences electrical and magnetic effects."
In simple words: Just as mass gives a body gravitational attraction, electric charge gives a body the ability to exert and experience electric forces on other charged objects.

There are two types of charges in nature: Positive Charge (carried by protons) and Negative Charge (carried by electrons). In an uncharged neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

Formula • Quantization of Charge
q = n · e
Where:
  • q = Total net charge on the body
  • n = An integer number of transferred electrons (n = ±1, ±2, ±3, ...)
  • e = Elementary magnitude of electronic charge = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
SI Unit: Coulomb (C) • 1 Coulomb = Charge carried by 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons

1. Quantization of Charge

Charge always exists in discrete integer multiples of the elementary charge e. A body cannot possess a fraction of an electron charge such as 0.5e, 1.7e, or 2.3e in an isolated state.

2. Conservation of Charge

The total algebraic sum of electric charge in an isolated system remains strictly constant. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another.

3. Additive Nature

Total charge of a system containing individual charges q₁, q₂, q₃... is simply their algebraic sum: q_total = q₁ + q₂ + q₃ + ... (taking their positive and negative signs into account).

4. Invariance of Charge

The magnitude of electric charge on a particle is independent of its speed or frame of reference. Unlike relativistic mass, charge does not change when moving close to the speed of light.

⚡ MDCAT High-Yield Tip
To find the number of electrons in 1 Coulomb of negative charge, simply compute:
n = q / e = 1 / (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Memorize this constant for lightning-fast calculations!
⚠️ Common Mistake
Do not assume that mass remains unchanged when a body is charged. When a body becomes negatively charged, it gains electrons, so its mass increases slightly. When a body becomes positively charged, it loses electrons, so its mass decreases slightly.
Topic 02

Coulomb's Law & Force Interactions

DEFINITION
Coulomb's Law
"The electrostatic force between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them."
In simple words: Double the charge, force doubles. Double the separation distance, force drops to one-fourth (inverse-square relationship).
Formula • Coulomb's Force in Vacuum & Medium
F = k · \frac{|q₁ · q₂|}{r²} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon₀\varepsilon_r} · \frac{|q₁ · q₂|}{r²}
Where:
  • F = Electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion (in Newtons, N)
  • q₁, q₂ = Magnitudes of the two interacting point charges (in Coulombs, C)
  • r = Distance of separation between the charges (in meters, m)
  • k = Coulomb's electrostatic constant = 1 / (4πε₀) ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
  • ε₀ = Permittivity of free space = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²)
  • ε_r = Relative permittivity (Dielectric constant) of the intervening medium (ε_r ≥ 1; for vacuum ε_r = 1, for air ε_r ≈ 1.0006)
SI Unit of Force: Newton (N) • SI Unit of k: N·m²/C² • SI Unit of ε₀: C²/(N·m²) or F/m

Effect of Dielectric Medium on Electrostatic Force

When an insulating medium (dielectric) of relative permittivity ε_r is inserted between two charges, the electric force is reduced due to polarization of the dielectric molecules:

F_{medium} = \frac{F_{vacuum}}{\varepsilon_r}
Since ε_r > 1 for all material media, electrostatic force is always maximum in vacuum and decreases in any dielectric.
Solved Example 1 • Coulomb's Law & Medium
Question: Two point charges of +2 μC and +8 μC are placed 20 cm apart in vacuum. (a) Calculate the force between them. (b) What will be the force if a dielectric slab with ε_r = 4 is inserted between them?
Given: q₁ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C, q₂ = 8 × 10⁻⁶ C, r = 20 cm = 0.2 m, k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², ε_r = 4
Required: Force in vacuum F_vac = ? and Force in medium F_med = ?
Formula: F_vac = k(q₁q₂) / r² and F_med = F_vac / ε_r
Substitution:
F_vac = [(9 × 10⁹)(2 × 10⁻⁶)(8 × 10⁻⁶)] / (0.2)² = (0.144) / 0.04 = 3.6 N
F_med = 3.6 N / 4 = 0.9 N
✓ Final Answer: Vacuum Force = 3.6 N (Repulsive); Medium Force = 0.9 N (Repulsive)
⭐ Must Remember: Vector Form of Coulomb's Law
Coulomb's force strictly obeys Newton's Third Law of Motion. The force exerted by q₁ on q₂ is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force exerted by q₂ on q₁:
F₁₂ = - F₂₁. Hence, electrostatic force is an action-reaction pair and acts along the line joining the centers of the two charges (central force).
Topic 03

Principle of Superposition

DEFINITION
Principle of Superposition
"When multiple charges are present, the total electrostatic force acting on any given charge is the vector sum of all individual forces exerted on it by the other charges independently."
F_{net} = F₁ + F₂ + F₃ + ... + F_n = \sum F_i
Key Concept: The force between any two charges is not affected by the presence of other surrounding charges.
Topic 04

Electric Field & Field Intensity

DEFINITION
Electric Field & Electric Field Intensity (E)
"Electric field is the region or space around an electric charge in which another charge experiences an electrostatic force. Electric Field Intensity (E) is the force experienced per unit positive test charge placed at that point."
In simple words: The electric field tells us the strength and direction of electrostatic push or pull at any point in space.
Formula • Electric Field of Point Charge
E = \frac{F}{q₀} = \frac{k · Q}{r²} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon₀} · \frac{Q}{r²}
Where:
  • E = Electric field intensity vector (N/C or V/m)
  • F = Electrostatic force on test charge q₀ (in Newtons)
  • q₀ = Infinitesimally small positive test charge (in Coulombs)
  • Q = Source charge producing the field (in Coulombs)
  • r = Distance from source charge to observation point (in meters)
SI Unit: Newton per Coulomb (N/C) or Volt per meter (V/m) • Dimensions: [M L T⁻³ I⁻¹] • Vector Quantity
Solved Example 2 • Electric Field Calculation
Question: Determine the electric field intensity at a point situated 30 cm away from an isolated point charge of +5 μC in air.
Given: Q = +5 × 10⁻⁶ C, r = 30 cm = 0.3 m, k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
Required: Electric field intensity E = ?
Formula: E = (k · Q) / r²
Substitution:
E = [(9 × 10⁹)(5 × 10⁻⁶)] / (0.3)² = (4.5 × 10⁴) / 0.09 = 5.0 × 10⁵ N/C
✓ Final Answer: E = 5.0 × 10⁵ N/C directed radially outward away from the positive charge.
Topic 05

Electric Field Lines & Physical Rules

DEFINITION
Electric Field Lines (Lines of Force)
"Electric field lines are imaginary continuous curves drawn in an electric field such that the tangent to the curve at any point gives the direction of the electric field intensity at that point."
Concept introduced by Michael Faraday to provide a visual map of the strength and direction of electric fields.

1. Origin and Termination

Field lines always originate from positive charges (+) and terminate on negative charges (−). For an isolated positive charge, they extend radially outward to infinity.

2. Tangent Gives Direction

A tangent drawn to an electric field line at any point indicates the exact direction of the electric force on a positive test charge placed at that location.

3. Never Intersect Each Other

Two field lines can never cross each other. If they did, there would be two tangents at the intersection point, meaning two distinct field directions at the same physical point, which is physically impossible.

4. Density Equals Strength

The number of field lines per unit cross-sectional area perpendicular to the lines is directly proportional to the magnitude of the electric field intensity. Closely spaced lines = Strong Field; Widely spaced lines = Weak Field.

5. Perpendicular to Conductors

Field lines always enter or leave the surface of a charged conductor at right angles (90°) in electrostatic equilibrium.

6. No Closed Loops

Electrostatic field lines never form closed loops because electrostatic fields are conservative in nature.

Topic 06

Electric Flux (Φ)

DEFINITION
Electric Flux (Φ)
"Electric flux is defined as the total number of electric field lines passing through a given surface area placed in an electric field."
Mathematically: It is the scalar dot product of the electric field vector E and the vector area A.
Formula • Electric Flux
\Phi = \vec{E} \cdot \vec{A} = E · A · \cos(\theta)
Where:
  • Φ = Electric flux (Scalar quantity)
  • E = Magnitude of electric field intensity (N/C)
  • A = Area of the surface (m²)
  • θ = Angle between the electric field vector E and the normal vector (vector area A) of the surface
SI Unit: N·m²/C or Volt·meter (V·m) • Scalar Quantity

Maximum Flux (Φ_max = EA)

Occurs when the surface is held perpendicular to the electric field lines. In this case, the normal area vector is parallel to E (θ = 0° ⟹ cos 0° = 1).

Zero Flux (Φ = 0)

Occurs when the surface is held parallel to the electric field lines. In this case, the normal area vector is perpendicular to E (θ = 90° ⟹ cos 90° = 0).

Negative Flux

When field lines enter a closed surface (θ > 90°), flux is negative (inward flux). When field lines leave a closed surface (θ < 90°), flux is positive (outward flux).

⚠️ Major MDCAT Angle Trap
Always check whether the question gives the angle with the surface plane or with the normal to the surface!
If a question says: "The plane of a surface makes an angle of 30° with the electric field", then the angle θ with the normal is θ = 90° - 30° = 60°. Thus \Phi = EA \cos(60°) = 0.5 EA.
Topic 07

Gauss's Law & Symmetrical Applications

DEFINITION
Gauss's Law
"The total electric flux passing outward through any closed imaginary surface (Gaussian surface) is equal to 1/ε₀ times the total net electric charge enclosed inside the surface."
Key Property: Gauss's law is independent of the size, shape, or geometric form of the closed surface.
Formula • Gauss's Law
\Phi_{total} = \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{enclosed}}{\varepsilon₀}
Where:
  • Φ_total = Total net electric flux passing through the closed Gaussian surface
  • Q_enclosed = Algebraic sum of all charges enclosed completely inside the surface
  • ε₀ = Permittivity of free space = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²)

Three Classic MDCAT Applications of Gauss's Law

1. Hollow Charged Conductor (Inside)

Inside a hollow charged conducting sphere of radius R, the enclosed charge is zero (Q_enc = 0).
Result: E_{inside} = 0
This is the scientific basis of Electrostatic Shielding (Faraday Cage).

2. Infinite Sheet of Charge

For an infinite plane sheet of charge with uniform surface charge density \sigma = Q / A:
Result: E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon₀}
Notice that the field is completely independent of distance from the sheet!

3. Between Two Oppositely Charged Plates

Between two parallel plates carrying equal and opposite charges (+\sigma and -\sigma):
Result: E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon₀}
(Fields from both plates add constructively between plates and cancel outside).

Topic 08

Electric Potential (V) & Potential Gradient

DEFINITION
Electric Potential (V)
"Electric potential at any point in an electric field is the work done per unit positive test charge in moving it from infinity to that point against the electrostatic force without producing any acceleration."
Formula • Electric Potential & Potential Gradient
V = \frac{W}{q₀} = \frac{k · Q}{r} \quad \text{and} \quad E = - \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r}
Where:
  • V = Electric potential at distance r (in Volts, V)
  • W = Work done by external agent (in Joules, J)
  • Q = Source charge (with algebraic sign included: positive for +Q, negative for −Q)
  • -ΔV/Δr = Potential Gradient (rate of change of potential with distance)
SI Unit of Potential: Volt (V) = Joule/Coulomb (J/C) • Scalar Quantity • 1 Electron-Volt (1 eV) = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
⚡ Crucial Direction Rule for Potential Gradient
The negative sign in E = - \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta r} indicates that electric field lines always point in the direction of decreasing electric potential.
• A positive charge naturally accelerates from high potential to low potential.
• A negative charge naturally accelerates from low potential to high potential.

Equipotential Surfaces & Potential Energy

Equipotential Surface

Any surface over which the electric potential is identical at all points (\Delta V = 0).
• Work done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface is ZERO (W = q\Delta V = 0).
• Electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.

Electric Potential Energy (U)

The work done to assemble a two-charge configuration from infinite separation:
U = \frac{k · q₁ · q₂}{r}
• Like charges: U > 0 (Repulsion, positive work required).
• Opposite charges: U < 0 (Attraction, stable bound state).

Topic 09

Capacitors, Capacitance & Dielectric Polarization

DEFINITION
Capacitor & Capacitance (C)
"A capacitor is a device designed to store electric charge and electrostatic potential energy. Capacitance (C) is the measure of a capacitor's ability to store charge per unit potential difference applied across its plates."
Formula • Capacitance & Parallel Plate Geometry
C = \frac{Q}{V}, \quad C_{vacuum} = \frac{\varepsilon₀ · A}{d}, \quad C_{medium} = \frac{\varepsilon_r · \varepsilon₀ · A}{d} = \varepsilon_r · C_{vacuum}
Where:
  • C = Capacitance (in Farads, F)
  • Q = Magnitude of charge on either plate (in Coulombs, C)
  • V = Potential difference between plates (in Volts, V)
  • A = Overlapping surface area of either plate (in m²)
  • d = Separation distance between the plates (in meters, m)
  • ε_r = Dielectric constant (relative permittivity) of the insulating medium
SI Unit: Farad (F) = 1 Coulomb/Volt • 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F • 1 nF = 10⁻⁹ F • 1 pF = 10⁻¹² F

How a Dielectric Increases Capacitance

When an insulating dielectric is inserted between capacitor plates, the external field aligns the molecular dipoles (Dielectric Polarization). This creates an internal induced electric field E_{ind} that opposes the original field E₀:

Net Electric Field Drops

E_{net} = \frac{E₀}{\varepsilon_r} (The electric field intensity is weakened by a factor of ε_r).

Potential Difference Drops

V = E_{net} · d = \frac{V₀}{\varepsilon_r} (Voltage decreases for a given stored charge Q).

Capacitance Increases

C = \frac{Q}{V} = \varepsilon_r · C₀ (Capacitance increases by exactly a factor of ε_r).

⭐ MDCAT Golden Table: Dielectric Insertion Effects

Physical Parameter Case A: Battery remains CONNECTED Case B: Battery DISCONNECTED (Isolated)
Capacitance (C) Increases to ε_r · C₀ Increases to ε_r · C₀
Potential Difference (V) Constant (V = V_battery) Decreases to V₀ / ε_r
Stored Charge (Q) Increases to ε_r · Q₀ Constant (Q = Q₀)
Electric Field (E) Constant (E = V/d) Decreases to E₀ / ε_r
Stored Energy (U) Increases to ε_r · U₀ (U = ½CV²) Decreases to U₀ / ε_r (U = Q²/2C)
Topic 10

Capacitor Combinations: Series & Parallel

Series Combination

Charge Q is Same
\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C₁} + \frac{1}{C₂} + \frac{1}{C₃} + ...
  • Charge: Same charge Q exists on every capacitor (Q₁ = Q₂ = Q_{total}).
  • Voltage: Potential divides across capacitors (V_{total} = V₁ + V₂ + ...).
  • Equivalent: C_{eq} is always less than the smallest capacitor in the series chain.
  • Shortcut for two capacitors: C_{eq} = \frac{C₁ · C₂}{C₁ + C₂}
  • Shortcut for n identical capacitors: C_{eq} = \frac{C}{n}

Parallel Combination

Voltage V is Same
C_{eq} = C₁ + C₂ + C₃ + ...
  • Voltage: Potential difference across every branch is identical (V₁ = V₂ = V_{supply}).
  • Charge: Total charge splits across branches (Q_{total} = Q₁ + Q₂ + ...).
  • Equivalent: C_{eq} is always greater than the largest individual capacitor.
  • Shortcut for n identical capacitors: C_{eq} = n · C
  • Usage: Used to maximize stored charge at a constant supply voltage.
Solved Example 3 • Series Network
Question: Three capacitors of capacitances 2 μF, 3 μF, and 6 μF are connected in series across a 12 V battery. Find: (a) Equivalent capacitance, (b) Charge on each capacitor, and (c) Potential difference across the 2 μF capacitor.
Given: C₁ = 2 μF, C₂ = 3 μF, C₃ = 6 μF, V = 12 V
Formulas: 1/C_eq = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + 1/C₃, Q = C_eq · V, V₁ = Q / C₁
Substitution:
1/C_eq = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = (3 + 2 + 1)/6 = 6/6 = 1 μF⁻¹ ⟹ C_eq = 1 μF
Total Charge Q = (1 μF)(12 V) = 12 μC (same on each capacitor in series)
Voltage across C₁: V₁ = Q / C₁ = (12 μC) / (2 μF) = 6 Volts
✓ Final Answers: C_eq = 1 μF; Charge = 12 μC on each; V₁ = 6 V, V₂ = 4 V, V₃ = 2 V (Total = 12 V)
Topic 11

Energy Stored in a Capacitor & Energy Density

DEFINITION
Energy Stored in Capacitor & Energy Density (u)
"The electrostatic potential energy stored in a capacitor is the total work done by the charging source in transferring charge from one plate to the other. Energy Density (u) is the electrostatic energy stored per unit volume of the dielectric field space."
Formula • Three Equivalent Forms of Stored Energy
U = \frac{1}{2} C V² = \frac{1}{2} Q V = \frac{Q²}{2 C} \quad \text{and} \quad u = \frac{1}{2} \varepsilon₀ \varepsilon_r E²
Where:
  • U = Total stored electrostatic energy (in Joules, J)
  • u = Energy density = U / (Area × d) (in J/m³)
  • E = Electric field intensity between the plates = V / d (in V/m)
Crucial Takeaway: Electrostatic energy is stored inside the electric field established in the space between the plates!
Topic 12

RC Circuits: Charging, Discharging & Time Constant (τ)

DEFINITION
Time Constant (τ = RC)
"The time constant (τ) of an RC circuit is the time required for the capacitor to charge up to 63.2% of its maximum equilibrium charge (q₀ = CV), or discharge down to 36.8% of its initial charge."

1. Time Constant Formula

\tau = R · C
SI Unit: [Ohm] × [Farad] = [Second (s)]
Dimensions: [M⁰ L⁰ T¹]

2. Charging Dynamics

q(t) = q₀ [1 - e^{-t / RC}]
• At t = 1τ: q = 0.632 q₀ (63.2% charged)
• At t = 5τ: q ≈ 0.993 q₀ (99.3% charged ≈ full)

3. Discharging Dynamics

q(t) = q₀ · e^{-t / RC}
• At t = 1τ: q = 0.368 q₀ (36.8% charge remaining)
• At t = 5τ: q drops below 1% (fully discharged)

Topic 16

MDCAT Solved Numericals Bank

Problem 1 • Charge Transfer
A metal sphere acquires a net negative charge of -4.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ C. How many excess electrons were transferred to the sphere?

Given: q = -4.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ C, e = -1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Formula: q = n · e ⟹ n = q / e

Substitution: n = (-4.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ C) / (-1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) = 3000 electrons

✓ Final Answer: 3000 excess electrons transferred
Problem 2 • Electric Flux
A uniform electric field of 2000 N/C passes through a flat square surface of side 10 cm. Calculate the electric flux if the surface plane makes an angle of 30° with the field lines.

Given: E = 2000 N/C, Area A = (0.1 m)² = 0.01 m²

Angle Note: The plane makes 30° with field ⟹ Angle with normal vector is θ = 90° - 30° = 60°

Formula: Φ = E · A · cos(θ)

Substitution: Φ = (2000 N/C)(0.01 m²) cos(60°) = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N·m²/C

✓ Final Answer: 10 N·m²/C (or 10 V·m)
Problem 3 • Potential Difference & Work
How much work is required to move a charge of +3 μC from a point at potential V_A = -20 V to a point at V_B = +60 V?

Given: q = +3 × 10⁻⁶ C, V_A = -20 V, V_B = +60 V

Formula: W = q · ΔV = q · (V_B - V_A)

Substitution: ΔV = 60 - (-20) = 80 V ⟹ W = (3 × 10⁻⁶ C)(80 V) = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ Joules

✓ Final Answer: 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ J (or 0.24 mJ)
Problem 4 • Parallel Plate Capacitance
A parallel plate capacitor has plate area 0.04 m² and plate separation 1.77 mm in vacuum. Calculate its capacitance.

Given: A = 0.04 m², d = 1.77 × 10⁻³ m, ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m

Formula: C = (ε₀ · A) / d

Substitution: C = [(8.854 × 10⁻¹²)(0.04)] / (1.77 × 10⁻³) = (0.354 × 10⁻¹²) / (1.77 × 10⁻³) = 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ F = 200 pF

✓ Final Answer: 200 pF (or 0.2 nF)
Problem 5 • Capacitor Stored Energy
A 100 μF capacitor is charged to 50 V. Calculate the stored energy and the total charge stored.

Given: C = 100 μF = 10⁻⁴ F, V = 50 V

Formula: U = ½ C V² and Q = C · V

Substitution: U = ½ (10⁻⁴ F)(50 V)² = ½ (10⁻⁴)(2500) = 0.125 J (125 mJ)
Q = (100 × 10⁻⁶ F)(50 V) = 5.0 × 10⁻³ C (5 mC)
