Introduction to Counting

What is Counting?

Counting is the branch of discrete mathematics (combinatorics) that allows us to determine the total number of possible outcomes, arrangements, or selections without listing every single possibility by hand. In high-stakes university entry tests like FAST-NUCES, NET, and scholarship exams, time is extremely limited: calculating possibilities directly using mathematical principles turns 10-minute brute-force questions into 15-second answers.

💡 Why Permutations & Combinations Matter in FAST Entry Tests
FAST entrance exams test your speed in identifying whether a problem is a selection or an arrangement. Candidates who memorize formulas blindly waste critical minutes, while students who recognize structural constraints solve them instantly.

A Real-Life Comparison

Consider 3 students: Ali, Bilal, and Dawood.

  • Choosing a 2-person project team: Choosing Ali & Bilal is the exact same team as choosing Bilal & Ali. The order does not produce a new team.
  • Assigning 1st and 2nd rank in class: Ali coming 1st and Bilal coming 2nd is completely different from Bilal coming 1st and Ali coming 2nd. The order creates a distinct outcome.
⭐ PERMUTATION
ORDER MATTERS

Used when the arrangement, sequence, or position creates a unique outcome. Examples include seatings, passwords, rankings, phone numbers, and distinct roles.

⭐ COMBINATION
ORDER DOES NOT MATTER

Used when we are simply selecting a collection, group, or subset. Examples include committees, poker hands, choosing pizza toppings, lottery numbers, and handshakes.

Fundamental Counting Principle

Before learning complex permutation and combination formulas, you must master the fundamental building block of all counting: the Multiplication Rule and the Addition Rule.

The Fundamental Principle of Multiplication
Total Ways = m × n × p × ...
If an operation can be performed in m ways, and for each of these a second operation can be performed in n ways, then the two operations in sequence can be performed in m × n ways.

8 Solved Fundamental Examples

Example 2.1 • Clothing Outfits
A student has 4 distinct shirts, 3 pairs of trousers, and 2 pairs of shoes. How many complete outfits can they wear?
Step 1: Shirts choices = 4
Step 2: Trousers choices = 3
Step 3: Shoes choices = 2
Calculation: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24
Final Answer: 24 different outfits.
Example 2.2 • Password Formation
A password is made of 1 uppercase letter followed by 3 numeric digits (0–9). If repetition of digits is allowed, how many passwords can be made?
Letter slot: 26 choices (A to Z)
Digit slots: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 choices
Calculation: 26 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 26,000
Final Answer: 26,000 passwords.
Example 2.3 • Travel Routes
There are 3 roads from City A to City B, and 4 roads from City B to City C. How many different round trips from A to C (via B) are possible if the return trip must use different roads?
Going A → B → C: 3 × 4 = 12 routes
Returning C → B → A (different roads): 3 remaining roads from C to B, 2 remaining from B to A = 3 × 2 = 6
Calculation: 12 × 6 = 72
Final Answer: 72 round trip routes.
Example 2.4 • Food Menu Selection
A university cafeteria combo offers a choice of 1 burger from 4 options, 1 side from 3 options, and 1 drink from 5 options. How many distinct meals can be chosen?
4 × 3 × 5 = 60
Final Answer: 60 meal combinations.
Example 2.5 • Digit Number Formation
How many 3-digit numbers can be formed using digits 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 without repeating any digit?
Hundreds digit: 5 choices (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
Tens digit: 4 remaining choices
Units digit: 3 remaining choices
5 × 4 × 3 = 60
Final Answer: 60 three-digit numbers.
Example 2.6 • Multiple Choice Test Outcomes
An entry test has 5 multiple-choice questions, each with 4 options. How many total ways can a student answer all 5 questions?
4 × 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 = 4^5 = 1,024
Final Answer: 1,024 answer keys.
Example 2.7 • Binary String Code
How many 8-bit binary bytes (strings of 0s and 1s) can be formed?
2^8 = 256
Final Answer: 256 bytes.
Example 2.8 • Seating Empty Slots
3 people enter an auditorium with 7 empty seats in a row. How many ways can they be seated?
Person 1: 7 seat choices
Person 2: 6 seat choices
Person 3: 5 seat choices
7 × 6 × 5 = 210
Final Answer: 210 seating arrangements.

Factorial Notation

For any non-negative integer n, the factorial of n (written as n!) is defined as the product of all positive integers less than or equal to n:

Factorial Definition
n! = n × (n - 1) × (n - 2) × ... × 3 × 2 × 1
Special Fundamental Identity: 0! = 1 (by definition of the empty product and to preserve algebraic consistency in formulas).
n Formula Expansion Value FAST Exam Note
0! Defined by algebraic convention 1 Frequently tested in trick questions!
1! 1 1 Base unit
2! 2 × 1 2 2! = 2
3! 3 × 2 × 1 6 Arranging 3 objects
4! 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 24 Arranging 4 objects
5! 5 × 24 120 Unit digit becomes 0 for all n ≥ 5
6! 6 × 120 720 Very common in word problems

Factorial Simplification & Cancellation

In entry exams, calculating huge products like 10! or 12! directly is a disastrous time trap. Always rewrite the larger factorial in terms of the smaller factorial and cancel immediately.

⚡ FAST Shortcut: The Factorial Peeling Trick
n! = n × (n - 1) × (n - 2)!
Whenever you see a fraction like 8! / 6!, "peel" the top: (8 × 7 × 6!) / 6! = 8 × 7 = 56.

12 Solved Simplification Examples

Example 4.1
Simplify: 8! / 6!
(8 × 7 × 6!) / 6! = 8 × 7 = 56
Answer: 56
Example 4.2
Simplify: 10! / 8!
(10 × 9 × 8!) / 8! = 10 × 9 = 90
Answer: 90
Example 4.3
Simplify: 12! / 9!
12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320
Answer: 1,320
Example 4.4
Simplify: 7! / (5! × 2!)
(7 × 6 × 5!) / (5! × 2) = 42 / 2 = 21
Answer: 21
Example 4.5
Simplify: 9! / (6! × 3!)
(9 × 8 × 7) / 6 = (3 × 4 × 7) = 84
Answer: 84
Example 4.6
Simplify: (n + 1)! / (n - 1)!
[(n + 1) × n × (n - 1)!] / (n - 1)! = n(n + 1) = n^2 + n
Answer: n^2 + n
Example 4.7
Simplify: (n + 2)! / n!
(n + 2)(n + 1) = n^2 + 3n + 2
Answer: n^2 + 3n + 2
Example 4.8
Find n if (n + 1)! = 20 × (n - 1)!
(n + 1)n = 20 ⇒ 5 × 4 = 20 ⇒ n = 4
Answer: n = 4
Example 4.9
Simplify: (100! - 99!) / 98!
Factor out 99!: [99!(100 - 1)] / 98! = [99 × 98! × 99] / 98! = 99 × 99 = 9,801
Answer: 9,801
Example 4.10
Evaluate: 6! / (3! × 3!)
(6 × 5 × 4) / 6 = 20
Answer: 20
Example 4.11
Evaluate: 50! / 48!
50 × 49 = 2,450
Answer: 2,450
Example 4.12
Simplify: (n!) / (n - 2)! for n = 8
n(n - 1) = 8 × 7 = 56
Answer: 56

Permutations & The nPr Formula

⭐ PERMUTATION = ORDER MATTERS

A permutation is an ordered arrangement of a set of distinct objects. If we have 3 letters {A, B, C}, the possible 3-letter arrangements are:

ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA (6 distinct arrangements = 3! = 6)

Notice that ABC and BAC are completely different because A is in the 1st position in the first case, while B is in the 1st position in the second.

The Permutation Formula
nPr = n! / (n - r)!
Where:
n = Total number of distinct objects available.
r = Number of objects selected and arranged in specific order (0 ≤ r ≤ n).
Shortcut: nPr is simply the product of r consecutive decreasing integers starting from n: n × (n-1) × (n-2) × ... (r factors).

Find the value of nPn
nPn = n! / 0! = n! / 1 = n!

How many ways can 4 runners finish in 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th place?
4P4 = 4! = 24

Permutations With Repetition Allowed

When an object can be used more than once, each position has the full n choices available.

Formula for Repetition Allowed
Total Permutations = n^r
Where n is the number of available choices for each slot, and r is the number of slots to fill.
Scenario Repetition NOT Allowed (nPr) Repetition ALLOWED (n^r)
3-digit code using 5 digits (1,2,3,4,5) 5P3 = 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 5^3 = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125
4-letter password from 26 letters 26P4 = 358,800 26^4 = 456,976
Assigning 3 prizes to 10 students 10P3 = 720 (1 prize per student) 10^3 = 1,000 (student can win multiple)
Topic 08 / 20

Arrangements With Restrictions & The Block Method

Entry tests love restricted permutation questions. Master the two indispensable techniques:

📦 The "Together" Rule: The Block Method (Tie Method)
When specific items must always be together:
1. Tie the specified items into 1 single compound block.
2. Count total units = (Other items + 1 block) and arrange them in K! ways.
3. Multiply by internal permutations of items inside the block: K! × (block items)!.
🚫 The "Never Together" Rule: Total Minus Together / Gap Method
When 2 items must never be together:
Never Together = Total Unrestricted Arrangements − Together Arrangements
For 3 or more items, use the Gap Method: arrange the unrestricted items first to create gaps (_A_B_C_), then place the restricted items in those gaps.
Topic 09 / 20

Circular Permutations

When objects are arranged in a circle, there is no fixed starting point. Rotating the entire circle does not create a new relative arrangement.

Circular Arrangement Formulas
Standard Circle: (n - 1)!
Necklace / Key Ring (Flippable): (n - 1)! / 2
We fix 1 person/object in place to serve as the reference origin. The remaining (n - 1) positions are arranged linearly in (n - 1)! ways. If the circle can be turned over (like a necklace or bracelet), clockwise and anticlockwise become identical, so we divide by 2.
Example 9.1 • Round Table
In how many ways can 6 diplomats be seated around a round negotiation table?
(6 - 1)! = 5! = 120
Answer: 120 ways

Combinations & The nCr Formula

⭐ COMBINATION = ORDER DOES NOT MATTER

A combination is an unordered selection of objects. Choosing Ali and Bilal for a committee is identical to choosing Bilal and Ali:

{A, B} = {B, A} (Only 1 combination, even though there are 2 permutations!)
The Combination Formula
nCr = n! / [r! × (n - r)!]
Where:
n = Total number of items.
r = Number of items chosen.
FAST Cancellation Rule: nCr = (Product of r decreasing terms from n) / r!.

Topic 12 / 20

Relationship Between nPr and nCr

The Fundamental Link
nPr = nCr × r!
nCr = nPr / r!
First select the r objects without order in nCr ways, then arrange those r objects in order in r! ways.
Example 12.1 • MCQ Speed
If nPr = 720 and nCr = 120, find r and n.
Step 1: r! = nPr / nCr = 720 / 120 = 6
Step 2: Since 3! = 6, we have r = 3.
Step 3: nP3 = n(n-1)(n-2) = 720 ⇒ 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 ⇒ n = 10.
Answer: n = 10, r = 3
Topic 13 / 20

Important Combination Properties & Symmetry

Symmetry Identity
nCr = nC(n - r)
Example: 10C7 = 10C3 = 120 (Massive calculation saver!)
Boundary Extremes
nC0 = 1 and nCn = 1
Choosing 0 items or all items can only happen in 1 way.
Single Selection
nC1 = n
Example: 8C1 = 8
Pascal's Identity
nCr + nC(r - 1) = (n + 1)Cr
Example: 10C3 + 10C4 = 11C4
Sum of Coefficients
∑(r=0 to n) nCr = 2^n
Example: 5C0 + 5C1 + ... + 5C5 = 2^5 = 32

Combinations With Restrictions & Group Selections

When selecting committees from multiple distinct groups (e.g. boys and girls, doctors and engineers), use the Product Rule across groups:

Multi-Group Selection Rule
Total = (Group 1 Selections) × (Group 2 Selections) × ...
Example 14.1 • Committee Selection
From 7 men and 5 women, a committee of 3 men and 2 women is formed. How many such committees are possible?
Men: 7C3 = (7 × 6 × 5) / 6 = 35
Women: 5C2 = (5 × 4) / 2 = 10
Total: 35 × 10 = 350
Answer: 350 committees

Permutations of Repeated Objects (Word Problems)

When some items in the set are identical, swapping them does not produce a new arrangement. We must divide by the factorials of the repetitions:

Repeated Objects Formula
Permutations = n! / (p! × q! × r! × ...)
Where n is total objects, and p, q, r... are the counts of identical objects.
Example 15.1 • MOM
How many distinct permutations of 'MOM'?
3! / 2! = 6 / 2 = 3
Answer: 3 (MOM, MMO, OMM)
Example 15.2 • LEVEL
How many distinct permutations of 'LEVEL'? (L=2, E=2, V=1)
5! / (2! × 2!) = 120 / 4 = 30
Answer: 30 arrangements
Example 15.3 • PAKISTAN
How many permutations of 'PAKISTAN'? (Total=8, A=2)
8! / 2! = 40,320 / 2 = 20,160
Answer: 20,160 arrangements

At Least / At Most & The Complement Method

⚡ The Golden FAST Entry Rule: "At Least One" = Total − None
Whenever a question asks for "at least one", calculating all positive cases (1, 2, 3, ...) takes 3 minutes. Calculating Total − (Zero Cases) takes 10 seconds!
Example 16.1 • Defective Bulbs
A box has 10 bulbs, of which 3 are defective. If 4 bulbs are selected, in how many ways can at least 1 defective bulb be chosen?
Total Selections: 10C4 = 210
Unwanted (0 defective, all 4 good from 7): 7C4 = 35
Calculation: 210 − 35 = 175
Answer: 175 ways

10 FAST & Scholarship Speed Shortcuts

Shortcut 1
Always Ask: "Does Order Matter?"
Never start calculating before verifying whether order changes the result. If not, immediately write nCr.
Shortcut 2
Cancel Factorials First (Peeling Trick)
Never expand 10! or 8!. Write 10! / 8! = 10 × 9 = 90 instantly.
Shortcut 3
Use Symmetry: nCr = nC(n-r)
Calculate 100C98 as 100C2 = (100 × 99) / 2 = 4,950 in 5 seconds.
Shortcut 4
Direct Slot Multiplication for Small Permutations
For 5 items in 3 seats, write 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 directly without writing factorials.
Shortcut 5
The Complement Rule for "At Least" Questions
At Least 1 = Total Combinations − Zero Combinations.
Shortcut 6
Fix the Restricted Positions First
If the 1st digit must be odd, fill that slot first (e.g. 3 choices), then fill remaining slots freely.
Shortcut 7
Block Method for Items That Must Stay Together
Bundle items into 1 block, arrange units, then multiply by internal block arrangements: Units! × (Block)!.
Shortcut 8
Handshake Formula: nC2 = n(n-1)/2
For 20 people shaking hands: 20 × 19 / 2 = 190.
Shortcut 9
Polygon Diagonals: n(n-3)/2
For an octagon (8 sides): 8 × 5 / 2 = 20 diagonals.
Shortcut 10
Circular Flippable: Divide by 2
Necklaces and key rings can be viewed from both sides: (n-1)! / 2.

18 Common Mistakes in Entry Tests

Avoid these 18 classical pitfalls that cost students easy marks in admission tests:

1. Confusing nPr and nCr
❌ Using nPr when choosing committee members.
✅ Use nCr because committee roles are unordered.
💡 Order only matters when positions (President, VP) are distinct.
2. Forgetting 0! = 1
❌ Thinking 0! = 0.
✅ 0! = 1 always.
💡 By definition, the empty product has the multiplicative identity 1.
3. Repeated Letters Overcounting
❌ Arranging 'LEVEL' as 5! = 120.
✅ 5! / (2! × 2!) = 30.
💡 Swapping identical L's or E's does not create a new word.
4. Linear vs Circular Permutations
❌ Arranging 5 people in a circle as 5! = 120.
✅ (5 - 1)! = 4! = 24.
💡 In a circle without a reference anchor, all rotations are identical.
5. Double Counting Internal Arrangements
❌ Arranging identical objects inside a tied block.
✅ If the block contains identical items (SSS), internal ways = 1.
💡 3! / 3! = 1.
6. Leading Zero in Multi-Digit Numbers
❌ Allowing 0 in the first slot of a 4-digit number.
✅ First slot has 9 choices (1–9). 0 can only go in slots 2–4.
💡 A number starting with 0 (e.g. 0245) is a 3-digit number.
7. Forgetting Necklaces / Keyrings Can Flip
❌ Using (n-1)! for beads on a necklace.
✅ Use (n-1)! / 2.
💡 A necklace can be turned over, making clockwise and anticlockwise identical.
8. Misinterpreting "At Least One"
❌ Adding complicated overlapping cases.
✅ Desired = Total − (Zero Cases).
💡 Always take the complement for maximum speed.
9. Misinterpreting "At Most"
❌ Only calculating the exact maximum number.
✅ "At most 2" means: 0 OR 1 OR 2 cases added together.
💡 "At most" is a ceiling, including zero.
10. Dividing Unnamed Groups vs Named People
❌ Dividing 6 people into 3 teams of 2 as 6C2 × 4C2 × 2C2.
✅ Divide by 3! because the 3 groups have no names.
💡 Unnamed equal partitions require dividing by k!.
11. Missing Internal Block Factorials
❌ Treating (Ali, Bilal) as 1 unit and forgetting 2!.
✅ Multiply by 2! for Ali-Bilal vs Bilal-Ali.
💡 Distinct persons can swap positions inside the block.
12. Handshakes vs Ordered Matches
❌ Using nP2 for handshakes.
✅ Use nC2 = n(n-1)/2.
💡 A shakes B's hand is the same single handshake as B shaking A's hand.
13. Incomplete Collinear Triangle Deduction
❌ Assuming all nC3 combinations form triangles.
✅ Subtract kC3 for k points that lie on a single straight line.
💡 Collinear points cannot form a valid triangle.
14. Missing the 1 Single Line in Collinear Points
❌ Total lines = nC2 − kC2.
✅ Total lines = nC2 − kC2 + 1.
💡 The k collinear points still form 1 single straight line.
15. Symmetry Sign Flip in nC(n-r)
❌ Writing 10C7 = 10C4.
✅ 10C7 = 10C(10-7) = 10C3.
💡 n - r = 10 - 7 = 3.
16. Adding Instead of Multiplying Independent Tasks
❌ 4 shirts + 3 trousers = 7 outfits.
✅ 4 × 3 = 12 outfits.
💡 When tasks happen together in sequence, multiply!
17. Multiplying Instead of Adding Mutually Exclusive Cases
❌ Multiplying Case 1 × Case 2.
✅ Add Case 1 + Case 2 when they cannot happen simultaneously.
💡 The Addition Rule applies to mutually exclusive branches.
18. Forgetting to Subtract Perimeter in Diagonals
❌ Number of diagonals in n-gon = nC2.
✅ Diagonals = nC2 − n = n(n-3)/2.
💡 The n outer boundary edges are sides, not internal diagonals.
