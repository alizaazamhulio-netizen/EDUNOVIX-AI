Topic 01 — 06

Acid–Base Concepts & The 3 Classical Theories

Understand how the definition of acids and bases evolved from aqueous ion producers to proton transfer and coordinate covalent electron pairs.

Definition What are Acids?

An acid is a chemical species capable of donating a proton (H⁺) or increasing the concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) in aqueous media.

Acid → H⁺(aq) + Anion⁻(aq) Increases [H₃O⁺] in water | pH < 7 at 25°C
  • Taste & Touch: Sour taste; corrosive to metals.
  • Electrolytic behavior: Conducts electricity in aqueous solution due to free mobile ions.
  • Common MDCAT Examples: Hydrochloric acid (HCl), Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), Nitric acid (HNO₃), Acetic acid (CH₃COOH).

Definition What are Bases?

A base is a chemical species capable of accepting a proton (H⁺) or increasing the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in aqueous media.

Base → Cation⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) Increases [OH⁻] in water | pH > 7 at 25°C
  • Taste & Touch: Bitter taste; slippery/soapy feel on skin.
  • Alkalis: Water-soluble bases are specifically termed alkalis (e.g. Group 1 & 2 hydroxides).
  • Common MDCAT Examples: Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), Potassium hydroxide (KOH), Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), Ammonia (NH₃).
🧠 Interactive Acid-Base Theories Comparison

Arrhenius Theory ⭐

Proposed by Svante Arrhenius. The simplest classical definition based strictly on ionization in water.

  • Arrhenius Acid: Produces hydrogen ions H⁺ (or hydronium ions H₃O⁺) in aqueous solution.
  • Arrhenius Base: Produces hydroxide ions OH⁻ in aqueous solution.
HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ (Acid)

NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) (Base)
⚠️ Arrhenius Limitations (High-Yield MDCAT Question)

Arrhenius theory fails because:

  1. It is strictly restricted to aqueous solutions (cannot explain gas-phase reactions like NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)).
  2. It cannot explain the basicity of substances that lack OH⁻ groups in their formula, such as NH₃, Na₂CO₃, or amines.

Brønsted–Lowry Theory ⭐

Introduced independently by Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry. Focuses on proton (H⁺) transfer.

  • Brønsted Acid: A substance that can donate a proton (Proton Donor).
  • Brønsted Base: A substance that can accept a proton (Proton Acceptor).
HCl (donor/acid) + H₂O (acceptor/base) ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
⭐ Major MDCAT Breakthrough

Brønsted-Lowry explains why ammonia (NH₃) is a base in water even though it contains no OH⁻ in its formula: NH₃ accepts a proton from water to form NH₄⁺ and generates OH⁻ from the solvent!

Lewis Acid–Base Theory ⭐

Formulated by G. N. Lewis. The most comprehensive electronic definition, independent of protons or solvents.

  • Lewis Acid: An electron-pair acceptor (species with an incomplete octet, empty d/p orbitals, or positive charge).
  • Lewis Base: An electron-pair donor (species with a lone pair of electrons or negative charge).
BF₃ (Lewis Acid) + :NH₃ (Lewis Base) → F₃B←NH₃ (Adduct)
⭐ Key Lewis Examples for MDCAT

Lewis Acids: BF₃, AlCl₃, FeCl₃, H⁺, Mg²⁺, SO₃, CO₂.

Lewis Bases: NH₃, H₂O, OH⁻, Cl⁻, CN⁻, R-OH, R-NH₂.

Topic 04 — 05

Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs & Amphiprotic Species

Master the single-proton difference rule, conjugate strength reciprocal laws, and amphiprotic behavior in biological and chemical systems.

Rule Conjugate Acid–Base Pair Mechanics

Two species that differ from each other by exactly one proton (H⁺) constitute a conjugate acid-base pair.

🔑 The Golden MDCAT Conjugate Formula:
• Acid − H⁺ = Conjugate Base
• Base + H⁺ = Conjugate Acid
Original Species Add/Remove H⁺ Conjugate Partner
HCl (Acid) Remove H⁺ Cl⁻ (Conjugate Base)
H₂O (Base) Add H⁺ H₃O⁺ (Conjugate Acid)
NH₃ (Base) Add H⁺ NH₄⁺ (Conjugate Acid)
CH₃COOH (Acid) Remove H⁺ CH₃COO⁻ (Conjugate Base)
HSO₄⁻ (Acid) Remove H⁺ SO₄²⁻ (Conjugate Base)

Phenomenon Amphiprotic (Amphoteric) Substances

An amphiprotic substance is a molecule or ion that can act as both a Brønsted acid (proton donor) and a Brønsted base (proton acceptor).

⭐ Water: The Classic Amphiprotic Molecule

Water as an Acid: H₂O + NH₃ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ (Water donates H⁺)

Water as a Base: H₂O + HCl ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ (Water accepts H⁺)

High-Yield MDCAT Amphiprotic Ions:

  • HCO₃⁻ (Bicarbonate): Acts as base forming H₂CO₃; acts as acid forming CO₃²⁻.
  • H₂PO₄⁻ (Dihydrogen Phosphate): Vital in intracellular biological buffer system.
  • HSO₄⁻ (Hydrogen Sulfate) and HS⁻ (Hydrogen Sulfide).
  • Amino Acids: Contain both acidic (-COOH) and basic (-NH₂) groups (zwitterions).

Strength, Concentration & Equilibrium Constants (Ka & Kb)

Demystify the critical difference between strength (ionization degree) and concentration (molarity), and calculate Ka, Kb, pKa, and pKb.

⚡ Microscopic Visualizer: Strong vs Weak Acids & Strength vs Concentration

Strong Acid (e.g. 0.1 M HCl)

Ionizes completely into H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻. Virtually zero unionized HCl molecules remain in solution.
⚠️ The Classic MDCAT Trap: Strong vs Concentrated

Never confuse strength with concentration!

  • Strength = Degree / fraction of ionization (chemical property, measured by Ka or α).
  • Concentration = Amount of solute present per unit volume of solution (moles/L).
Solution Description Example Ionization Solute Molarity
Concentrated Strong Acid 12 M HCl 100% High (~12 mol/L)
Dilute Strong Acid 0.001 M HCl 100% Low (~0.001 mol/L)
Concentrated Weak Acid 17.4 M Glacial Acetic Acid ~0.4% High (~17.4 mol/L)
Dilute Weak Acid 0.01 M CH₃COOH ~4.2% Low (~0.01 mol/L)

Calculator Acid Dissociation Constant (Ka & pKa)

For a weak monoprotic acid: HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq)

Ka = ([H⁺] × [A⁻]) / [HA] pKa = −log₁₀(Ka) | Larger Ka = Stronger Acid = Lower pKa
Ka = (1.34 × 10⁻³ × 1.34 × 10⁻³) / (0.09866)
Ka = 1.82 × 10⁻⁵
pKa = −log(1.82 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74
Ka = 1.82 × 10⁻⁵ | pKa = 4.74

Calculator Base Dissociation Constant (Kb & pKb)

For a weak base: B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Kb = ([BH⁺] × [OH⁻]) / [B] pKb = −log₁₀(Kb) | Larger Kb = Stronger Base = Lower pKb
Kb = (1.34 × 10⁻³ × 1.34 × 10⁻³) / (0.09866)
Kb = 1.82 × 10⁻⁵
pKb = −log(1.82 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74
Kb = 1.82 × 10⁻⁵ | pKb = 4.74

Topic 13, 19

Self-Ionization of Water & Ion-Product Constant (Kw)

Explore auto-protolysis, how temperature alters Kw and neutral pH, and the foundational conjugate relationship: Ka × Kb = Kw.

Equilibrium Auto-Ionization of Water

Water undergoes a slight reversible self-ionization (auto-protolysis) where one water molecule donates a proton to another.

2H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C / 298 K)

In pure water at 25°C:

[H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M
⭐ Temperature Dependency (High-Yield MDCAT MCQ)

The self-ionization of water is an endothermic process (ΔH > 0). According to Le Chatelier's principle, as temperature increases:

  • Kw increases (e.g. at 100°C, Kw ≈ 5.13 × 10⁻¹³).
  • [H⁺] increases, so pure water pH at 100°C drops to ~6.14.
  • Crucial: The water remains completely neutral because [H⁺] still strictly equals [OH⁻]!

Relationship Conjugate Relationship: Ka × Kb = Kw

For any conjugate acid-base pair in aqueous solution at a given temperature:

Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C) pKa + pKb = pKw = 14.00
Conjugate Reciprocal Law:

The stronger an acid (large Ka, small pKa), the weaker its conjugate base (small Kb, large pKb). For example, HCl is a very strong acid, so Cl⁻ has negligible basicity in water.

Interactive [H⁺] ↔ [OH⁻] at 25°C Calculator

[OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ M | pOH = 10.00 | pH = 4.00

⚡ MDCAT Mental Math Shortcut

If [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻ⁿ M, then pH = n directly!
If [H⁺] = a × 10⁻ⁿ M (where 1 < a < 10), then pH = n − log(a).
Example: [H⁺] = 2 × 10⁻⁴ M → pH = 4 − log(2) = 4 − 0.301 = 3.699.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Formula: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
pH = −log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻³) = 3.00
pOH = 14 − pH = 14 − 3.00 = 11.00
[OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ = 10⁻¹¹ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ M
pH = 3.00 (Acidic Solution)
Topic 20 — 23

Neutralization Reactions, Salts & Salt Hydrolysis

Understand why dissolving a neutral-looking salt can produce an acidic, basic, or neutral aqueous solution through cation and anion hydrolysis.

Type 1 Normal / Neutral Salts

Formed when all replaceable hydrogen atoms of an acid are completely neutralized by metal cations or ammonium.

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
  • Contains no replaceable H⁺ or OH⁻.
  • Examples: NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄, K₂SO₄.
  • Formed by complete neutralization.

Type 2 Acid Salts

Formed by partial neutralization of a polyprotic (dibasic or tribasic) acid. Still contains replaceable acidic hydrogen.

H₂SO₄ + NaOH → NaHSO₄ + H₂O
  • Can react further with bases.
  • Examples: NaHCO₃, NaHSO₄, NaH₂PO₄.
  • Turns blue litmus red upon further ionization.

Type 3 Basic Salts

Formed by incomplete neutralization of a polyhydroxy base. Contains unneutralized hydroxide ions in the lattice.

Pb(OH)₂ + HNO₃ → Pb(OH)NO₃ + H₂O
  • Can react further with acids to form normal salts.
  • Examples: Basic lead nitrate Pb(OH)NO₃, Bi(OH)₂Cl, Cu(OH)₂·CuCO₃.
🧪 Interactive Salt Hydrolysis Diagnostic Lab

Salt Hydrolysis Breakdown

Parent Acid: HCl (Strong Acid)
Parent Base: NaOH (Strong Base)
Hydrolysis Behavior: Neither cation nor anion hydrolyzes
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + H₂O → No Reaction [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M at 25°C

Solution Character & Litmus Test:

Because both parent acid and base are strong, the conjugate ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) are spectator ions and do not react with water. The resulting solution is approximately neutral (pH ≈ 7).

Topic 24 — 25

Buffer Solutions & The Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

Master the chemistry of pH resistance, biological blood buffering, and calculate buffer pH using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

Concept What is a Buffer Solution?

A buffer solution is an aqueous solution that resists significant changes in its pH when small amounts of an acid (H⁺) or base (OH⁻) are added.

1. Acidic Buffers (pH < 7)

Prepared by mixing a weak acid + its salt with a strong base (its conjugate base).

CH₃COOH (Weak Acid) + CH₃COONa (Conjugate Salt)

2. Basic Buffers (pH > 7)

Prepared by mixing a weak base + its salt with a strong acid (its conjugate acid).

NH₃ (Weak Base) + NH₄Cl (Conjugate Salt)

High-Yield Buffer Mechanism & Henderson–Hasselbalch

When H⁺ is added to an acidic buffer, the conjugate base absorbs it:

CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH (Suppresses pH drop)

When OH⁻ is added, the unionized weak acid neutralizes it:

CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O (Suppresses pH rise)
pH = pKa + log₁₀([Conjugate Base] / [Acid]) For Basic Buffers: pOH = pKb + log₁₀([Conjugate Acid] / [Base])
⭐ Optimal Buffer Capacity (MDCAT Rule)

Maximum buffer capacity occurs when [Salt] = [Acid], which makes log(1) = 0, meaning pH = pKa! Effective buffer range is pH = pKa ± 1.

Indicators are weak organic acids or bases (HIn) whose unionized form and ionized conjugate base form possess distinct colors.

⭐ Equivalence Point vs. Endpoint (Must-Know MDCAT Difference)
  • Equivalence Point (Theoretical): The stoichiometric point where moles of H⁺ exactly equal moles of OH⁻ added.
  • Endpoint (Experimental): The practical visual point where the chemical indicator changes color.
  • In an ideal titration, the indicator is chosen so that Endpoint ≈ Equivalence Point.

Equivalence Point pH Values:

  • Strong Acid + Strong Base: Equivalence pH = 7.00 (Neutral salt NaCl).
  • Weak Acid + Strong Base: Equivalence pH > 7.00 (Basic salt CH₃COONa hydrolyzes).
  • Strong Acid + Weak Base: Equivalence pH < 7.00 (Acidic salt NH₄Cl hydrolyzes).

Calculator Volumetric Neutralization Stoichiometry (M₁V₁ / n₁ = M₂V₂ / n₂)

Determine the unknown concentration or volume of acid/base for both monoprotic and polyprotic systems.

Stoichiometric Calculation:

Equation: (n₁ × M₁ × V₁) = (n₂ × M₂ × V₂)
(1 × 0.10 M × 25.0 mL) = (1 × 0.20 M × V₂)
V₂ = 2.50 / 0.20 = 12.50 mL
Required Base Volume (V₂) = 12.50 mL
