01

Atomic Spectrum

An Atomic Spectrum is the array of characteristic wavelengths or frequencies of electromagnetic radiation absorbed or emitted by electrons during transitions between quantized energy levels within an atom.

📖 Concept Bridge (Urdu / Roman Urdu)
Jab atoms ko heat ya electric discharge se energy di jati hai, unke electrons higher energy levels (excited states) par chaley jate hain. Jab ye electrons wapas lower energy levels par aate hain, to energy electromagnetic photons ki form mein emit hoti hai. Is emitted radiation ko wavelength ke mutabiq prism ya spectroscope se disperse karne par jo pattern milta hai, use Atomic Spectrum kehte hain.

Atomic spectra are strictly classified into two fundamental types:

  • Emission Spectrum: Light emitted by excited atoms when electrons drop from higher to lower levels.
  • Absorption Spectrum: Specific wavelengths absorbed when white light passes through unexcited, cooler gas atoms.
02

Emission Spectrum

When a volatile substance or gas is subjected to a high-voltage electric discharge or flame, its atoms become thermally or electrically excited. As the electrons jump back down to lower stationary states:

$$\Delta E = E_{\text{initial}} - E_{\text{final}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

The emitted light is passed through a prism or diffraction grating spectroscope. The result is a series of bright, distinct, sharp colored lines against a dark background.

💡
MDCAT Key Fact: In an emission spectrum, only specific quantized wavelengths corresponding to discrete transitions are released. It is a discontinuous line spectrum.
03

Absorption Spectrum

When continuous white light (containing all wavelengths of visible light) is transmitted through a container of relatively cool gas, the gas atoms absorb precisely those photons whose energies match the exact difference between their energy levels.

When the transmitted beam is analyzed with a spectroscope, the spectrum appears as a continuous rainbow background crossed by dark absorption lines (missing wavelengths).

⚠️
Crucial MDCAT Difference: The dark lines in the absorption spectrum appear at the exact same positions and wavelengths as the bright lines in the emission spectrum for the same element!
04

Hydrogen Atomic Spectrum

Hydrogen is the simplest atom, containing only one electron and one proton. When hydrogen gas ($H_2$) is dissociated and excited by an electric discharge at low pressure in a discharge tube (Geissler tube), a bluish-pink glow is observed.

Spectroscopic analysis reveals that the hydrogen spectrum is not randomly scattered but grouped into several distinct mathematically structured spectral series.

📖 Urdu Quick Concept
Hydrogen atom mein sirf 1 electron hota hai, lekin sample mein trillions of hydrogen atoms hote hain. Har atom ka electron alag alag level par excite hota hai aur wapas aate waqt alag alag transitions karta hai. Is liye hume aik waqt mein bohat si alag spectral lines (UV, Visible, IR) milti hain.
05

06

Lyman Series (Ultraviolet)

Discovered by Theodore Lyman in 1906. All transitions end at the first energy level ($n_f = 1$):

  • First Line (Longest λ, Smallest ΔE): $n_i = 2 \rightarrow n_f = 1$ ($\lambda = 121.6\text{ nm}, \Delta E = 10.2\text{ eV}$)
  • Second Line: $n_i = 3 \rightarrow n_f = 1$ ($\lambda = 102.6\text{ nm}, \Delta E = 12.09\text{ eV}$)
  • Limiting Line (Shortest λ, Maximum ΔE): $n_i = \infty \rightarrow n_f = 1$ ($\lambda = 91.2\text{ nm}, \Delta E = 13.6\text{ eV}$)

Because the energy difference between $n=1$ and any higher orbit is substantial ($\ge 10.2\text{ eV}$), Lyman lines have the shortest wavelengths and highest photon energies in the entire hydrogen spectrum, lying strictly in the Ultraviolet (UV) region.

07

Balmer Series (Visible Region)

Discovered empirically by Johann Jakob Balmer in 1885. This is the only series that lies within the human visible light region.

All Balmer transitions end at $n_f = 2$ from initial levels $n_i = 3, 4, 5, 6...$:

Spectral Line Transition Color Observed Wavelength (λ) Photon Energy (ΔE)
H-α (Alpha) 3 → 2 Red 656.3 nm (6563 Å) 1.89 eV
H-β (Beta) 4 → 2 Cyan / Blue-Green 486.1 nm (4861 Å) 2.55 eV
H-γ (Gamma) 5 → 2 Blue / Indigo 434.0 nm (4340 Å) 2.86 eV
H-δ (Delta) 6 → 2 Violet 410.2 nm (4102 Å) 3.02 eV
Series Limit ($H_\infty$) ∞ → 2 Near UV boundary 364.6 nm 3.40 eV

Visual Property: As $n_i$ increases, the energy gap between successive levels decreases, so the spectral lines become progressively closer (crowded) towards the short-wavelength limit.

08-10

Paschen, Brackett, and Pfund Series (Infrared)

08. Paschen Series Near IR
n_f = 3, n_i = 4, 5, 6...

Transitions to the 3rd orbit. Lowest energy transition is $4 \rightarrow 3$ ($\lambda = 1875\text{ nm}$). Lies completely in the Near Infrared region.

09. Brackett Series Mid IR
n_f = 4, n_i = 5, 6, 7...

Transitions to the 4th orbit. Lowest energy transition is $5 \rightarrow 4$ ($\lambda = 4051\text{ nm}$). Lies in the Middle Infrared region.

10. Pfund Series Far IR
n_f = 5, n_i = 6, 7, 8...

Transitions to the 5th orbit. Lowest energy transition is $6 \rightarrow 5$ ($\lambda = 7460\text{ nm}$). Lies in the Far Infrared region.

11

Rydberg Equation

Johannes Rydberg formulated a comprehensive mathematical equation to calculate the wave number ($\bar{\nu} = 1/\lambda$) and wavelength of any spectral line in hydrogen:

General Rydberg Formula for Hydrogen
\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)

Where:
• $\bar{\nu}$ = Wave number ($m^{-1}$)
• $\lambda$ = Wavelength of photon ($m$)
• $R_H$ = Rydberg constant $\approx 1.097373 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$ (or $1.09678 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$)
• $n_f$ = Final/lower energy level quantum number
• $n_i$ = Initial/higher energy level quantum number ($n_i > n_f$)

💡
Shortcuts for MDCAT Numericals:
• Longest Wavelength ($\lambda_{\max}$): Put $n_i = n_f + 1$ (Smallest energy gap).
• Shortest Wavelength ($\lambda_{\min}$ / Series limit): Put $n_i = \infty$, so $\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}} = \frac{R_H}{n_f^2} \implies \lambda_{\min} = \frac{n_f^2}{R_H}$.
• Note: $1/R_H \approx 91.2\text{ nm} = 912\text{ Å}$.
12

Photon Energy & Fundamental Relationships

When an electron changes its energy state, electromagnetic radiation is absorbed or emitted in discrete packets of energy called photons or quanta.

Planck's Energy Equation
E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\bar{\nu}

• $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$ (Planck's constant)
• $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ (Speed of light in vacuum)
• $\nu = c/\lambda$ (Frequency in Hz or $s^{-1}$)

⭐
Core Proportionality Laws for MDCAT:
1. Energy & Frequency: $E \propto \nu$ (Higher frequency $\rightarrow$ Higher energy)
2. Energy & Wavelength: $E \propto \frac{1}{\lambda}$ (Shorter wavelength $\rightarrow$ Higher energy)
3. Frequency & Wavelength: $\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} \implies \nu \propto \frac{1}{\lambda}$ (Shorter wavelength $\rightarrow$ Higher frequency)
13

Bohr's Explanation of Atomic Spectrum

Classical electromagnetic physics (Maxwell's theory) predicted that an accelerating electron revolving in an orbit must continuously radiate energy, spiral into the nucleus, and produce a continuous spectrum.

In 1913, Niels Bohr resolved this paradox by proposing three revolutionary postulates:

  • Stationary Orbits: Electrons move only in specific discrete, non-radiating orbits where their orbital angular momentum is quantized: $mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}$.
  • No Continuous Radiation: While staying in an allowed stationary state, an electron does NOT lose or gain energy.
  • Quantum Jumps: Radiation is emitted or absorbed ONLY when an electron transitions between two allowed orbits: $\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu$.

Because only specific energy values are permissible ($E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2}\text{ eV}$), only specific discrete differences ($\Delta E$) are possible, perfectly accounting for the sharp, discontinuous line spectra of atoms.

Ground State vs Excited State

Feature Ground State Excited State
Definition The lowest, most stable allowed energy level Any temporary level of higher energy than the ground state
Principal Quantum No. $n = 1$ (for Hydrogen) $n = 2, 3, 4, 5...$
Energy Value (H-atom) $E_1 = -13.6\text{ eV}$ (most negative) $E_2 = -3.4\text{ eV}$, $E_3 = -1.51\text{ eV}$, etc.
Electron Stability Completely stable; infinite lifetime Unstable; lifetime $\approx 10^{-8}\text{ seconds}$
15

Ionization Energy of Hydrogen

Ionization Energy (IE) is the minimum amount of energy required to completely remove an electron from the ground state ($n = 1$) to infinity ($n = \infty$) where electrostatic attraction ceases ($E_\infty = 0$).

\text{IE} = E_\infty - E_1 = 0 - (-13.6\text{ eV}) = +13.6\text{ eV} = 1312\text{ kJ/mol} = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}
⭐
MDCAT Must-Remember Values for Hydrogen:
• Ionization Energy from $n=1$: +13.6 eV (or 1312 kJ·mol⁻¹)
• Ionization Energy from $n=2$ (1st excited state): $+3.4\text{ eV}$
• Ionization Energy from $n=3$ (2nd excited state): $+1.51\text{ eV}$

Line Spectrum vs Continuous Spectrum

Parameter Continuous Spectrum Line Spectrum (Atomic Spectrum)
Appearance Unbroken continuous band of colors merging without gaps (rainbow) Distinct sharp isolated colored lines separated by dark spaces
Source Incandescent solids, liquids, or dense hot gases (Sun, bulb filament) Isolated excited atoms/ions in gaseous phase at low pressure
Origin Bulk thermal radiation; unquantized macroscopic vibration modes Quantized transitions of bound electrons between discrete orbitals
Characteristic Not characteristic of specific elements Unique fingerprint characteristic of each element
17

Why Every Element Has a Characteristic Spectrum

Every chemical element has:

  1. A unique nuclear charge (atomic number, $Z$) with a distinct number of protons.
  2. A unique number and spatial orbital configuration of electrons.
  3. Consequently, a unique set of quantized electronic energy level differences ($\Delta E$).

Because the allowed energy differences ($\Delta E$) are never identical between any two different elements, the frequencies ($\nu = \Delta E/h$) and wavelengths ($\lambda = hc/\Delta E$) emitted or absorbed are completely distinct.

🔬 Forensic & Astronomical Application
Just like no two humans have the exact same fingerprints, no two chemical elements produce the exact same atomic spectral lines. This allows astronomers to identify the chemical composition of distant stars and galaxies millions of light years away by analyzing starlight absorption lines (Fraunhofer lines)!

🔥 MDCAT High-Yield Facts Summary

  • Lyman series: Final level $n_f = 1$, occurs exclusively in Ultraviolet (UV) region.
  • Balmer series: Final level $n_f = 2$, occurs in Visible light region ($400 - 700\text{ nm}$).
  • Paschen series: Final level $n_f = 3$, occurs in Near Infrared region.
  • Brackett series: Final level $n_f = 4$, occurs in Mid Infrared region.
  • Pfund series: Final level $n_f = 5$, occurs in Far Infrared region.
  • Emission transition: Higher energy level $\rightarrow$ Lower energy level (Energy released as photon).
  • Absorption transition: Lower energy level $\rightarrow$ Higher energy level (Energy absorbed from photon).
  • Ground state energy of Hydrogen: $E_1 = -13.6\text{ eV}$.
  • Ionization energy of Hydrogen: $+13.6\text{ eV} = 1312\text{ kJ/mol} = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J}$.
  • Rydberg Constant: $R_H \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$.
  • Wavelength-Energy inverse rule: Shorter $\lambda \implies$ Higher frequency $\implies$ Higher photon energy $E$.
  • Line spectrum: Direct experimental proof of quantized stationary energy levels in atoms.

🧠 Common MDCAT Traps & Antidotes

Common Student Trap / Misconception Scientific Reality & MDCAT Antidote
❌ Trap 1: Thinking Balmer series lies in Ultraviolet. ✓ Fact: Balmer series lies in Visible light. Lyman is UV.
❌ Trap 2: Thinking emission means jumping to higher levels. ✓ Fact: Emission is Higher $\rightarrow$ Lower. Absorption is Lower $\rightarrow$ Higher.
❌ Trap 3: Longer wavelength means higher energy. ✓ Fact: $E = hc/\lambda$. Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength. Shorter $\lambda$ = Higher $E$.
❌ Trap 4: 1st excited state means $n = 1$. ✓ Fact: Ground state is $n = 1$. 1st excited state is $n = 2$, 2nd excited state is $n = 3$.
❌ Trap 5: All lines in hydrogen have equal spacing. ✓ Fact: Lines crowd closer as $n_i \rightarrow \infty$ because the energy gap shrinks ($\Delta E \propto 1/n^2$).

📐 Premium MDCAT Formula Sheet

Photon Energy (Planck)
E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}

Relates photon energy to frequency ($\nu$) and wavelength ($\lambda$). $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$.

Rydberg Equation
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)

Calculates wave number and wavelength for any hydrogen series. $R_H \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1}$.

Bohr Orbital Energy
E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2}\text{ eV} = -\frac{2.18 \times 10^{-18}}{n^2}\text{ J}

Energy of an electron in the $n$-th stationary orbit of hydrogen.

Angular Momentum Quantization
mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}

Bohr's 1st postulate. Angular momentum is an integral multiple of $h/2\pi$.

Series Limit Wavelength
\lambda_{\min} = \frac{n_f^2}{R_H} = n_f^2 \times 91.2\text{ nm}

Shortest wavelength (limiting line) for any hydrogen series ($n_i = \infty$).

Number of Spectral Lines
N = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} = \frac{\Delta n(\Delta n + 1)}{2}

Total possible emission lines when an electron drops from level $n$ to ground state ($n=1$).

🔥 MDCAT Exam Zone
High-Scoring Vault
⚡ Must Remember
  • Lyman: $n_f=1$, UV (Invisible)
  • Balmer: $n_f=2$, Visible (Red, Cyan, Blue, Violet)
  • Paschen: $n_f=3$, Near IR (Invisible)
  • Brackett: $n_f=4$, Mid IR
  • Pfund: $n_f=5$, Far IR
  • $1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$
⚠️ Frequently Confused
  • H-α line: $n=3 \rightarrow 2$ (Red, 656.3 nm, lowest energy in Balmer).
  • H-δ line: $n=6 \rightarrow 2$ (Violet, 410.2 nm, highest visible energy in Balmer).
  • Lyman Limit: $n=\infty \rightarrow 1$ (91.2 nm, highest energy line in all H-spectrum).
  • Continuous spectrum has NO lines or gaps (e.g. blackbody, incandescent bulb).
🎯 Numerical Shortcuts
  • To find total spectral lines from level $n=4$ to ground state: $N = 4(4-1)/2 = 6$ lines.
  • Ratio of longest to shortest wavelength in Lyman: $\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} = \frac{4}{3}$.
  • Ratio of longest to shortest wavelength in Balmer: $\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} = \frac{9}{5}$.
  • Energy of $n=2$ is $-3.4\text{ eV}$, requiring $10.2\text{ eV}$ excitation from $n=1$.
