Academic Overview

Introduction to Geometry & Mensuration

Geometry (from Ancient Greek: geo = earth, metron = measurement) is the mathematical branch investigating the properties, relationships, and spatial orientations of points, lines, angles, surfaces, and solids.

Mensuration is the computational branch dedicated to calculating geometric quantities: linear distance (perimeter, circumference, diagonal), two-dimensional boundary enclosure (area, lateral area), and three-dimensional spatial capacity (volume, capacity, total surface area).

💡
Why Geometry & Mensuration Dominates FAST & Scholarship Entry Tests
FAST-NUCES and competitive STEM scholarship tests allocate 18% to 25% of the quantitative section to geometry. Rather than simple direct formula recall, questions test multi-step deductions: ratio of scale factors ($k, k^2, k^3$), composite subtraction, perimeter traps (omitting internal boundaries), and Pythagorean triples for rapid 20-second problem solving.

Real-World Engineering & Quantitative Applications:

  • Room & Construction Dimensions: Tiling floors, skirting board perimeter, air conditioning volume requirements.
  • Fluid Dynamics & Storage: Cylindrical water tanks, conical hoppers, spherical gas holders ($V = \pi r^2 h$, $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$).
  • Civil Infrastructure: Circular running tracks, road curves, rectangular field fencing with cost calculations.
  • Manufacturing & Packaging: Cardboard surface area optimization for cuboid boxes and wrapping paper calculations.
Topic 01 & 02

Basic Geometry Vocabulary: Points, Lines, Rays & Planes

Rigorous mathematical reasoning begins with clear foundational definitions:

  • Point: A dimensionless location in space having no length, width, or depth (denoted by a capital letter, e.g., $P$).
  • Line: A straight one-dimensional figure extending infinitely in both directions without endpoints ($\overleftrightarrow{AB}$).
  • Line Segment: A finite portion of a line bounded by two definite endpoints ($\overline{AB}$). Its length is measurable.
  • Ray: A portion of a line with one fixed endpoint that extends infinitely in one direction ($\overrightarrow{AB}$).
  • Plane: A flat, infinite two-dimensional surface determined by at least three non-collinear points.
  • Collinear Points: Points lying on the exact same straight line.
  • Coplanar Points/Lines: Geometric entities residing entirely within the same flat plane.
Figure 1.1: Visual anatomy of lines, segments, rays, and planar orientations.

Vocabulary Checkpoint (10 Questions)

Speed Drill
1. How many lines can pass through two distinct points?
Axiom: Exactly one unique straight line is determined by two distinct points.
Answer: Exactly 1 line
Speed Drill
2. How many distinct line segments are formed by 5 collinear points $A, B, C, D, E$?
Combinatorial Formula: Choosing 2 endpoints out of $n$ points: $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
For $n = 5$: $\frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10$ line segments.
Answer: 10 line segments
Try Yourself: Ray Direction

Are ray $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and ray $\overrightarrow{BA}$ the exact same geometric figure?

No: $\overrightarrow{AB}$ originates at endpoint $A$ and extends through $B$. $\overrightarrow{BA}$ originates at $B$ and extends in the opposite direction through $A$. They share only the segment $\overline{AB}$.
Topic 03 & 04

Angles & Angle Classifications

An angle is formed by two rays sharing a common initial point called the vertex. Angle magnitude measures the amount of rotation from the initial ray to the terminal ray in degrees ($^\circ$) or radians ($180^\circ = \pi\text{ rad}$).

Classification Degree Range Key Characteristic Example
Acute Angle $0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ$ Sharp, less than a quarter turn $30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ$
Right Angle $\theta = 90^\circ$ Perpendicular rays ($L$-shape) $90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad}$
Obtuse Angle $90^\circ < \theta < 180^\circ$ Blunt, between quarter and half turn $120^\circ, 135^\circ, 150^\circ$
Straight Angle $\theta = 180^\circ$ Forms a single continuous straight line $180^\circ = \pi\text{ rad}$
Reflex Angle $180^\circ < \theta < 360^\circ$ Greater than a straight line $210^\circ, 270^\circ, 315^\circ$
Complete Angle $\theta = 360^\circ$ One full revolution back to initial ray $360^\circ = 2\pi\text{ rad}$

Topic 04

Angle Relationships & Missing Angle Problems

Essential Angle Theorems
Complementary: $\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ$  |  Supplementary: $\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$
  • Vertically Opposite Angles: When two straight lines intersect, opposite angles are strictly equal ($\angle 1 = \angle 3, \angle 2 = \angle 4$).
  • Angles at a Point: Sum of all adjacent angles surrounding a point is $360^\circ$.
  • Linear Pair: Two adjacent angles on a straight line always sum to $180^\circ$.

FAST-Style Solved Angle Examples

Example 1 • Complementary Ratio
Two complementary angles are in the ratio $2 : 3$. Find the measure of the smaller angle.
1. Let angles be $2x$ and $3x$.
2. Since they are complementary: $2x + 3x = 90^\circ \implies 5x = 90^\circ \implies x = 18^\circ$.
3. Smaller angle $= 2x = 2(18^\circ) = 36^\circ$. (Larger $= 54^\circ$).
Answer: 36°
Example 2 • Supplementary Algebraic Equation
An angle is $24^\circ$ more than its supplement. Find the angle.
1. Let the angle be $\theta$. Its supplement is $(180^\circ - \theta)$.
2. Equation: $\theta = (180^\circ - \theta) + 24^\circ \implies 2\theta = 204^\circ \implies \theta = 102^\circ$.
3. Check: Supplement is $180^\circ - 102^\circ = 78^\circ$. Difference $= 102^\circ - 78^\circ = 24^\circ$.
Answer: 102°
Example 3 • Linear Pair with Variable
Two angles forming a linear pair are $(3x - 10)^\circ$ and $(2x + 30)^\circ$. Find the value of $x$.
1. Linear pair sum $= 180^\circ \implies (3x - 10) + (2x + 30) = 180$.
2. $5x + 20 = 180 \implies 5x = 160 \implies x = 32$.
3. Angles are $3(32) - 10 = 86^\circ$ and $2(32) + 30 = 94^\circ$. $86 + 94 = 180^\circ$.
Answer: x = 32
Topic 05

Parallel Lines & Transversals

When a straight line (the transversal $t$) intersects two parallel lines ($L_1 \parallel L_2$), it generates 8 distinct angles with key equivalence rules:

Figure 5.1: Angles formed by transversal $t$ crossing parallel lines $L_1 \parallel L_2$. Blue angles are equal acute angles ($\angle 1 = \angle 4 = \angle 5 = \angle 8$); Orange angles are equal obtuse angles ($\angle 2 = \angle 3 = \angle 6 = \angle 7$).
Angle Relationship Pairs Geometric Property
Corresponding Angles $(\angle 1, \angle 5), (\angle 2, \angle 6), (\angle 3, \angle 7), (\angle 4, \angle 8)$ Equal ($F$-pattern)
Alternate Interior Angles $(\angle 3, \angle 6), (\angle 4, \angle 5)$ Equal ($Z$-pattern)
Alternate Exterior Angles $(\angle 1, \angle 8), (\angle 2, \angle 7)$ Equal
Co-Interior (Consecutive Interior) $(\angle 3, \angle 5), (\angle 4, \angle 6)$ Supplementary ($\sum = 180^\circ$, $C$-pattern)
⚡
FAST Universal Two-Angle Rule for Parallel Lines
When a straight transversal cuts parallel lines, there are only TWO angle values on the entire diagram: acute ($\alpha$) and obtuse ($\beta$). Every acute angle equals every other acute angle ($\alpha = \alpha$). Every obtuse angle equals every other obtuse angle ($\beta = \beta$). Any acute + any obtuse always equals $180^\circ$ ($\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$).
Topics 06 – 10

🔺 Triangles: Classifications & Angle Properties

A triangle is a 3-sided 2D polygon with 3 vertices and 3 interior angles. The fundamental theorem of Euclidean triangle geometry states:

Fundamental Triangle Theorems
Interior Sum: $\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ$  |  Exterior: $\angle \text{Ext} = \angle A_{\text{opp}} + \angle B_{\text{opp}}$
  • Triangle Inequality Theorem: The sum of any two sides must strictly exceed the third side: $a + b > c$, $a + c > b$, $b + c > a$.
  • Side-Angle Ordering: The largest angle is always directly opposite the longest side; the smallest angle is opposite the shortest side.
Figure 6.1: Classification of Triangles by Sides and Angles.

FAST-Style Algebraic Triangle Problems

Algebraic Angles
The angles of a triangle are in the ratio $2x : 3x : 4x$. Find the measure of the largest angle.
1. Sum of interior angles is $180^\circ$: $2x + 3x + 4x = 180^\circ \implies 9x = 180^\circ \implies x = 20^\circ$.
2. Angles are: $2(20^\circ) = 40^\circ$, $3(20^\circ) = 60^\circ$, $4(20^\circ) = 80^\circ$.
3. Largest angle is $80^\circ$.
Answer: 80°
Exterior Angle Theorem
An exterior angle of a triangle is $125^\circ$. One of the opposite interior angles is $45^\circ$. Find the other opposite interior angle.
1. By the Exterior Angle Theorem: $\angle \text{Ext} = \angle A + \angle B$.
2. $125^\circ = 45^\circ + \angle B \implies \angle B = 125^\circ - 45^\circ = 80^\circ$.
Answer: 80°
Topics 11 – 14

Pythagorean Theorem, Triples & Special Right Triangles

Pythagorean Theorem
$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \iff c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$
Where $a$ and $b$ are the perpendicular legs, and $c$ is the hypotenuse (the side strictly opposite the $90^\circ$ angle, and always the strictly longest side).

Triangle Area Formulas & Heron's Formula

Standard & Advanced Area Formulas
Standard: $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$  |  Heron's: $A = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$
Where $s = \frac{a+b+c}{2}$ is the semi-perimeter. For an equilateral triangle with side $a$: $\text{Area} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2$.
Heron's Formula Worked Example
Find the exact area of a scalene triangle having side lengths $a = 13\text{ cm}$, $b = 14\text{ cm}$, $c = 15\text{ cm}$.
1. Compute semi-perimeter: $s = \frac{13 + 14 + 15}{2} = \frac{42}{2} = 21\text{ cm}$.
2. Differences: $(s-a) = 21-13 = 8$; $(s-b) = 21-14 = 7$; $(s-c) = 21-15 = 6$.
3. Area $= \sqrt{21 \times 8 \times 7 \times 6} = \sqrt{(3 \times 7) \times (8) \times 7 \times (2 \times 3)} = \sqrt{7^2 \times 3^2 \times 16} = 7 \times 3 \times 4 = 84\text{ cm}^2$.
Answer: 84 cm²
Topic 15

Similar Triangles vs. Congruent Triangles

A critical distinction frequently tested in FAST & scholarship mathematics:

  • Congruent Triangles ($\cong$): Identical shape AND identical size. Corresponding sides are strictly equal ($k=1$), and corresponding angles are equal. Criteria: SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS.
  • Similar Triangles ($\sim$): Identical shape, but DIFFERENT sizes. Corresponding angles are equal, and corresponding sides are in a constant linear ratio $k$: $\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} = k$.
🚀
FAST Power Law: The Area Ratio of Similar Figures
If two similar triangles have linear side ratio $\frac{\text{Side}_1}{\text{Side}_2} = k$, then their Perimeter ratio is $k$, and their Area ratio is strictly $k^2$! For 3D solids, their Volume ratio is strictly $k^3$.
Similar Triangles Area Ratio Problem
Two similar triangles $\Delta ABC \sim \Delta DEF$ have corresponding sides in the ratio $3 : 5$. If the area of $\Delta ABC$ is $36\text{ cm}^2$, find the area of $\Delta DEF$.
1. Linear ratio $k = \frac{3}{5}$.
2. Area ratio $= k^2 = \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{25}$.
3. Set up proportion: $\frac{\text{Area}(\Delta ABC)}{\text{Area}(\Delta DEF)} = \frac{9}{25} \implies \frac{36}{\text{Area}(\Delta DEF)} = \frac{9}{25}$.
4. $\text{Area}(\Delta DEF) = \frac{36 \times 25}{9} = 4 \times 25 = 100\text{ cm}^2$.
Answer: 100 cm²
Topics 16 – 20

Quadrilaterals, Polygons & Angle Sums

A quadrilateral is any four-sided polygon. The sum of all four interior angles is strictly $360^\circ$.

Quadrilateral Area Formula Perimeter Diagonal Properties
Square $A = a^2 = \frac{1}{2}d^2$ $P = 4a$ $d = a\sqrt{2}$; bisect each other at $90^\circ$; equal
Rectangle $A = l \times w$ $P = 2(l+w)$ $d = \sqrt{l^2 + w^2}$; bisect each other; equal
Parallelogram $A = b \times h$ $P = 2(a+b)$ Bisect each other; unequal (unless rectangle)
Rhombus $A = \frac{1}{2}d_1 d_2$ $P = 4a$ Perpendicular bisectors ($90^\circ$); $a^2 = (\frac{d_1}{2})^2 + (\frac{d_2}{2})^2$
Trapezium (Trapezoid) $A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$ $P = a+b+c+d$ Only 1 pair of opposite sides parallel ($a \parallel b$)
Kite $A = \frac{1}{2}d_1 d_2$ $P = 2(a+b)$ Diagonals intersect perpendicularly at $90^\circ$

Polygons & Regular Polygon Formulas

$n$-Sided Polygon General Rules
Interior Sum $= (n-2) \times 180^\circ$  |  Exterior Sum $= 360^\circ$ (Always)
For a Regular Polygon (all sides equal, all angles equal):
  • Each Interior Angle: $\frac{(n-2) \times 180^\circ}{n} = 180^\circ - \frac{360^\circ}{n}$
  • Each Exterior Angle: $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$
  • Number of Diagonals: $\frac{n(n-3)}{2}$
Regular Polygon Fast Calculation
A regular polygon has an interior angle of $150^\circ$. How many sides does it have, and how many diagonals can be drawn?
1. FAST Technique: Calculate exterior angle first! $\text{Ext Angle} = 180^\circ - 150^\circ = 30^\circ$.
2. Number of sides $n = \frac{360^\circ}{\text{Ext Angle}} = \frac{360^\circ}{30^\circ} = 12\text{ sides (Dodecagon)}$.
3. Diagonals $= \frac{n(n-3)}{2} = \frac{12(12-3)}{2} = \frac{12 \times 9}{2} = 54\text{ diagonals}$.
Answer: n = 12 sides, 54 diagonals
Topics 21 – 25

⭕ Circles, Sectors, Arcs & Composite Figures

A circle is the locus of all coplanar points equidistant from a fixed center $O$. The distance is the radius ($r$), and the distance across through the center is the diameter ($d = 2r$).

Figure 21.1: Circle anatomy, tangent perpendicularity, arc length, and sector area.
Circle, Arc & Sector Formulas
$C = 2\pi r = \pi d$  |  $A = \pi r^2$  |  $\text{Arc } L = \left(\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\right)2\pi r$  |  $\text{Sector } A = \left(\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\right)\pi r^2$
$\pi \approx \frac{22}{7} \approx 3.14159$. Note: Perimeter of a Sector $= \text{Arc Length } L + 2r = \left(\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\right)2\pi r + 2r$.
Sector Perimeter Exam Trap
A sector of a circle with radius $r = 14\text{ cm}$ has a central angle $\theta = 90^\circ$. Find the total perimeter of the sector. (Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$).
1. Arc length $L = \frac{90^\circ}{360^\circ} \times 2 \pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = \frac{1}{4} \times 88 = 22\text{ cm}$.
2. Total Perimeter of Sector $= \text{Arc } L + \text{radius} + \text{radius} = 22 + 14 + 14 = 50\text{ cm}$.
Answer: 50 cm

Topic 31

20 Fully Solved Entry-Test Word Problems

Every problem follows the standardized FAST solution protocol:

Understand → Draw/Visualize → Formula → Substitute → Calculate → Check Units → Final Answer
Problem 1 • Flooring & Tile Count
A rectangular conference room is $12\text{ m}$ long and $8\text{ m}$ wide. How many square marble tiles of side $40\text{ cm}$ are required to cover the entire floor?
1. Convert room dimensions to cm: Length $= 1200\text{ cm}$, Width $= 800\text{ cm}$.
2. Floor Area $= 1200 \times 800 = 960,000\text{ cm}^2$.
3. Area of 1 Tile $= 40 \times 40 = 1,600\text{ cm}^2$.
4. Number of tiles $= \frac{960,000}{1,600} = \frac{9600}{16} = 600\text{ tiles}$.
Answer: 600 tiles
Problem 2 • Circular Wheel Revolutions
A bicycle wheel has a diameter of $70\text{ cm}$. How many complete revolutions will it make to travel a distance of $1.1\text{ km}$? (Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$).
1. Distance in 1 revolution $= \text{Circumference } C = \pi d = \frac{22}{7} \times 70 = 220\text{ cm} = 2.2\text{ m}$.
2. Total distance $= 1.1\text{ km} = 1,100\text{ m}$.
3. Number of revolutions $= \frac{1,100\text{ m}}{2.2\text{ m}} = 500\text{ revolutions}$.
Answer: 500 revolutions
Problem 3 • Water Tank Volume & Capacity
A cylindrical water tank has base radius $r = 1.4\text{ m}$ and height $h = 5\text{ m}$. How many liters of water can it hold when completely full? (Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$).
1. Volume $V = \pi r^2 h = \frac{22}{7} \times (1.4)^2 \times 5 = \frac{22}{7} \times 1.96 \times 5 = 22 \times 0.28 \times 5 = 30.8\text{ m}^3$.
2. Since $1\text{ m}^3 = 1,000\text{ Liters}$, capacity $= 30.8 \times 1,000 = 30,800\text{ Liters}$.
Answer: 30,800 Liters
Problem 4 • Garden Pathway Area
A rectangular garden measures $30\text{ m} \times 20\text{ m}$. A uniform concrete walking path $2\text{ m}$ wide is built all around the outside of the garden. Find the area of the concrete path.
1. Inner Area (Garden) $= 30 \times 20 = 600\text{ m}^2$.
2. Outer Dimensions: Length $= 30 + 2(2) = 34\text{ m}$; Width $= 20 + 2(2) = 24\text{ m}$.
3. Outer Area $= 34 \times 24 = 816\text{ m}^2$.
4. Path Area $= \text{Outer Area} - \text{Inner Area} = 816 - 600 = 216\text{ m}^2$.
Answer: 216 m²
Problem 5 • Sphere Melting into Small Spheres
A solid metallic sphere of radius $6\text{ cm}$ is melted and recast into small solid spheres of radius $2\text{ cm}$ each. How many small spheres are obtained?
1. FAST Scale Law: Number of recast objects $= \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^3$.
2. Radius ratio $= \frac{6}{2} = 3$.
3. Number $= 3^3 = 27\text{ small spheres}$.
Answer: 27 spheres

🚀 15 FAST Speed Shortcuts & 20 Exam Traps

15 FAST Speed Shortcuts

01
Instant 3-4-5 Multiples: If legs are in ratio $3:4$, hypotenuse is immediately $5 \times \text{factor}$. E.g., legs $21$ and $28$ ($7 \times 3, 7 \times 4$) → Hypotenuse $= 7 \times 5 = 35$.
02
Square Diagonal Shortcut: Diagonal is always $a\sqrt{2}$. If given diagonal $d$, Area is simply $\frac{1}{2}d^2$.
03
Regular Polygon Exterior Angle: Always find exterior angle first ($\frac{360^\circ}{n}$). It is 3x faster than solving $(n-2)180/n$.
04
Similar Solids Volume Factor: When linear dimensions double ($2\times$), surface area quadruples ($4\times$), volume increases eightfold ($8\times$).
05
Cube Longest Space Diagonal: $d = a\sqrt{3}$. For a room $l, w, h$, longest rod $= \sqrt{l^2+w^2+h^2}$.

20 Dangerous Common Exam Traps

Trap 1: Diameter vs. Radius
❌ Using diameter directly into area formula $\pi d^2$.
✅ Always divide diameter by 2 first: $r = d/2$, then $A = \pi r^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$.
💡 Circle diameter given as 14 cm → Radius is 7 cm!
Trap 2: Internal Lines in Composite Perimeter
❌ Adding internal dividing lines to perimeter.
✅ Perimeter measures ONLY the outer boundary enclosing the shape.
💡 For a rectangle with attached semicircle, do NOT add the shared diameter line.
Trap 3: Solid Hemisphere Total Surface Area
❌ Writing $2\pi r^2$ for total surface area.
✅ A solid hemisphere has a flat circular base: $\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + \pi r^2 = 3\pi r^2$.
💡 Curved SA $= 2\pi r^2$, Total SA $= 3\pi r^2$.
Trap 4: Square Meters to Square Centimeters
❌ Writing $1\text{ m}^2 = 100\text{ cm}^2$.
✅ $1\text{ m}^2 = (100\text{ cm})^2 = 10,000\text{ cm}^2$.
💡 Square linear conversion factors for area; cube them for volume!

⚡ 7-Minute High-Yield Geometry Revision

Read through this concise high-yield checklist on exam morning:

  • Angle Rules: $\text{Complement} = 90^\circ - \theta$; $\text{Supplement} = 180^\circ - \theta$. Vertically opposite angles are strictly equal.
  • Parallel Lines: Transversal makes only 2 angle values ($\alpha, 180-\alpha$). Alternate interior and corresponding are equal; co-interior sum to $180^\circ$.
  • Triangles: Angles sum to $180^\circ$. Exterior angle equals sum of two opposite interior angles. Sum of any 2 sides $> 3\text{rd side}$.
  • Pythagorean Triples: $3-4-5$, $5-12-13$, $7-24-25$, $8-15-17$, $9-40-41$ and their multiples ($k$).
  • Special Triangles: $45^\circ-45^\circ-90^\circ$ is $1:1:\sqrt{2}$; $30^\circ-60^\circ-90^\circ$ is $1:\sqrt{3}:2$ ($1$ opposite $30^\circ$, $2$ is hypotenuse).
  • Quadrilaterals: Angles sum to $360^\circ$. Square diagonal $= a\sqrt{2}$, Area $= \frac{1}{2}d^2$. Rhombus Area $= \frac{1}{2}d_1 d_2$. Trapezium $= \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$.
  • Polygons: Interior sum $= (n-2)180^\circ$. Exterior angle of regular polygon $= \frac{360^\circ}{n}$. Diagonals $= \frac{n(n-3)}{2}$.
  • Circles: $C = 2\pi r$, $A = \pi r^2$. $\text{Arc } L = \frac{\theta}{360}2\pi r$, $\text{Sector } A = \frac{\theta}{360}\pi r^2$.
  • 3D Mensuration: Cylinder $V = \pi r^2 h, \text{CSA}=2\pi rh$. Cone $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$. Sphere $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3, A=4\pi r^2$. Solid Hemisphere $\text{TSA}=3\pi r^2$.
  • Scale Multipliers: Length multiplied by $k$ → Perimeter $\times k$, Area $\times k^2$, Volume $\times k^3$.
  • Unit Rules: $1\text{ m}^2 = 10,000\text{ cm}^2$, $1\text{ m}^3 = 1,000,000\text{ cm}^3 = 1,000\text{ Liters}$.
