Functional Groups & Structural Principles

Alcohol

R–OH
CH₃–CH₂–OH
  • Attachment: –OH attached to a saturated (sp³) carbon atom.
  • General Formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH or R–OH.
  • Example: Methanol (CH₃OH), Ethanol (C₂H₅OH).
  • Acidity: Very weakly acidic (pKa ~16–18); does not react with NaOH.

Phenol

Ar–OH
C₆H₅–OH
  • Attachment: –OH attached directly to an aromatic benzene ring (sp² carbon).
  • Also Known As: Carbolic Acid.
  • Example: Phenol (C₆H₅OH), o-Cresol, Resorcinol.
  • Acidity: Moderately acidic (pKa ~10); dissolves in dilute NaOH.

Ether

R–O–R′
CH₃–O–CH₃
  • Attachment: Divalent oxygen atom bonded to two alkyl/aryl groups.
  • General Formula: R–O–R′ or Ar–O–R.
  • Example: Dimethyl ether, Diethyl ether, Anisole.
  • H-Bonding: Cannot form intermolecular H-bonds with itself (no O–H bond).
⚠️

Crucial MDCAT Conceptual Distinction

Phenol is not simply an aromatic alcohol! Alcohols with phenyl groups like Benzyl Alcohol (C₆H₅CH₂OH) have –OH on an aliphatic sp³ side-chain carbon and behave as aliphatic primary alcohols. In Phenol (C₆H₅OH), the –OH is directly attached to the benzene nucleus, yielding resonance stabilization of the conjugate phenoxide ion.

Classification of Alcohols

Primary (1°), Secondary (2°), and Tertiary (3°) Alcohols

Alcohols are classified by the degree of substitution of the carbon atom directly bonded to the hydroxyl group (α-carbon).

Primary (1°) R–CH₂–OH

The α-carbon carrying the –OH group is attached to only one other carbon atom (or none, in methanol).

CH₃–CH₂–OH (Ethanol)
• Contains 2 α-hydrogens.
• Oxidizes to Aldehydes then Carboxylic Acids.
Secondary (2°) R₂CH–OH

The α-carbon carrying the –OH group is attached to two other carbon atoms.

CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃ (Propan-2-ol)
• Contains 1 α-hydrogen.
• Oxidizes to Ketones only.
Tertiary (3°) R₃C–OH

The α-carbon carrying the –OH group is attached to three other carbon atoms.

(CH₃)₃C–OH (2-Methylpropan-2-ol)
• Contains 0 α-hydrogens.
• Resistant to oxidation under normal conditions.
⭐

MDCAT Exam Trick

Always inspect the specific carbon directly attached to the –OH group, not the total molecular formula! For example, Isobutanol ((CH₃)₂CH–CH₂–OH) contains 4 total carbons with branching, yet it is a 1° primary alcohol because its –CH₂–OH carbon is bonded to only one adjacent carbon.

Classification by Number of Hydroxyl Groups (–OH)

Monohydric Alcohols

Contains exactly one –OH group.

CH₃CH₂OH (Ethanol)

Dihydric Alcohols (Glycols)

Contains exactly two –OH groups on adjacent carbons.

HO–CH₂–CH₂–OH (Ethane-1,2-diol)

Trihydric Alcohols

Contains three –OH groups.

CH₂(OH)CH(OH)CH₂(OH) (Glycerol)

IUPAC & Common Nomenclature

IUPAC Systematic Naming Rules

  1. Find the Longest Continuous Carbon Chain that contains the –OH group. The root alkane name will be modified.
  2. Number the Parent Chain from the end nearest the –OH group so the carbon carrying –OH gets the lowest possible locant.
  3. Replace the terminal '-e' of the parent alkane name with the suffix '-ol' (e.g. Propane → Propanol).
  4. Specify the Locant: Insert the position of the –OH group before the suffix or parent name (e.g. CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃ is Propan-2-ol).
  5. Name and Number Substituents: List all alkyl and halogen substituents alphabetically with their positions.

Physical Properties, Boiling Points & Solubility

1. Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding

The oxygen atom in the O–H bond is highly electronegative (δ–) while the hydrogen is electropositive (δ+). This creates strong dipole-dipole attractions called intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

R—O(δ–)—H(δ+) ••••• O(δ–)(R)—H(δ+) ••••• O(δ–)(R)—H

• Increases boiling and melting points considerably compared to alkanes and ethers of similar molar mass.
• Allows lower alcohols to form extensive H-bonds with water molecules.

2. Boiling Point Comparative Analysis

Alcohols have vastly higher boiling points than isomeric ethers and alkanes of comparable molecular weight because ethers cannot form hydrogen bonds with each other.

Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH, 46 g/mol): b.p. 78.3 °C (Strong H-Bonding)
Dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃, 46 g/mol): b.p. –24 °C (Dipole only, no O–H)
Propane (CH₃CH₂CH₃, 44 g/mol): b.p. –42 °C (London dispersion only)

MDCAT Conceptual Principle

An alcohol molecule consists of two parts: a hydrophilic (water-loving) polar –OH group and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) non-polar alkyl group R. As the hydrocarbon chain grows, the hydrophobic effect dominates, causing water solubility to drop dramatically.

Preparation of Alcohols

A. Acid-Catalyzed Hydration of Alkenes Industrial Route

Alkenes react with water (steam) in the presence of concentrated mineral acid (such as H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄) at elevated temperature and pressure to yield alcohols.

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O ──[ H₃PO₄ / 300°C / 60 atm ]──► CH₃CH₂OH
Markovnikov's Rule Application: For unsymmetrical alkenes (e.g. propene), the electrophilic proton attaches to the carbon with more hydrogens, generating Propan-2-ol as the major product:
CH₃–CH=CH₂ + H₂O ──[H⁺]──► CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃ (Propan-2-ol)
B. Biological Fermentation of Sugars Biochemical Route

Ethanol is produced biologically by the enzymatic breakdown of molasses or starch by yeast under anaerobic conditions at 25–35 °C.

C₆H₁₂O₆ (Glucose) ──[ Zymase (Yeast) ]──► 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂↑
• Enzymes in Yeast: Invertase (converts sucrose into glucose + fructose), Zymase (converts glucose to ethanol).
• Maximum Yield: Fermentation naturally stops when ethanol concentration reaches ~12–14% because high alcohol is toxic to yeast. Fractional distillation produces 95.6% rectified spirit.

Reactions of Alcohols (Chemical Lab)

1. Reaction with Active Metals (Na / K) O–H Cleavage
2 R–OH + 2 Na ──► 2 R–O⁻Na⁺ + H₂↑
Example: 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 Na → 2 C₂H₅ONa (Sodium ethoxide) + H₂↑
Bond Cleaved: O—H bond (proves weak acidic nature of alcohol).
Observation: Vigorous effervescence / bubbles of flammable Hydrogen gas evolved.
⭐ MDCAT Note: Reactivity order of alcohols toward Na metal: 1° > 2° > 3° (due to steric hindrance and +I effect of alkyl groups).
2. Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration Elimination
CH₃–CH₂–OH ──[ conc. H₂SO₄ / 170°C ]──► CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
Reagents: Concentrated H₂SO₄ or Al₂O₃ at heat.
Ease of Dehydration: 3° Alcohol > 2° Alcohol > 1° Alcohol.
Temperature Sensitive: 170°C produces Ethene; 140°C produces Diethyl Ether!
⭐ MDCAT Trap: Tertiary alcohols dehydrate most readily (even with 20% H₂SO₄ at 85°C) because they form the most stable 3° carbocation intermediate.
3. Esterification (Fischer Esterification) Reversible Condensation
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ◄══[ conc. H₂SO₄ ]══► CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O
Reactants: Carboxylic Acid + Alcohol (catalyzed by mineral acid).
Product: Ethyl ethanoate (Ester) + Water.
Observation: Characteristic sweet, pleasant fruity fragrance.
⭐ Isotopic Tracer Mechanism: Using O¹⁸ labeled alcohol proves that the –OH group comes from the Carboxylic Acid and only the –H atom comes from the Alcohol!
4. Halogenation (C–O Cleavage) Nucleophilic Sub.
R–OH + SOCl₂ ──[ Pyridine ]──► R–Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑
Reagents: SOCl₂ (Thionyl chloride), PCl₅, or PCl₃.
Best Method: SOCl₂ with pyridine is best because by-products (SO₂ and HCl) are gases and escape easily.
Pure alkyl chloride obtained without tedious separation.
⭐ MDCAT Point: Ease of C–O bond cleavage follows 3° > 2° > 1°.

MDCAT Golden Memory Formula

1° Alcohol ──► Aldehyde ──► Carboxylic Acid (Same number of carbons)
2° Alcohol ──► Ketone (Resistant to mild oxidation; severe oxidation cleaves C–C bonds giving acid mixture with fewer carbons)
3° Alcohol ──► Resistant under normal conditions (No α-hydrogen on C–OH carbon; undergoes elimination/dehydration to alkene under severe acidic conditions!)

Phenol: Structure, Acidity & Resonance Stabilization

Why is Phenol Far More Acidic than Aliphatic Alcohols? pKa ~ 10 vs pKa ~ 16–18

When phenol loses a proton (H⁺), it forms the Phenoxide ion (C₆H₅O⁻). The negative charge on oxygen is not confined to oxygen; it is delocalized through resonance into the ortho and para positions of the benzene ring.

Phenoxide Ion (Phenol Conjugate Base):

Negative charge is dispersed over 5 resonant positions (O atom and ortho/para carbons). High stability favors proton dissociation.

Alkoxide Ion (Alcohol Conjugate Base):

Negative charge is strictly localized on a single oxygen atom, and destabilized by the electron-donating (+I) alkyl group. Thus, alcohols are extremely weak acids.

Phenol + Sodium Metal H⁺ Displacement
2 C₆H₅OH + 2 Na ──► 2 C₆H₅ONa + H₂↑
Product: Sodium phenoxide + Hydrogen gas bubbles.
Shared by both alcohols and phenols.
Phenol + Sodium Hydroxide Acid-Base Neutralization
C₆H₅OH + NaOH ──► C₆H₅ONa + H₂O
Critical MDCAT Distinction: Phenol is acidic enough to neutralize aqueous NaOH, whereas aliphatic alcohols DO NOT react with NaOH!
Water-soluble Sodium Phenoxide is formed.
Phenol + Sodium Bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) Diagnostic Test
C₆H₅OH + NaHCO₃ ──► NO REACTION (No CO₂ effervescence)
Concept: Phenol is weaker than Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), so it cannot liberate CO₂ from NaHCO₃.
Distinguishes Phenol from Carboxylic Acids (Carboxylic acids give rapid effervescence of CO₂).
No bubbles / no gas evolved.
Bromination of Phenol (Bromine Water) Electrophilic Sub.
C₆H₅OH + 3 Br₂(aq) ──► 2,4,6-Tribromophenol↓ + 3 HBr
Activating Effect: –OH strongly activates the benzene ring and directs incoming Br⁺ to ortho & para positions simultaneously without a catalyst!
Observation: Instant WHITE PRECIPITATE of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Diagnostic test!
Nitration of Phenol Condition Dependent
C₆H₅OH + dilute HNO₃ (25°C) ──► o-Nitrophenol + p-Nitrophenol
Steam Distillation: o-Nitrophenol (intramolecular H-bonding, lower b.p., steam-volatile) is easily separated from p-Nitrophenol (intermolecular H-bonding, higher b.p.).
Conc. HNO₃ + Conc. H₂SO₄: Yields 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (Picric Acid).
Yields yellow crystalline Picric acid under concentrated conditions.
Neutral FeCl₃ Test for Phenol Confirmatory Test
6 C₆H₅OH + FeCl₃ ──► [Fe(OC₆H₅)₆]³⁻ + 3 H⁺ + 3 HCl
Product: Violet / Purple colored complex ion.
Immediate deep violet/purple coloration. Alcohols give NO color!

Ethers: Structure, Williamson Synthesis & Cleavage

1. Structure & Classification of Ethers

Ethers have the general formula R–O–R′ with an angular C–O–C bond angle (~110° in dimethyl ether due to repulsion between bulky alkyl groups).

Symmetrical (Simple) Ethers: Both alkyl groups are identical.
• Diethyl ether: C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅
• Dimethyl ether: CH₃–O–CH₃
Unsymmetrical (Mixed) Ethers: The two alkyl groups differ.
• Ethyl methyl ether: C₂H₅–O–CH₃ (Methoxyethane)
• Anisole (Methoxybenzene): C₆H₅–O–CH₃

2. Chemical Inertness & Solubility

Ethers lack an active acidic hydrogen (O–H) and have no unshared electron-deficient center, making them remarkably inert toward bases, reducing agents, and active metals at room temperature.

  • Excellent Solvents: Because of their chemical inertness and moderate dipole moment, ethers (like diethyl ether and THF) are widely used for Grignard reagents and organic extractions.
  • Hydrogen Bond Acceptance: Oxygen has two lone pairs and can accept H-bonds from water, granting lower ethers moderate water solubility.

⚗️ Williamson Ether Synthesis (Interactive Mechanism)

SN2 Mechanism

The most versatile laboratory method for preparing symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers via SN2 nucleophilic substitution of an alkyl halide by an alkoxide/phenoxide ion.

R–O⁻Na⁺ (Sodium Alkoxide) + R′–X (Alkyl Halide) ──► R–O–R′ (Ether) + NaX

✅ Ideal Preparation Example (Ethyl Methyl Ether):

C₂H₅O⁻Na⁺ + CH₃–I ──► C₂H₅–O–CH₃ + NaI

Uses a primary alkyl halide (CH₃I) to favor clean SN2 displacement without competing elimination.

⚠️ MDCAT Major Pitfall (3° Alkyl Halides):

(CH₃)₃C–Br + C₂H₅ONa ──► (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ (Alkene!) + C₂H₅OH + NaBr

Alkoxide ions are strong bases! When reacted with a 3° alkyl halide, E2 elimination occurs exclusively yielding an alkene, NOT an ether!

3. Cleavage of Ethers by Strong Acids (HI / HBr)

Ethers are cleaved by concentrated halogen acids (HI > HBr > HCl) at elevated temperature.

A. Cold / Limited Hydrogen Halide (1 Mole HI):
C₂H₅–O–CH₃ + HI ──► CH₃I + C₂H₅OH

Rule: The halogen attaches to the smaller alkyl group (via SN2 attack on the less sterically hindered methyl carbocation).

B. Hot Excess Hydrogen Halide (2 Moles HI):
C₂H₅–O–CH₃ + 2 HI ──► CH₃I + C₂H₅I + H₂O

Both alkyl groups are completely converted into alkyl iodides and water is eliminated.

Diagnostic Tests & Qualitative Analysis Matrix

Lucas Test: Distinguishing 1°, 2°, and 3° Alcohols

Lucas Reagent: Equimolar mixture of concentrated HCl and anhydrous ZnCl₂. Forms insoluble alkyl chloride turbidity.

Alcohol Class Observation with Lucas Reagent Reaction Speed / Mechanism
Tertiary Alcohol (3°) Immediate oily turbidity / cloudiness (within seconds) Fast SN1 via highly stable 3° carbocation.
Secondary Alcohol (2°) Turbidity appears after 5–10 minutes at room temperature Moderate rate via 2° carbocation.
Primary Alcohol (1°) No turbidity at room temp (cloudiness only upon heating) Slow SN2; unstable 1° carbocation.

Iodoform Test (Haloform Reaction)

Reagent: I₂ + NaOH (or NaOI). Positive test yields a yellow crystalline precipitate of Triiodomethane (CHI₃) with a characteristic medicinal odor.

💡

Structural Requirement for Alcohols

Only alcohols containing the CH₃–CH(OH)– group give a positive iodoform test.
• Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) is the ONLY primary alcohol that gives a positive iodoform test!
• Secondary alcohols with a terminal methyl group like Propan-2-ol and Butan-2-ol give a positive test.
• Methanol, Propan-1-ol, and 3° alcohols give a negative test.
