Core Fundamentals

Introduction to Inheritance

Key definitions forming the absolute foundation of genetic science for MDCAT.

🧬

What is Inheritance?

Inheritance is the biological process of transmitting genetic information and characteristics from parents to their offspring. It ensures both the continuity of species traits and the emergence of distinctive individual variations.

Transmission

Heredity

The transmission of physical, physiological, or biochemical traits from one generation to the next through genes.

Key Focus: Ensures that offspring resemble their parents in fundamental biological characteristics.
Diversity

Variation

The visible or biochemical differences in characteristics exhibited by individuals of the same species.

MDCAT Point: Arises due to crossing over, independent assortment of chromosomes, and spontaneous mutations.
Unit of Heredity

Gene

The basic physical and functional unit of heredity. Chemically, it is a specific segment of DNA located at a designated locus on a chromosome that encodes a specific polypeptide or functional RNA.

Mendel's Name: Mendel referred to genes as "factors" or "elementen".
Alternate Form

Allele

An alternative, contrasting form of a gene that occupies the exact same locus on a pair of homologous chromosomes.

Example: For the height gene (T), the dominant allele is T (Tall) and recessive allele is t (Dwarf).
Genetic Formula

Genotype

The complete genetic constitution or allelic combination of an organism with respect to one or more specific traits.

Examples: TT (Homozygous dominant), Tt (Heterozygous), tt (Homozygous recessive).
Observable Trait

Phenotype

The observable physical appearance, morphological structure, or physiological manifestation of an organism produced by the interaction of its genotype with environmental influences.

Formula: Phenotype = Genotype + Environment

Historical Milestone

Mendel & His Experiments

How Gregor Johann Mendel established the quantitative laws of biological inheritance.

🌱

Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884)

The Father of Classical Genetics

An Austrian Augustinian monk and teacher who performed groundbreaking quantitative hybridization experiments in his monastery garden at Brno (Czech Republic) from 1856 to 1863.

✓ Experimental Organism: Garden Pea plant (Pisum sativum).
✓ Paper Published: 1866 in the Proceedings of Natural History Society of Brünn.
✓ Rediscovery in 1900: Independently by Hugo de Vries (Holland), Carl Correns (Germany), and Erich von Tschermak (Austria).

Why Mendel Selected Pisum sativum

Exam Favorite

Mendel achieved success because of the ideal biological characteristics of the garden pea:

  • Clear Contrasting Traits: Possessed 7 pairs of easily distinguishable, sharply defined alternative characters without blending.
  • Short Generation Time: Annual herbaceous plant with a quick life cycle, allowing multiple generations to be analyzed in a short span.
  • Self-Pollinating Nature: Naturally self-pollinates due to enclosed flower structure (cleistogamy), producing stable pure-breeding / true-breeding lines.
  • Easy Artificial Hybridization: Can be easily cross-pollinated manually by removing anthers (emasculation) and dusting desired pollen.
  • Prolific Seed Production: Generates large numbers of seeds per cross, providing robust sample sizes for statistical rigor.

Mendel's Monohybrid Cross Workflow (Stem Height)

Parental (P₁) Cross
Pure Tall (TT) ✕ Pure Dwarf (tt)
Gametes: T and t
➔
F₁ Generation (First Filial)
100% Heterozygous Tall (Tt)

Dwarf trait completely disappears in F₁ due to dominance of T allele.

F₁ Self-Fertilization: Tt ✕ Tt
➔
F₂ Generation (Second Filial)
Phenotypic Ratio 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf (3:1)
Genotypic Ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1:2:1)

Dwarf phenotype reappears unchanged in F₂, proving alleles do not blend!

Core Principles

Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

The three fundamental postulates that govern classical transmission genetics.

1st Postulate

1. Law of Dominance

"When two contrasting alleles for a trait are brought together in a heterozygous individual (F₁ hybrid), only one allele expresses itself completely (the Dominant allele), while the other allele remains masked or unexpressed (the Recessive allele)."
Symbolic Demonstration:
T = Tall Allele (Dominant) + t = Dwarf Allele (Recessive)
TT ➔ Tall Phenotype (Homozygous dominant)
Tt ➔ Tall Phenotype (Heterozygote — T masks t)
tt ➔ Dwarf Phenotype (Homozygous recessive)
⚠️ MDCAT Note: Dominance is not universal. Incomplete dominance (e.g. Mirabilis jalapa pink flowers) and Co-dominance (e.g. AB Blood Group) are post-Mendelian exceptions.
1st Universal Law

2. Law of Segregation (Law of Purity of Gametes)

"The two alleles of a gene present in a diploid organism remain distinct and separate (segregate) from each other during gamete formation (meiosis), such that each gamete receives only one allele with equal probability."
Cytological Basis:

During Anaphase I of Meiosis, homologous chromosomes with their respective alleles separate and move to opposite poles. Hence, gametes are always pure for a given character.

A gamete will carry either T (50%) OR t (50%), but NEVER BOTH.
⭐ MDCAT High Yield: The Law of Segregation has no exceptions in sexually reproducing diploid organisms (as long as non-disjunction does not occur).
2nd Universal Law

3. Law of Independent Assortment

"When two pairs of traits are combined in a dihybrid cross, the segregation of alleles of one gene pair is completely independent of the segregation of alleles of another gene pair during gamete formation, provided the genes are on non-homologous chromosomes."
Dihybrid Cross: Seed Shape (R/r) & Seed Color (Y/y)
Phenotypic Class Genotype Formula Ratio in F₂ Proportion
Round Yellow (Both Dominant) R_Y_ (RRYY, RRYy, RrYY, RrYy) 9 9/16 (56.25%)
Round Green (Recombinant 1) R_yy (RRyy, Rryy) 3 3/16 (18.75%)
Wrinkled Yellow (Recombinant 2) rrY_ (rrYY, rrYy) 3 3/16 (18.75%)
Wrinkled Green (Both Recessive) rryy 1 1/16 (6.25%)
⚡ Universal Exception: Gene Linkage is the major exception to the Law of Independent Assortment. Linked genes on the same chromosome tend to travel together into gametes.

Essential Terminology

Important Genetic Terms & Crosses

Key definitions and diagnostic crosses tested repeatedly in PMDC entrance tests.

Genetic Term Precise Definition Genotype Example MDCAT Exam Importance
Homozygous An individual containing two identical alleles at a given locus on homologous chromosomes. Also termed "pure breed". TT or tt, IᴬIᴬ Produces only one type of gamete for that gene.
Heterozygous An individual containing two different/contrasting alleles at a given locus. Also called "hybrid". Tt, RrYy, IᴬIᴮ Produces multiple gamete types (2ⁿ where n = number of heterozygous pairs).
Dominant An allele that expresses its phenotypic trait in both homozygous (TT) and heterozygous (Tt) states. T for tall, R for round Capital letter notation; requires only one copy to manifest.
Recessive An allele whose phenotypic expression is masked in heterozygous state and only manifests when homozygous (tt). t for dwarf, r for wrinkled Lowercase notation; requires two copies (in autosomes) to express.
F₁ Generation First Filial generation; progeny produced from crossing two pure-breeding parents with contrasting traits. Parent: TT ✕ tt ➔ F₁: Tt 100% uniform hybrid exhibiting the dominant trait.
F₂ Generation Second Filial generation; progeny obtained by self-fertilization or intercrossing of F₁ individuals. F₁ Tt ✕ Tt ➔ F₂ Monohybrid: 3:1 phenotypic, 1:2:1 genotypic.
Monohybrid Cross A genetic cross between two individuals studying the inheritance pattern of a single pair of contrasting characters. Stem Height: TT ✕ tt Used to demonstrate the Law of Segregation.
Dihybrid Cross A genetic cross between two individuals studying the inheritance patterns of two distinct pairs of contrasting traits simultaneously. Seed Shape & Color: RRYY ✕ rryy Used to demonstrate the Law of Independent Assortment (9:3:3:1 ratio).
Test Cross A diagnostic cross of an individual having a dominant phenotype (unknown genotype TT or Tt) with a homozygous recessive individual (tt). T_ ✕ tt • If 100% Tall ➔ Parent was TT
• If 1:1 (50% Tall : 50% Dwarf) ➔ Parent was Tt
Diagnostic Tool

Deep Dive: How a Test Cross Works in MDCAT

Case 1: Unknown Parent is Homozygous Dominant
TT (Tall) ✕ tt (Dwarf)
Gametes: T from parent, t from tester
Offspring: 100% Tt (All Tall)

✅ Conclusion: If all offspring show dominant phenotype, unknown parent is Homozygous (TT).

Case 2: Unknown Parent is Heterozygous
Tt (Tall) ✕ tt (Dwarf)
Gametes: 50% T, 50% t ✕ t
Offspring: 50% Tt (Tall) : 50% tt (Dwarf)

✅ Conclusion: If offspring segregate in a 1:1 ratio, unknown parent is Heterozygous (Tt).

Chromosome Mechanics

Gene Linkage

Physical co-localization of genes on the same chromosome and its genetic consequences.

Definition & Key Concepts

Gene linkage is the physical association and tendency of two or more genes located on the same chromosome to be inherited together as a unit into the same gamete during meiosis.

🔗
Linked Genes: Genes situated on the same chromosome that do not assort independently.
📏
Distance Rule: The physical distance between linked genes determines their linkage strength. Closer genes = stronger linkage (fewer crossovers).
🧬
Linkage Group: All the genes located on a single chromosome form one linkage group. The number of linkage groups equals the haploid (n) chromosome number.

Linkage Groups in Organisms (MDCAT Data)

Organism Diploids (2n) Linkage Groups (n)
Human Female (XX) 46 23
Human Male (XY) 46 24 (22 autosomes + X + Y)
Garden Pea (Pisum sativum) 14 7
Fruit Fly (Drosophila melanogaster) 8 4
Maize (Zea mays) 20 10
*Notice: Human male has 24 linkage groups because X and Y carry largely non-homologous gene sets.

Meiotic Recombination

Crossing Over (Genetic Recombination)

The mechanism responsible for generating novel allelic combinations and genetic diversity.

🔄

Definition of Crossing Over

Crossing over is the reciprocal physical exchange of genetic material (DNA segments) between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes occurring during Prophase I of Meiosis.

Stage 1: Synapsis

Zygotene

Maternal and paternal homologous chromosomes pair up gene-for-gene with the aid of the synaptonemal complex to form a bivalent or tetrad.

Stage 2: Recombination (MDCAT ⭐)

Pachytene

Crossing over actually takes place here! Non-sister chromatids break and rejoin at precise homologous points with the enzyme recombinase.

Stage 3: Chiasma Appearance

Diplotene

Synaptonemal complex dissolves; homologous chromosomes begin to repel each other but remain held at X-shaped crossover junctions called chiasmata.

Stage 4: Separation

Diakinesis

Terminalization of chiasmata occurs; nuclear envelope disintegrates and meiotic spindle forms for metaphase I alignment.

Recombination Frequency & Gene Mapping Formula

Recombination Frequency (%) = (Total Number of Recombinant Offspring / Total Number of Offspring) ✕ 100

• 1% Recombination Frequency = 1 Map Unit = 1 Centimorgan (cM) (named in honor of Thomas Hunt Morgan).
• Maximum recombination frequency between two linked genes cannot exceed 50%.

Sex Chromosome Genetics

X-Linked Recessive Inheritance

Why sex-linked traits follow distinct transmission patterns between males and females.

Sex Chromosome Composition in Humans

♀ Human Female
44 Autosomes + XX

Homogametic sex; produces only one type of egg (22 + X). Females have two X chromosomes.

♂ Human Male
44 Autosomes + XY

Heterogametic sex; produces 50% (22 + X) and 50% (22 + Y) sperm. Males are hemizygous.

🎯 MDCAT Core Question

Why are X-linked recessive traits far more common in males?

Because human males have only one X chromosome (Hemizygous XY).

  • In Males: If a male receives just one mutant recessive allele on his solitary X chromosome (XʳY), he will 100% express the disease because there is no second X chromosome to provide a healthy dominant allele.
  • In Females: Females have two X chromosomes (XX). For a female to express the disorder, she must inherit two mutant alleles (XʳXʳ). If she inherits one (XᴿXʳ), she remains an asymptomatic healthy carrier.

Clinical Genetics Example

Hemophilia ("Royal Disease")

X-linked recessive bleeding disorder and criss-cross inheritance rules.

Bleeding Disorder

Clinical Pathology of Hemophilia

Xᴴ = Normal clotting allele (Dominant) Xʰ = Hemophilia allele (Recessive)

Hemophilia is a genetically inherited bleeding disorder where blood fails to clot normally due to the congenital absence or deficiency of specific clotting factors. Minor injuries can cause fatal prolonged hemorrhage.

Sex Genotype Phenotype / Clinical Status Clotting Factor Status
Female (♀) XᴴXᴴ Normal Female 100% Normal Clotting
Female (♀) XᴴXʰ Carrier Female (Phenotypically Normal) Normal clotting (Carries 1 mutant allele)
Female (♀) XʰXʰ Hemophilic Female (Extremely Rare / Often Fatal) Severe Clotting Deficiency
Male (♂) XᴴY Normal Male 100% Normal Clotting
Male (♂) XʰY Hemophilic Male (Affected) Severe Clotting Deficiency

Criss-Cross Inheritance Rules for MDCAT

Rule 1
Father ➔ Son Transmission is IMPOSSIBLE

An affected hemophilic father (XʰY) contributes his Y chromosome to all his sons. Therefore, a father CAN NEVER transmit an X-linked trait to his sons.

Rule 2
Father ➔ 100% Daughters are Carriers

An affected father gives his only Xʰ chromosome to all his daughters. If the mother is normal (XᴴXᴴ), 100% of daughters will be obligate carriers (XᴴXʰ).

Rule 3
Carrier Mother ➔ 50% Sons Affected

A carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) has a 50% chance of passing Xʰ to any son, making him hemophilic (XʰY), and a 50% chance of passing Xʰ to daughters, making them carriers.

Exam Goldmine

MDCAT High-Yield Points & Traps

Key examiner favorites, edge cases, and numerical shortcuts for maximum score.

Fact #1 Purity of Gametes

Gametes are NEVER Heterozygous

Because of the Law of Segregation, the two alleles of a gene separate during Anaphase I. A gamete always contains only one allele of each gene pair and is strictly pure.

Fact #2 Test Cross Ratios

Diagnostic Test Cross Ratios

• Monohybrid Test Cross (Tt ✕ tt) = 1 : 1
• Dihybrid Test Cross (RrYy ✕ rryy) = 1 : 1 : 1 : 1
• Trihybrid Test Cross = 1:1:1:1:1:1:1:1

Fact #3 Formula Shortcuts

Genetics Mathematical Shortcuts (n = heterozygous pairs)

• Types of gametes produced = 2ⁿ
• Types of F₂ phenotypes = 2ⁿ
• Types of F₂ genotypes = 3ⁿ
• Total F₂ zygotic combinations = 4ⁿ

Fact #4 Crossing Over Stage

Pachytene vs Diplotene Trap

Recombination / crossing over occurs in Pachytene stage of Prophase I. However, Chiasmata (X-shaped physical points) become visible in the subsequent Diplotene stage.

Fact #5 Sex-Linked Traits

Criss-Cross Transmission Pattern

X-linked traits pass from affected grandfather ➔ carrier daughter ➔ affected grandson. Traits skip a generation in females due to carrier status.

Fact #6 Linkage Exception

Mendel's 2nd Law Exception

The Law of Independent Assortment is NOT universal. It applies strictly to genes on different chromosomes or genes far apart on the same chromosome. Linked genes violate it.
