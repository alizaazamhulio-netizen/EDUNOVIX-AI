What is Motion?

💡 Understand

Motion is the change in position of an object with respect to a reference point as time passes.

A car moving along a road changes its position with time, so it is in motion. But to judge motion, we must compare the object's position against a reference point — a fixed object or location we consider "stationary".

Real-life example: A passenger sitting inside a moving bus is at rest relative to another passenger, but moving relative to a person standing on the road. Motion is always relative to the observer.
🧠 Think About It

How do we know that an object has moved?

An object has moved if its position changes relative to a reference point over time. If the distance from the reference point changes, the object is in motion. If it stays the same, the object is at rest relative to that point.

02

Distance & Displacement

💡 Understand

Distance is the total path length travelled by an object. It is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude only and is always positive.

Displacement is the straight-line change in position from the initial point to the final point, including direction. It is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.

Example: If a student walks 5 m east and then 5 m west:
Distance = 10 m (total path walked)
Displacement = 0 m (start and end are the same point)
⚠ Common Trap
Distance is scalar, displacement is vector. Distance can never be negative. Displacement can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the chosen direction.

03

Speed & Velocity

💡 Understand

Speed tells us how fast an object covers distance. Speed = Distance / Time. It is a scalar quantity. SI unit: m/s.

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement. v = Δx / Δt. It is a vector — it has magnitude and direction. SI unit: m/s.

Speed
Scalar · Distance/time · No direction · Cannot be negative
Velocity
Vector · Displacement/time · Has direction · Can be +/−
🎯 MDCAT Focus

A car going 60 km/h north and another going 60 km/h south have the same speed but different velocities — because direction matters for velocity.

04

Acceleration

💡 Understand

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time.

a = (vf − vi) / t

Where vi = initial velocity, vf = final velocity, t = time. SI unit: m/s².

Positive acceleration: velocity increases in the chosen positive direction. Negative acceleration (deceleration): velocity decreases when acceleration acts opposite to velocity.

⚠ Common Trap
Zero acceleration does NOT mean zero velocity. It means velocity is constant — the object can be moving fast, just not speeding up or slowing down.

05

Equations of Motion

For uniform acceleration, these three equations connect velocity, displacement, acceleration, and time.

v = u + at
First Equation
Use when you have u, a, t and need v.
s = ut + ½at²
Second Equation
Use when you have u, a, t and need s.
v² = u² + 2as
Third Equation
Use when you have u, a, s and need v (no time).
s = ½(u + v)t
Fourth Equation
Use when you have u, v, t and need s (no acceleration).
u = initial velocity v = final velocity a = acceleration s = displacement t = time

06

Force

💡 Understand

A force is an interaction that can change an object's motion — it can make a stationary object move, speed it up, slow it down, change its direction, or change its shape.

Force is a vector quantity. SI unit: Newton (N), where 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

📌 Remember

Net force determines acceleration. If the net force on an object is zero, its velocity does not change (Newton's First Law).

Newton's Laws of Motion

First Law

Law of Inertia

An object remains at rest, or moving with constant velocity, unless an unbalanced external force acts on it.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion. Greater mass → greater inertia.

Interactive: Bus Inertia Demo

Click a scenario to see how inertia affects the passenger.

Seat belt
When a car suddenly stops, your body tends to continue moving forward because of inertia.
Bus
When a stationary bus suddenly starts, passengers lean backward because their bodies tend to remain at rest.
Tablecloth
A quick pull leaves dishes in place — they resist motion due to inertia.
Dust from carpet
Beating a carpet dislodges dust — the dust stays in place while the carpet moves.
Second Law

F = ma

Newton's Second Law connects force, mass, and acceleration: F = ma. Greater force → greater acceleration. Greater mass → smaller acceleration for the same force.

Interactive: F = ma Simulator

Mass:5 kg
Force:10 N
Acceleration:2.00 m/s²

Force vs Acceleration Graph

As force increases (with constant mass), acceleration increases linearly. The slope of the line equals 1/m.

🔢 Numerical Example 1

Question: A net force of 8 N acts on a 2 kg object. Find the acceleration.

Given: F = 8 N, m = 2 kg

Formula: F = ma → a = F/m

Substitution: a = 8 / 2 = 4 m/s²

Answer: 4 m/s²

🔢 Numerical Example 2

Question: A 5 kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². What force is needed?

Given: m = 5 kg, a = 2 m/s²

Formula: F = ma

Substitution: F = 5 × 2 = 10 N

Answer: 10 N

Third Law

Action & Reaction

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. When object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction on object A.

⚠ Common Trap
Action and reaction are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on DIFFERENT objects. They do not cancel each other because they act on different bodies.

Interactive: Action-Reaction

The person pushes the box right (green), and the box pushes the person left (red). Equal magnitude, opposite direction, different objects.

Walking
You push the ground backward; the ground pushes you forward.
Swimming
You push water backward; water pushes you forward.
Rocket propulsion
Exhaust gases are pushed downward; the rocket is pushed upward.

Weight & Friction

Weight

Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object: W = mg. Mass stays essentially constant everywhere, but weight changes if gravitational field strength changes.

Interactive: Earth vs Moon Weight

Mass:10 kg
Gravity (g):9.8 m/s²
Weight:98.0 N

Mass stays the same on Earth and Moon. Only weight changes because g is different.

🎯 MDCAT Focus

Mass is the amount of matter in an object — it does not change with location. Weight is the force of gravity on that mass — it depends on the local gravitational field strength.

🔢 Numerical Example

Question: An 8 kg object is on Earth. Find its weight (g = 9.8 m/s²).

Given: m = 8 kg, g = 9.8 m/s²

Formula: W = mg

Substitution: W = 8 × 9.8 = 78.4 N

Answer: 78.4 N

Friction

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion between surfaces in contact.

Static friction: acts when surfaces are not sliding relative to each other. It prevents motion from starting.

Kinetic friction: acts when surfaces are sliding relative to each other. It opposes ongoing motion.

🧠 Think

Is friction always bad? No! Without friction, we couldn't walk, cars couldn't brake, and we couldn't hold objects. Friction is useful for walking, braking, writing, and gripping. It's harmful when it causes wear, heat, and energy loss in machinery.

Walking
Friction between feet and ground lets us push forward.
Car brakes
Friction between brake pads and wheels stops the car.
Writing
Friction between pencil and paper leaves marks.
Holding objects
Friction between fingers and objects prevents slipping.

Free-Body Diagram (FBD)

A Free-Body Diagram shows all external forces acting on an object. It is the first step in solving any force problem: draw the object → identify forces → choose directions → apply Newton's laws.

Interactive: FBD Builder

💡 Understand

How to construct an FBD:

  1. Isolate the object — draw it alone, separated from its surroundings.
  2. Draw all external forces — weight, normal, applied, friction, tension, etc.
  3. Choose coordinate directions — pick x and y axes for the problem.
  4. Resolve forces if needed — break angled forces into x and y components.
  5. Apply Newton's laws — ΣF = ma in each direction.
09

Momentum & Conservation

💡 Understand

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity: p = mv. It is a vector quantity. SI unit: kg·m/s.

A heavier object or a faster object generally has greater momentum. A heavy slow truck can have similar momentum to a light fast bullet.

Conservation of Momentum: For an isolated system, total initial momentum = total final momentum.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Interactive: Momentum Calculator

Mass:2 kg
Velocity:5 m/s
Momentum (p = mv):10 kg·m/s

Interactive: Two-Cart Collision

Initial Total Momentum:8 kg·m/s
Final Total Momentum:8 kg·m/s
🎯 MDCAT Focus

In an isolated system (no external force), total momentum is always conserved. The carts may exchange momentum, but the total stays the same.

🔢 Numerical Example

Question: A 2 kg ball moving at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg ball. After collision, the 2 kg ball stops. Find the velocity of the 1 kg ball.

Given: m₁ = 2 kg, u₁ = 3 m/s, m₂ = 1 kg, u₂ = 0, v₁ = 0

Formula: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Substitution: 2(3) + 1(0) = 2(0) + 1(v₂) → 6 = v₂

Answer: v₂ = 6 m/s

Circular Motion

💡 Understand

When an object moves along a circular path, its velocity continuously changes direction. Therefore, even if speed remains constant, the object is accelerating.

The inward force required for circular motion is called centripetal force:

Fc = mv² / r
⚠ Common Trap
Constant speed in circular motion can still mean acceleration — because velocity is a vector and its direction is changing. Any change in velocity (magnitude OR direction) means acceleration.

2-Minute Revision

⚡ Quick Revision Bullets

  • Motion = change in position relative to a reference point over time.
  • Distance = total path (scalar, always positive). Displacement = straight-line start to end (vector, can be zero/negative).
  • Speed = distance/time (scalar). Velocity = displacement/time (vector).
  • Acceleration = rate of change of velocity. a = (v−u)/t. Unit: m/s².
  • Equations of motion: v = u+at, s = ut+½at², v² = u²+2as, s = ½(u+v)t.
  • 1st Law: Object stays at rest or constant velocity unless net force acts (inertia).
  • 2nd Law: F = ma. More force → more accel. More mass → less accel.
  • 3rd Law: Action-reaction equal, opposite, on different objects.
  • Weight W = mg. Mass constant, weight changes with g.
  • Friction opposes relative motion. Static (no sliding) vs kinetic (sliding).
  • Momentum p = mv. Conserved in isolated systems.
  • Centripetal force Fc = mv²/r. Direction changes → acceleration even at constant speed.

🔥 10 Things You Must Remember

  1. Distance is scalar; displacement is vector. Distance ≥ |displacement|.
  2. Speed is scalar; velocity is vector. Same speed, different velocity = different directions.
  3. Zero acceleration means constant velocity, NOT zero velocity.
  4. Newton's 1st Law is the law of inertia. Mass measures inertia.
  5. F = ma. Force causes acceleration, not velocity.
  6. Action and reaction never cancel — they act on different objects.
  7. Mass ≠ weight. Mass is constant; weight = mg changes with gravity.
  8. Momentum p = mv is conserved in isolated systems (no external force).
  9. Circular motion at constant speed still has acceleration (direction changes).
  10. Friction opposes relative motion — useful for walking/braking, harmful for machinery wear.
