Human Physiology kya hai? (Overview & Organization)

Foundations of physiological function, homeostatic coordination, and biological hierarchy.

📖

Definition of Human Physiology

Human Physiology is the branch of biological science that deals with the study of mechanical, physical, and biochemical functions of humans, their organs, tissues, and the cells of which they are composed.

Human physiology body ke organs, tissues aur cells ke normal functioning aur apas ke coordination ka study hai jo life aur homeostasis ko sustain karta hai.

Levels of Structural Organization

The human body is structured in a hierarchy of increasing structural complexity:

1
Chemical Level

Atoms (C, H, O, N, Ca, P) combine to form biological macromolecules (Proteins, Lipids, Carbohydrates, DNA/RNA).

→
2
Cellular Level

The basic structural & functional unit of life (e.g. Neurons, Erythrocytes, Myocytes, Nephrons).

→
3
Tissue Level

Group of similar cells performing a specialized function: Epithelial, Connective, Muscle, Nervous tissues.

→
4
Organ Level

Two or more primary tissues joined together to perform specific functions (Heart, Lungs, Kidneys, Stomach).

→
5
Organ System

A group of organs working in synchronized harmony to maintain vital systemic balance.

02

Nervous System (Nerve Impulse & Reflex Arc)

Information processing, neuron anatomy, synapse transmission, and rapid reflex pathways.

Divisions

1. Central Nervous System (CNS)

The processing and control center of the entire body.

  • Brain: Forebrain (Cerebrum, Thalamus, Hypothalamus), Midbrain, Hindbrain (Cerebellum, Pons, Medulla Oblongata).
  • Spinal Cord: Conducts 2-way impulses between brain and PNS; centers simple spinal reflexes.
Divisions

2. Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

All neural tissue outside the brain and spinal cord.

  • Somatic Nervous System: Controls voluntary skeletal muscle contractions.
  • Autonomic Nervous System: Involuntary regulation of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands (Sympathetic "Fight or Flight" & Parasympathetic "Rest and Digest").

03

Circulatory System (Heart, Blood & Double Circulation)

Heart chambers, cardiac cycle, vascular circuits, and blood plasma formed elements.

Anatomy

The Four Chambers & Valves

  • Right Atrium: Receives deoxygenated blood from body via Superior & Inferior Vena Cava.
  • Right Ventricle: Pumps deoxygenated blood to lungs via Pulmonary Trunk/Arteries (low pressure).
  • Left Atrium: Receives oxygenated blood from lungs via 4 Pulmonary Veins.
  • Left Ventricle: ⭐ Has the thickest muscular myocardium; generates high pressure to pump oxygenated blood to the entire systemic body via the Aorta.
  • Tricuspid Valve: Between Right Atrium & Right Ventricle.
  • Bicuspid (Mitral) Valve: Between Left Atrium & Left Ventricle.
  • Semilunar Valves: Aortic & Pulmonary semilunar valves prevent backflow into ventricles during diastole.
Hematology

Blood Components (Plasma & Cells)

  • Plasma (55% of blood): 90-92% water, 7-8% plasma proteins (Albumin for osmotic balance, Globulins for antibodies, Fibrinogen for clotting), hormones, urea, glucose.
  • Red Blood Cells (RBCs / Erythrocytes): Biconcave discs, no nucleus or mitochondria at maturity, lifespan ~120 days. Packed with Haemoglobin for O₂ & CO₂ transport.
  • White Blood Cells (WBCs / Leucocytes): Nucleated defense cells. Phagocytes (Neutrophils, Monocytes) and Lymphocytes (B & T cells).
  • Platelets (Thrombocytes): Non-nucleated cytoplasmic fragments of megakaryocytes; essential for blood clotting and hemostasis.

04

Respiratory System (Tract, Gas Exchange & Ventilation)

Airway anatomy, alveolar structural adaptations, and the mechanics of inspiration and expiration.

Anatomical Air Pathway

Nose / Nasal Cavity ➔ Pharynx ➔ Larynx (Epiglottis) ➔ Trachea ➔ Bronchi ➔ Bronchioles ➔ Alveoli (Exchange Site)
Adaptations

Why Alveoli are Optimal for Gas Exchange

  • Huge Surface Area: Over 300 million alveoli provide ~70–100 m² of surface area.
  • Extremely Thin Diffusion Barrier: Single layer of simple squamous epithelial cells (< 1 µm thick).
  • Dense Capillary Meshwork: Red blood cells flow in single file maximizing O₂ saturation.
  • Moist Surface with Surfactant: Phospholipid surfactant reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse during expiration.
Mechanics

Inspiration vs Expiration Summary

  • Inspiration (Active): Diaphragm contracts (moves downwards/flattens), External intercostals contract (ribs move up & out) ➔ Thoracic volume increases ➔ Intra-pulmonary pressure decreases below atmosphere ➔ Air rushes IN.
  • Expiration (Passive at rest): Diaphragm relaxes (domes upward), External intercostals relax (ribs move down & in) ➔ Thoracic volume decreases ➔ Intra-pulmonary pressure rises above atmosphere ➔ Air forced OUT.

05

Digestive System (GI Tract, Enzymes & Villi Absorption)

Mechanical breakdown, gastric acid secretions, pancreatic enzymes, and small intestinal villi.

Alimentary Canal Sequence

Mouth (Oral Cavity) ➔ Oesophagus ➔ Stomach ➔ Small Intestine (Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum) ➔ Large Intestine ➔ Rectum & Anus
Stomach

Gastric Digestion

HCl (pH 1.5–2.0): Kills ingested bacteria & converts inactive Pepsinogen into active Pepsin.

Pepsin: Initiates protein digestion into peptones and polypeptides.

Mucus: Protects gastric epithelium from autodigestion by acid.

Small Intestine

Villi & Maximum Absorption

Duodenum: Receives Bile (from liver; emulsifies fats) and Pancreatic juice (Lipase, Trypsin, Amylase).

Villi & Microvilli: ⭐ Increase absorption surface area ~600 times.

Lacteals: Central lymph vessels absorbing fatty acids & glycerol.

Large Intestine

Water & Faeces

Primary Function: Absorption of water, electrolytes, and specific vitamins.

Microbiota: Symbiotic colonic bacteria synthesize Vitamin K and certain B vitamins.

Rectum: Stores semi-solid faeces until defecation.

06

Urinary System (Kidneys, Nephron & Osmoregulation)

Ultrafiltration, selective reabsorption, tubular secretion, and countercurrent multiplication.

Organs

Main Organs & Kidney Functions

  • Two Kidneys: Bean-shaped retroperitoneal organs receiving 20-25% of cardiac output via Renal Arteries.
  • Two Ureters: Muscular tubes transporting urine from renal pelvis to bladder via peristalsis.
  • Urinary Bladder: Expandable reservoir lined with transitional epithelium.
  • Urethra: Canal for urine excretion to exterior.
  • Functions: Excretion of nitrogenous urea, uric acid, creatinine; osmoregulation of body fluid volume; electrolyte balance (Na+, K+, Cl-); maintenance of arterial blood pH (7.35–7.45).
Processes

The Three Steps of Urine Formation

  • 1. Ultrafiltration: Blood filtered under high hydrostatic pressure in Glomerulus into Bowman's Capsule. Cells and plasma proteins remain in blood.
  • 2. Selective Reabsorption: ~99% of filtrate reabsorbed back into peritubular capillaries (100% of glucose & amino acids in PCT; Na+, water throughout).
  • 3. Tubular Secretion: Active transport of excess H+, K+, creatinine, and drug metabolites from blood into renal tubule.

Glomerulus & Bowman's Capsule (Malpighian Body)

Renal Cortex

Endocrine System (Glands, Hormones & Glucose Balance)

Ductless gland regulation, feedback loops, insulin/glucagon antagonism, and emergency hormones.

🧪

What are Hormones?

Hormones are organic chemical messengers synthesized by specialized ductless (endocrine) glands, secreted directly into blood circulation, and acting upon specific distant target cells equipped with complementary receptors.

Insulin

📉
Target Organs: Liver, Skeletal Muscle, Adipose tissue
Main Action: Lowers Blood Glucose (Hypoglycemic)
Mechanism: Stimulates cellular glucose uptake, Glycogenesis (glucose ➔ glycogen), and lipogenesis.

Glucagon

📈
Target Organs: Primarily Liver
Main Action: Raises Blood Glucose (Hyperglycemic)
Mechanism: Promotes Glycogenolysis (glycogen ➔ glucose) and Gluconeogenesis in fasting states.
⭐ Direct antagonist to Insulin
Hypothalamus (stored in Post. Pituitary)

ADH (Vasopressin)

💧
Target Organs: Nephron Collecting Ducts & DCT
Main Action: Conserves Water & Concentrates Urine
Mechanism: Inserts Aquaporin-2 water channels, drastically increasing water reabsorption.
⭐ Deficiency leads to Diabetes Insipidus (large volumes of dilute urine)
Thyroid Gland (Follicles)

Thyroxine (T₄ / T₃)

🔥
Target Organs: Almost all somatic body cells
Main Action: Controls Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)
Mechanism: Increases cellular respiration, ATP turnover, body heat generation & growth.
⭐ Requires Iodine; deficiency in infancy causes Cretinism
Adrenal Medulla

Adrenaline (Epinephrine)

⚡
Target Organs: Heart, Blood vessels, Bronchioles, Liver
Main Action: "Fight or Flight" Emergency Response
Mechanism: Increases heart rate, cardiac output, pupil dilation, and diverts blood to skeletal muscles.
⭐ Rapidly secreted under sympathetic stimulation
Adrenal Cortex (Mineralocorticoid)

Aldosterone

🧂
Target Organs: Nephron Distal Convoluted Tubules
Main Action: Na⁺ Retention & K⁺ Excretion
Mechanism: Increases Na+ reabsorption and secondary water retention, raising blood pressure.
⭐ Key regulator of Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)

Musculoskeletal System (Bones, Joints & Antagonistic Muscles)

Skeletal divisions, muscle tissue classification, and the biomechanics of antagonistic muscle pairs.

Skeleton

Human Skeletal Divisions (206 Bones)

  • Axial Skeleton (80 bones): Skull/Cranium (28), Vertebral Column (26), Ribs (12 pairs = 24), Sternum (1), Hyoid bone (1).
  • Appendicular Skeleton (126 bones): Pectoral Girdle & Upper Limbs (64), Pelvic Girdle & Lower Limbs (62).
  • Functions: Support, delicate organ protection (brain, heart, lungs), mineral calcium storage, and blood cell formation (hematopoiesis) in red bone marrow.
Muscles

Antagonistic Muscle Action (Biceps & Triceps)

  • Muscles can only pull (contract); they cannot actively push. Therefore, they work in antagonistic pairs.
  • Flexion of Arm: Biceps contracts (Flexor) while Triceps relaxes.
  • Extension of Arm: Triceps contracts (Extensor) while Biceps relaxes.
  • Origin = immovable bone attachment; Insertion = movable bone attachment.

The Three Muscle Types Comparison

Voluntary

Skeletal Muscle

  • Striations: Yes (Clear A & I bands)
  • Nuclei: Multiple, Peripheral
  • Control: Voluntary (Somatic)
  • Location: Attached to skeleton
Involuntary

Smooth Muscle

  • Striations: No (Unstriated)
  • Nuclei: Single, Centrally located
  • Control: Involuntary (Autonomic)
  • Location: Visceral walls (GI, vessels)
Involuntary / Heart

Cardiac Muscle

  • Striations: Yes + Intercalated discs
  • Nuclei: 1–2, Central
  • Control: Involuntary / Autorhythmic
  • Location: Heart myocardium exclusively

Immune System (Innate Defense, Adaptive Immunity & Antibodies)

Lines of host defense, phagocytosis, B and T lymphocytes, antibodies, and antigen specificity.

The Three Lines of Host Defense

1st Line: External Innate

Physical & Chemical Barriers

  • Skin: Stratified keratinized dry barrier and acidic sebum (pH 3–5).
  • Mucous Membranes: Traps microbes in respiratory and digestive tracts.
  • Cilia: Mucociliary escalator in trachea sweeps trapped pathogens out.
  • Lysozyme: Enzyme in tears and saliva that hydrolyzes bacterial cell walls.
  • Gastric Acid: HCl in stomach kills swallowed pathogens.
2nd Line: Internal Innate

Cellular & Chemical Internal

  • Phagocytes: Neutrophils & Macrophages engulf pathogens via endocytosis.
  • Natural Killer (NK) Cells: Induce apoptosis in virus-infected and tumor cells.
  • Inflammatory Response: Histamine release causes vasodilation and increased capillary permeability.
  • Complement System: Plasma proteins that form Membrane Attack Complexes (MAC) to lyse microbes.
3rd Line: Adaptive (Specific)

Lymphocytes & Antibodies

  • Humoral Immunity (B-Cells): Differentiate into Plasma cells that secrete specific Antibodies, and long-lived Memory B cells.
  • Cell-Mediated (T-Cells): Helper T-cells (CD4+) stimulate B/T cells; Cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+) directly lyse infected host cells.
  • Immunological Memory: Secondary exposure produces a drastically faster, stronger response.
Antigen

Antigens (Immunogens)

An Antigen is any foreign macromolecule (surface glycoprotein, capsid protein, toxin) capable of specifically binding to an antibody receptor and triggering an adaptive immune response.

Antibody

Antibodies (Immunoglobulins)

Antibodies are Y-shaped glycoproteins consisting of 4 polypeptide chains (2 Heavy and 2 Light chains joined by disulfide bridges), containing variable antigen-binding sites (Fab fragment) providing lock-and-key specificity.

Side-by-side high-yield comparative tables to master frequently tested distinctions.

1. Arteries vs. Veins vs. Capillaries

Feature Arteries Veins Capillaries
Direction of Flow Away from Heart (➔ Tissues) Towards Heart (➔ Atria) Connects Arterioles to Venules
Blood Oxygenation Oxygenated (Except Pulmonary Artery) Deoxygenated (Except Pulmonary Veins) Gas & nutrient exchange zone
Vessel Wall Thickness Thick, muscular & elastic Thin, less muscular Microscopic single-cell endothelium
Internal Lumen Narrow lumen Wide lumen Microscopic (fits 1 RBC)
Internal Valves Absent (High pressure prevents backflow) Present (Prevents backflow in low pressure) Absent
Internal Blood Pressure High & Pulsatile (120/80 mmHg) Low (~5–10 mmHg) Intermediate (~35 ➔ 15 mmHg)

2. RBCs vs. WBCs vs. Platelets

Parameter RBCs (Erythrocytes) WBCs (Leucocytes) Platelets (Thrombocytes)
Primary Function O₂ & CO₂ Gas Transport Immune defense & phagocytosis Blood clotting & hemostasis
Nucleus Absent at maturity Present (often lobed) Absent (cytoplasmic fragments)
Normal Count 4.5 – 5.5 Million / mm³ 4,000 – 11,000 / mm³ 150,000 – 400,000 / mm³
Average Lifespan ~120 Days Hours to years (Memory cells) 7 – 10 Days

3. Skeletal vs. Smooth vs. Cardiac Muscle

Characteristic Skeletal Muscle Smooth Muscle Cardiac Muscle
Microscopic Appearance Striated, cylindrical fibers Unstriated, spindle-shaped Striated, branched fibers
Nuclei per Cell Multinucleated, Peripheral Uninucleated, Central 1–2 Nuclei, Central
Intercalated Discs Absent Absent Present (⭐ MDCAT classic)
Contraction Control Voluntary (Somatic control) Involuntary (Autonomic control) Involuntary (Autorhythmic pacemaker)
Speed of Contraction Rapid & fatigues easily Slow, rhythmic & sustained Rhythmic & fatigue-resistant

4. Insulin vs. Glucagon (Pancreatic Antagonism)

Feature Insulin Glucagon
Secretory Cell Source Beta (β) cells of Islets of Langerhans Alpha (α) cells of Islets of Langerhans
Trigger for Release High blood glucose levels (Hyperglycemia) Low blood glucose levels (Hypoglycemia)
Overall Net Effect Lowers blood glucose (Hypoglycemic) Raises blood glucose (Hyperglycemic)
Key Biochemical Actions Glycogenesis, cellular uptake, lipogenesis Glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis

5. Central Nervous System (CNS) vs. Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

Feature Central Nervous System (CNS) Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)
Structural Components Brain and Spinal Cord 12 Cranial nerves + 31 Spinal nerves
Bony Protection Protected by Cranium & Vertebral column No enclosing bone protection
Myelinating Glial Cell Oligodendrocytes Schwann Cells
Main Role Integration, processing, command center Conducts sensory & motor inputs to/from CNS
