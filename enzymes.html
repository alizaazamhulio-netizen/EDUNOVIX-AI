SECTION 01

What are Enzymes?

The biological engines accelerating cellular life at the speed of chemistry.

🧪

Definition & Biochemical Nature

Proteinaceous Catalysts

Enzymes are biological catalysts that tremendously increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being permanently altered or consumed in the process.

"Most enzymes are globular proteins, although specialized catalytic RNA molecules called ribozymes can also act as biological catalysts (e.g., peptidyl transferase in 23S rRNA)."

⭐
MDCAT Core Point: Enzymes accelerate reaction velocity ($V$) by lowering activation energy ($E_a$). They do NOT alter the equilibrium constant ($K_{eq}$) or standard free energy change ($\Delta G$).
📖

Fundamental Terminology

Essential MDCAT Definitions
Enzyme
Biological catalyst that speeds up chemical reactions without being consumed.
Substrate
The specific reactant substance on which an enzyme acts and transforms.
Active Site
Three-dimensional pocket/cleft where substrate binds via complementary bonds.
ES Complex
Unstable, high-energy temporary intermediate formed during transition state binding.
Product
Substance(s) released after the catalytic transformation is completed.
CORE PROPERTIES

7 Inviolable Characteristics of Enzymes

01
🎯

Highly Specific

Each enzyme acts on a particular substrate or specific chemical bond (e.g., Urease acts only on urea; Sucrase splits only sucrose).

02
⚡

Biological Catalysts

Accelerate biochemical reaction rates by factors of $10^6$ to $10^{12}$ times compared to uncatalyzed reactions.

03
🔄

Not Consumed

They enter the reaction, facilitate bond reconfiguration, and emerge chemically unchanged at the end.

04
💧

Work in Minute Quantities

Due to very high turnover numbers ($k_{cat}$), even tiny quantities can process millions of substrate molecules per second.

05
🌡️

Temperature Sensitive

Have a distinct optimum temperature (human ~37°C). Excessive heat disrupts tertiary structure, causing denaturation.

06
🧪

pH Sensitive

Optimum pH varies per enzyme (Pepsin: pH 1.5–2.0 vs Trypsin: pH 8.0). Deviation changes active site charge ionization.

07
🔁

Completely Reusable

Once products detach from the active site, the enzyme immediately accepts new substrate molecules in a continuous catalytic cycle.

SECTION 02

Structure of an Enzyme & Catalytic Cycle

The spatial chemistry of active sites, apoenzymes, cofactors, and intermediates.

Anatomy of the Active Site

The active site is a specialized three-dimensional cleft consisting of few specific amino acids brought together by precise protein folding. It comprises two sub-regions:

Binding Site

Recognizes, orients, and holds the substrate via hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and hydrophobic interactions.

Catalytic Site

Transforms the bound substrate into products by cleaving or synthesizing chemical bonds.

Cofactor Hierarchy (MDCAT High Yield)

  • Holoenzyme = Apoenzyme (Inactive Protein Part) + Non-protein Part (Cofactor)
  • Prosthetic Group: Covalently / tightly bound non-protein group (e.g., Heme in Cytochromes).
  • Coenzyme: Loosely bound organic molecule, often vitamin derivatives (e.g., $NAD^+$, $FAD$, $CoA$).
  • Activator: Inorganic metallic ions (e.g., $Mg^{2+}$, $Fe^{2+}$, $Zn^{2+}$, $Cu^{2+}$).

Flexible, hand-in-glove dynamic conformation

Substrate (Inducer)

  • Active site is flexible and dynamic.
  • Binding of substrate induces slight conformational change in enzyme amino acids.
  • Strains substrate chemical bonds, facilitating transition state and lowering $E_a$.
  • More widely accepted scientific model.
SECTION 04

Activation Energy ($E_a$) & Reaction Profiles

How enzymes overcome thermodynamic barriers without altering standard free energy ($\Delta G$).

Thermodynamic Principles

Activation Energy ($E_a$) is the minimum kinetic energy required for reactant molecules to collide with sufficient force, reach the high-energy transition state, and form products.

🔥
Without Enzyme: Huge $E_a$ barrier requires high heat or lengthy time for spontaneous transition.
⚡
With Enzyme: Drastically lowered $E_a$ enables rapid catalysis at physiological temperature (~37°C).
⚖️
Thermodynamic Constant: Overall Gibbs Free Energy Change ($\Delta G$) and equilibrium constant ($K_{eq}$) remain strictly identical.
Enzyme → ↓ Activation Energy ($E_a$) → Faster Reaction Rate ($V$)

SECTION 05

Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Interactive kinetic laboratory testing temperature, pH, enzyme concentration, and substrate saturation.

🌡️

1. Effect of Temperature

Kinetic vs Denaturation

As temperature rises from 0°C to optimum (~37°C), molecular collisions increase ($Q_{10} \approx 2$). Above optimum, violent thermal vibrations break tertiary bonds, causing irreversible denaturation.

Optimum Activity
0°C (Inactive) 37°C (Optimum) 80°C (Denatured)
Functional
⚠️ MDCAT Trap: Low temperature inactivates enzymes reversibly; excessive high temperature denatures enzymes irreversibly.
🧪

2. Effect of pH

Ionization of Active Site

Every enzyme has an Optimum pH. Altering pH alters the ionization state of acidic and basic amino acid side-chains in the active site and substrate.

Preset:
Max Activity
pH 0 (Acidic) pH 7 (Neutral) pH 14 (Alkaline)
+ -
Optimal Amino Acid Ionization
⭐ MDCAT Point: Optimum pH is NOT uniform across enzymes (Pepsin = 1.5–2.0 vs Trypsin = 7.8–8.5).
📈

3. Enzyme Concentration

Direct Proportionality

With unlimited/excess substrate available, reaction rate is directly proportional to enzyme concentration ($Rate \propto [E]$). More active sites available per second.

Linear Rate
📌 Limiting Factor Rule: If substrate becomes limited, increasing enzyme concentration eventually plateaus the rate.
🍬

4. Substrate Concentration

Michaelis-Menten & $V_{max}$ Saturation

At constant $[E]$: Initially $\uparrow [S]$ increases rate. At high $[S]$, all active sites are 100% occupied (saturated), reaching maximum velocity ($V_{max}$).

Linear Phase
Low [S] Half-Max ($K_m$) Saturation ($V_{max}$)
Active Site Saturation: 65%
⭐ MDCAT Point: Once $V_{max}$ is reached, adding more substrate has zero effect on reaction velocity unless more enzyme is added.

⚔️

Competitive Inhibition

Substrate 🔑 vs Inhibitor 🔑
  • Structural Homology: Inhibitor resembles the substrate shape closely.
  • Target: Binds strictly to the active site.
  • Reversibility: Increasing substrate concentration successfully outcompetes the inhibitor!
  • Classic Example: Malonate inhibits Succinate Dehydrogenase (competes with succinate); Sulfonamides compete with PABA for bacterial folate synthesis.
🧬

Non-Competitive Inhibition

Allosteric Site Deformation
  • No Structural Resemblance: Does not resemble the substrate.
  • Target: Binds to an allosteric site (other than active site).
  • Mechanism: Distorts tertiary protein conformation, inactivating the active site.
  • Reversibility: Adding excess substrate CANNOT overcome inhibition.
  • Classic Example: Cyanide poisons Cytochrome Oxidase; heavy metal ions ($Ag^+, Hg^{2+}, Pb^{2+}$).
EXAM VAULT

🔥 MDCAT MUST-KNOW (High-Yield Facts)

The golden points frequently repeated in medical entrance examinations.

⭐

1. Biological Catalysts

Enzymes speed up chemical reactions without being permanently consumed.

⭐

2. Proteinaceous Nature

Most enzymes are globular proteins; some catalytic RNA are ribozymes.

⭐

3. Active Site

Composed of binding and catalytic sites that specifically bind the substrate.

⭐

4. Lowering Activation Energy

Enzymes accelerate reaction velocity exclusively by reducing $E_a$.

⭐

5. Reusability

Unchanged at reaction completion and recycled immediately for new substrates.

⭐

6. Lock-and-Key Model

Proposed by Emil Fischer; emphasizes fixed, rigid stereospecific complementary shape.

⭐

7. Induced-Fit Model

Proposed by Koshland; enzyme adjusts active site shape dynamically upon binding.

⭐

8. Thermal Denaturation

Excess heat permanently destroys tertiary folding and active site geometry.

⭐

9. Enzyme-Specific Optimum pH

Optimum pH differs per enzyme (e.g. Pepsin ~2.0 vs Trypsin ~8.0).

⭐

10. Substrate Proportionality

Increasing $[S]$ initially increases rate proportionally until active sites saturate.

⭐

11. Saturation ($V_{max}$)

At 100% active site saturation, further addition of substrate yields no rate increase.

⭐

12. Competitive Inhibition

Competes for the active site; surmountable by increasing substrate concentration.

RAPID RECAP

⚡ Quick Revision Cards

Click any card to expand high-yield exam-focused answers.

An enzyme is a biological protein catalyst that increases the rate of chemical reactions in living cells without being consumed or permanently altered in the process.

The active site is a specialized three-dimensional cleft or pocket on the enzyme surface composed of specific binding and catalytic amino acid residues where the substrate attaches and undergoes reaction.

Activation energy ($E_a$) is the minimum kinetic energy required to initiate a chemical reaction by bringing reactants to the transition state.

Denaturation is the irreversible alteration and breakdown of the globular, tertiary 3D structure of an enzyme (due to extreme heat or extreme pH), which destroys active site shape and eliminates catalytic activity.

The optimum pH is the specific hydrogen ion concentration at which a given enzyme exhibits its maximum catalytic activity (e.g., pH 2.0 for Pepsin, pH 8.0 for Trypsin).

A type of inhibition where an inhibitor molecule has structural similarity to the natural substrate and competes directly for binding at the active site. It can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration.

A type of inhibition where an inhibitor binds to an allosteric site (a site distinct from the active site), causing a conformational change in the enzyme that inactivates catalysis. It cannot be reversed by adding more substrate.
