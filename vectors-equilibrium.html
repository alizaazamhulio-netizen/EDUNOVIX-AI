Scalars and Vectors

Scalar Quantity

A scalar quantity is a physical quantity that has magnitude only — it has no direction associated with it. Scalars are fully described by a single number and a unit.

MassTimeTemperature DistanceSpeedEnergy

Vector Quantity

A vector quantity is a physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction. A direction must always be specified for a vector to be fully described.

DisplacementVelocity AccelerationForce Momentum

02

Representation of Vectors

How a Vector is Drawn

A vector is represented graphically by a directed arrow. The length of the arrow is proportional to the magnitude of the vector, and the arrowhead points in the direction of the vector.

  • Initial point (tail): where the vector starts.
  • Terminal point (head): where the arrow points.
  • Magnitude: the length of the arrow (drawn to scale).
  • Direction: indicated by the arrowhead.

In notation, a vector is written with an arrow above the letter, such as A⃗ or →A, or in bold as A.

Unit Vector Notation

A vector in 3D can be written using unit vectors î, ĵ, k̂ along the x, y and z axes:

A⃗ = Axî + Ayĵ + Azk̂
03

Types of Vectors

Unit Vector

A vector whose magnitude is exactly 1. It indicates direction only. Common unit vectors are î (x-axis), ĵ (y-axis) and k̂ (z-axis).

Equal Vectors

Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction, regardless of where they start.

Negative / Opposite Vectors

Same magnitude but opposite direction. If A⃗ is a vector, then −A⃗ is its negative.

Zero Vector

A vector whose magnitude is zero. It has no defined direction. Written as 0⃗.

Parallel Vectors

Vectors acting along parallel lines in the same direction.

Anti-parallel Vectors

Parallel vectors acting in opposite directions.

04

Addition of Vectors

Head-to-Tail Method

Place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first vector. The resultant goes from the tail of the first to the head of the last. This is also called the triangle method.

Parallelogram Method

If two vectors act from the same point, they are represented by the adjacent sides of a parallelogram. The diagonal drawn from the common point gives the resultant.

05

Resultant of Two Vectors

When two vectors A and B make an angle θ with each other, the magnitude of the resultant R is given by:

R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cosθ)

Where: A = magnitude of first vector, B = magnitude of second vector, θ = angle between the two vectors, R = magnitude of the resultant.

Special Cases

Same Direction (θ = 0°)
R = A + B

The vectors add directly since cos 0° = 1.

Opposite Direction (θ = 180°)
R = |A − B|

The vectors subtract since cos 180° = −1.

Perpendicular (θ = 90°)
R = √(A² + B²)

cos 90° = 0, so the cross term vanishes.

Worked Example

Question: Two vectors A = 3 N and B = 4 N act at an angle of 60° to each other. Find the magnitude of the resultant.

Step 1: R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cosθ)

Step 2: R = √(3² + 4² + 2 × 3 × 4 × cos 60°)

Step 3: cos 60° = 0.5, so R = √(9 + 16 + 24 × 0.5) = √(9 + 16 + 12)

Step 4: R = √37 ≈ 6.08 N

06

Resolution of Vectors

Components of a Vector

A single vector can be split into two perpendicular components — one along the x-axis and one along the y-axis. If vector A makes an angle θ with the x-axis:

Ax = A cosθ
Ay = A sinθ

The original vector can be recovered from its components:

A = √(Ax² + Ay²)

This is extremely important for MDCAT numerical questions.

Component Diagram

Solved Example

Question: A force of 10 N acts at an angle of 30° to the horizontal. Find its horizontal and vertical components.

Step 1: Ax = A cosθ = 10 × cos 30° = 10 × 0.866 = 8.66 N

Step 2: Ay = A sinθ = 10 × sin 30° = 10 × 0.5 = 5.0 N

07

Equilibrium

An object is in equilibrium when the net force acting on it is zero.

ΣF = 0

For complete equilibrium in two dimensions, the forces must balance along each axis:

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0

Static Equilibrium

The object remains at rest. All forces and torques balance, and there is no motion at all.

Dynamic Equilibrium

The object moves with constant velocity — no acceleration. Forces still balance, but the object is in motion.

Equilibrium Diagram — Three Forces Balancing

In both static and dynamic equilibrium, acceleration a = 0 and the net force is zero.

08

Torque / Moment of Force

Torque is the turning effect of a force about a pivot point. It depends on the force, the distance from the pivot, and the angle at which the force is applied.

τ = F r sinθ

Where: τ = torque, F = force, r = distance from pivot to point of force application, θ = angle between F and r.

τ = F × r (when θ = 90°)

SI Unit: Newton-meter (N·m)

Key Terms

  • Pivot: the fixed point about which rotation occurs.
  • Force (F): the applied push or pull.
  • Lever arm (r): the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.
  • Angle (θ): the angle between the force vector and the lever arm.

Rotating Lever Diagram

Maximum torque occurs when θ = 90° (force perpendicular to the lever arm), because sin 90° = 1.

09

Principle of Moments

For a body in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about any pivot.

Clockwise moments = Anticlockwise moments
Στ = 0

This principle is commonly used in beam, balance and lever problems.

Balanced Beam Example

A uniform beam is balanced on a pivot. A 20 N weight hangs 2 m to the left of the pivot. What weight must hang 1 m to the right to balance it?

Step 1: Anticlockwise moment = 20 N × 2 m = 40 N·m

Step 2: Clockwise moment = W × 1 m

Step 3: W × 1 = 40 → W = 40 N

10

Couple

A couple consists of two equal and opposite parallel forces acting at different points on a body. The net force is zero, but the couple produces a pure rotational effect (torque) without any translation.

τ = F × d

Where: F = magnitude of each force, d = perpendicular distance between the two forces.

11

Centre of Gravity

The centre of gravity (C.G.) is the point through which the entire weight of an object appears to act. It is the average position of all the weight distributed throughout the body.

  • For a symmetrical, uniform object, the centre of gravity is at its geometric centre.
  • For a uniform rod, the C.G. is at its midpoint.
  • For a uniform circular disc, the C.G. is at its centre.
  • For a triangle, the C.G. is at the intersection of the medians (1/3 from the base).
  • An object is stable when its centre of gravity lies directly above its base of support.
12

Conditions of Equilibrium

Translational Equilibrium

The net force on the body is zero, so there is no linear acceleration.

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0

Rotational Equilibrium

The net torque on the body is zero, so there is no angular acceleration.

Στ = 0

Complete equilibrium requires both conditions to be satisfied simultaneously:

Net force = 0
Net torque = 0

MDCAT Quick Tips

Always identify the magnitude and direction of every vector before solving.

Draw a diagram before solving any vector problem — it prevents sign errors.

Remember sin/cos when resolving vectors: cos for x, sin for y.

For perpendicular vectors, use the Pythagorean theorem: R = √(A² + B²).

Equilibrium means net force is zero — both ΣFx and ΣFy must vanish.

For rotational equilibrium, net torque is zero — clockwise = anticlockwise.

Maximum torque occurs when force is perpendicular to the lever arm (θ = 90°).

Always check units — torque is in N·m, not joules, even though the dimensions match.

Common Mistakes

1

Confusing scalar with vector. Speed is a scalar; velocity is a vector. Always check if direction matters.

2

Forgetting direction. Two vectors with the same magnitude but different directions are not equal.

3

Using sin instead of cos for the wrong component. Remember: Ax = A cosθ, Ay = A sinθ.

4

Ignoring signs of clockwise and anticlockwise torque. Assign one direction positive and the other negative consistently.

5

Forgetting that equilibrium requires zero resultant force AND zero net torque — not just one of them.

6

Mixing up distance and perpendicular distance in torque. Use the perpendicular distance (lever arm), not the full length.

60-Second Revision

  • Scalar: magnitude only (mass, time, speed).
  • Vector: magnitude + direction (force, velocity).
  • Unit vector: magnitude = 1 (î, ĵ, k̂).
  • Resultant: R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cosθ).
  • Same direction: R = A + B.
  • Opposite direction: R = |A − B|.
  • Perpendicular: R = √(A² + B²).
  • Components: Ax = A cosθ, Ay = A sinθ.
  • Magnitude from components: A = √(Ax² + Ay²).
  • Equilibrium: ΣF = 0 (net force zero).
  • Static: at rest. Dynamic: constant velocity.
  • Torque: τ = F r sinθ. Unit: N·m.
  • Max torque: θ = 90°, τ = F r.
  • Principle of moments: clockwise = anticlockwise.
  • Couple: two equal, opposite, parallel forces; τ = F d.
  • Centre of gravity: point where weight acts; at geometric centre for uniform objects.
  • Complete equilibrium: ΣF = 0 AND Στ = 0.
