01

Matter & Classification

Matter is defined as anything that possesses mass and occupies physical space (volume). In chemistry, all matter in the universe is broadly categorized into pure substances (elements and compounds) and mixtures.

Element Only one type of atom present. (e.g. Fe, Na, He)
Compound Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed ratio. (e.g. H₂O, CO₂)
Mixture Physical blend of distinct substances; variable composition. (e.g. Air, Sea water)
⭐
MDCAT Fundamental Rule An element cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by chemical means because all atoms share the same atomic number (nuclear charge $Z$).
02

Pure Substances vs. Mixtures

A Pure Substance has an invariable, fixed composition and distinct physical properties (sharp melting point, fixed boiling point). Mixtures have variable compositions with constituents retaining their original identities.

Type A
Homogeneous Mixture (Solution) Has a uniform composition and a single observable phase throughout. Individual particles are dispersed at the molecular/ionic level and do not settle. Examples: Pure air, saline water, brass alloy, vinegar.
Type B
Heterogeneous Mixture Has a non-uniform composition containing two or more distinct phases with visible boundary interfaces. Examples: Sand in water, oil-water emulsion, smoke, garden soil, blood.
03

Physical and Chemical Changes

Understanding the distinction between state transformations and chemical bond reorganizations is critical for MDCAT reasoning questions:

Physical Change

No new chemical substance is formed. Intermolecular distances or state of matter alter, but molecular formula remains identical.

H₂O(s) ➔ H₂O(l)
Melting, boiling, dissolving sugar in water.
Chemical Change

One or more new chemical substances are formed. Chemical bonds break and reform, resulting in altered chemical properties.

2H₂ + O₂ ➔ 2H₂O
Rusting of iron, combustion of methane, cellular respiration.
Diagnostic Test

Check whether chemical bonds within molecules have been broken or if the mass ratio of constituent elements remains intact.

04

Laws of Chemical Combination

Law 1
Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1789) Mass is neither created nor destroyed during an ordinary chemical reaction.
Total Mass of Reactants = Total Mass of Products
Law 2
Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions (Proust, 1799) A pure chemical compound always contains the exact same elements combined in the exact same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of source or method of preparation. Example: Pure water ($H_2O$) always contains Hydrogen and Oxygen in a 1:8 mass ratio ($2.016\text{ g H} : 16.00\text{ g O}$).
Law 3
Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton, 1803) When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the different masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other bear a simple whole-number ratio. Example: In Carbon Monoxide ($CO$) and Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$), for a fixed mass of 12g Carbon, Oxygen masses combine as $16\text{ g} : 32\text{ g} = 1:2$.
05

Relative Atomic Mass (Ar)

Relative Atomic Mass ($A_r$) is the weighted average mass of naturally occurring atoms of an element relative to $\frac{1}{12}\text{th}$ the mass of one atom of Carbon-12 ($^{12}\text{C}$).

Definition of Unified Atomic Mass Unit
1 \text{ amu} = \frac{1}{12} \times \text{Mass of 1 atom of }^{12}\text{C} \approx 1.66054 \times 10^{-24} \text{ g}
💡
Why are atomic masses in decimals? Because elements exist as isotopes with distinct natural abundances. For example, Chlorine has $^{35}\text{Cl}$ (75.77%) and $^{37}\text{Cl}$ (24.23%), giving an average $A_r \approx 35.45$.
06

Relative Molecular Mass (Mr)

Relative Molecular Mass ($M_r$) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule of a covalent substance.

Worked Calculation Examples
\text{For } \text{H}_2\text{O}: 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.016 \approx 18
\text{For } \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6: 6(12.01) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.16 \approx 180
07

Formula Mass (Ionic Compounds)

Ionic compounds (such as $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{CaCO}_3$, $\text{CuSO}_4$) do not exist as discrete independent molecules in solid state. They form infinite three-dimensional crystal lattices where each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions.

⭐
Critical MDCAT Distinction For ionic compounds, we strictly use Formula Mass (mass of one formula unit), NOT molecular mass!
$\text{NaCl Formula Mass} = 22.99\text{ (Na)} + 35.45\text{ (Cl)} = 58.44 \approx 58.5\text{ amu}$.
08

Mole Concept & Avogadro's Constant

A Mole is the SI unit for the amount of substance containing exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary particles. This fundamental constant is called the Avogadro Constant ($N_A$).

The Avogadro Constant
N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1}
Entities
Applicable Particle Species: • Atoms: 1 mol C = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ C atoms (12 g)
• Molecules: 1 mol H₂O = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ H₂O molecules (18 g)
• Formula Units: 1 mol NaCl = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ NaCl units (58.5 g)
• Ions: 1 mol $\text{Na}^+$ = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ $\text{Na}^+$ ions (23 g)
09

Molar Mass ($M$)

Molar Mass ($M$) is the mass in grams of one mole of a chemical substance. Its SI unit is $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$.

Numerically, the molar mass in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ is identical to the substance's relative atomic mass, molecular mass, or formula mass in $\text{amu}$.

10

Mole Calculations & Conversion Triangle

Every numerical problem in MDCAT chemistry stems from this fundamental conversion triangle:

Fundamental Conversion Formulas
n = \frac{m}{M} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad N = n \times N_A \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad n = \frac{N}{N_A}
Where $n$ = moles, $m$ = mass in grams, $M$ = molar mass ($\text{g/mol}$), $N$ = particle count, $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$.
📝
Worked Numerical: How many molecules are in 36 g of water ($H_2O$)?
1. $n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{36\text{ g}}{18\text{ g/mol}} = 2.0\text{ mol}$.
2. $N = n \times N_A = 2.0 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.2044 \times 10^{24}\text{ molecules}$.

11

Percentage Composition

The Percentage Composition of an element is the percentage by mass of that element in the compound.

Formula for Percentage Composition
\% \text{ Element} = \frac{\text{Mass of element in 1 mole of compound}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times 100
⭐
Classic MDCAT Exam Example (Water, H₂O, M = 18.016 g/mol):
• $\% \text{H} = \frac{2 \times 1.008}{18.016} \times 100 = \mathbf{11.19\%} \approx 11.1\%$
• $\% \text{O} = \frac{16.000}{18.016} \times 100 = \mathbf{88.81\%} \approx 88.9\%$

12

Empirical Formula

The Empirical Formula expresses the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

Step 1
Assume 100 g Sample Convert given percentages directly into grams ($40\% \➔ 40\text{ g}$).
Step 2
Calculate Moles Divide grams by atomic mass ($n = \frac{m}{A_r}$).
Step 3
Divide by Smallest Divide each mole quantity by the smallest mole value obtained.
Step 4
Convert to Whole Numbers If non-integer decimals appear (e.g. $.5 \times 2$, $.33 \times 3$), multiply by suitable integer.
13

Molecular Formula

The Molecular Formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in a single molecule.

Relationship Formula
\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{Empirical Formula})_n \quad \text{where} \quad n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}
💡
Example: Glucose has empirical formula $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ ($\text{EF mass} = 12 + 2 + 16 = 30$).
With molar mass = $180\text{ g/mol}$, $n = \frac{180}{30} = 6$.
$\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{CH}_2\text{O})_6 = \mathbf{C_6H_{12}O_6}$.

14

Stoichiometry & Balanced Equations

Stoichiometry deals with the quantitative relationships between the amounts of reactants consumed and products formed in a chemical reaction.

Mole Ratio from Balanced Equation
2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
2 moles H₂ react with 1 mole O₂ to yield 2 moles H₂O (Mole ratio: 2 : 1 ➔ 2).
⭐
MDCAT Requirement: Never perform stoichiometry without balancing the chemical equation first!
15

Limiting and Excess Reactants

The Limiting Reactant is the reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction. It restricts and determines the maximum theoretical yield of products.

🥪
The Sandwich Analogy: If 1 sandwich requires 2 slices of bread + 1 slice of cheese, and you have 10 slices of bread and only 3 slices of cheese: Cheese is the limiting reactant (you can only make 3 sandwiches; 4 slices of bread remain in excess).
5-Step Strategy
How to Find the Limiting Reactant in MDCAT: 1. Balance the chemical equation.
2. Convert given masses/volumes of all reactants into moles ($n = m/M$).
3. Divide each mole value by its stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation.
4. The reactant with the smallest quotient is the Limiting Reactant.
5. Use the moles of the limiting reactant to compute theoretical product yield!

16

Theoretical, Actual & Percentage Yield

In chemical manufacturing and lab synthesis, reactions rarely deliver 100% efficiency.

Theoretical
Theoretical Yield The maximum amount of product calculated from stoichiometric equations assuming 100% complete conversion.
Actual
Actual Yield The real amount of product physically collected and weighed experimentally in the laboratory ($\text{Actual} \le \text{Theoretical}$).
Percentage Yield Formula
\% \text{ Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}} \times 100
