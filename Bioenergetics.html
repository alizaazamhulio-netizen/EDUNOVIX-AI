Section 01

1. What is Bioenergetics?

Bioenergetics is the quantitative study of how living organisms acquire, transform, store, and utilize energy to sustain life and prevent the increase of entropy.

Core Principle of Biological Energy:

According to the First Law of Thermodynamics, energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another (e.g., radiant light energy into chemical bond energy during photosynthesis, then chemical energy into mechanical/electrical/thermal energy in animal cells).

Why Cells Need Energy

Biological systems are in a continuous non-equilibrium steady state. Living cells require constant input of free energy for:

  • Active Transport: Pumping ions and metabolites against electrochemical concentration gradients (e.g., Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, Ca²⁺ pump in sarcoplasmic reticulum).
  • Mechanical Work & Muscle Contraction: Cross-bridge cycling of actin and myosin filaments, beating of cilia and flagella, and chromosome movement during mitosis.
  • Biosynthesis (Chemical Work): Anabolic synthesis of polymers from monomers (DNA replication, RNA transcription, protein translation, polysaccharide synthesis).
  • Cell Division & Morphogenesis: Cytokinesis, membrane reorganization, and organelle biogenesis.
  • Growth, Tissue Repair & Turnover: Continuous synthesis to replace denatured cellular components.
  • Maintaining Cellular Organization: Counteracting thermodynamic entropy to maintain structural and biochemical integrity.
MDCAT Key Takeaway: The ultimate immediate energy-transfer molecule in all living cells is ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate).
Section 02 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

2. ATP — The Energy Currency of the Cell

ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) is a ribonucleotide derivative known universally as the "universal energy currency" of cellular metabolism.

1. Nitrogenous Base
Adenine (a double-ring purine base attached to C-1' of ribose).
2. Pentose Sugar
Ribose (a 5-carbon aldopentose sugar with 2'-OH and 3'-OH groups).
3. Three Phosphates
Alpha (α), Beta (β), Gamma (γ) phosphates linked via high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds.

ATP Hydrolysis Equation

ATP + H₂O ➔ ADP + Pi + Energy (ΔG°' ≈ -30.5 kJ/mol / -7.3 kcal/mol)

Where ADP = Adenosine Diphosphate, and Pi = Inorganic Phosphate (HPO₄²⁻/H₂PO₄⁻). Further hydrolysis of ADP yields AMP (Adenosine Monophosphate) and another inorganic phosphate.

Three Major Categories of ATP-Driven Cellular Work

🦾
1. Mechanical Work

Hydrolysis of ATP changes the conformation of motor proteins (myosin heads along actin filaments, kinesin/dynein along microtubules).

🚚
2. Transport Work

Phosphorylation of transport proteins directly drives the translocation of solutes across membranes (e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump against gradient).

🧪
3. Chemical Work

Coupling endergonic anabolic syntheses (glutamine synthesis, peptide bond creation) to the highly favorable exergonic hydrolysis of ATP.

🔥 MDCAT Trap Alert!

ATP is an immediate energy-transfer intermediate, NOT a long-term energy-storage molecule. A typical human cell possesses only enough ATP to sustain life for a few seconds. Long-term energy is stored as glycogen, starch, or triacylglycerols (fats).

Section 03 • Core Concept ⭐⭐

3. Metabolism: Anabolism vs. Catabolism

Metabolism is the sum total of all biochemical reactions occurring within an organism or individual cell to maintain life, sustain growth, and reproduce.

Anabolism (Building)

Anabolic Pathways

Constructs complex macromolecules from simpler precursors.

Small Molecules + Energy ➔ Large Molecules
  • Thermodynamics: Endergonic (Requires Energy, +ΔG).
  • Examples: Protein synthesis from amino acids, glycogenesis (glucose ➔ glycogen), photosynthesis (CO₂ + H₂O ➔ glucose).
  • Reductive: Often uses NADPH / ATP as reducing and energy currency.
Catabolism (Breaking)

Catabolic Pathways

Degrades complex macromolecules into simpler end products.

Large Molecules ➔ Small Molecules + Energy
  • Thermodynamics: Exergonic (Releases Energy, -ΔG).
  • Examples: Cellular respiration (Glucose ➔ CO₂ + H₂O + ATP), glycolysis, lipolysis, beta-oxidation of fatty acids.
  • Oxidative: Transfers electrons to NAD⁺ / FAD to generate NADH / FADH₂.
💡 MDCAT Memory Trick:

ANABOLISM = Assemble (Constructs & Consumes Energy) | CATABOLISM = Cut (Cuts down & Releases Energy)

Section 04

4. Energy Coupling & Gibbs Free Energy

Cells drive non-spontaneous, thermodynamically unfavorable (endergonic) reactions by mechanically and chemically pairing them with spontaneous, thermodynamically favorable (exergonic) reactions.

Exergonic Catabolism Nutrient Breakdown ΔG°' < 0
ATP Universal Intermediate
Endergonic Anabolism Cellular Work & Synthesis ΔG°' > 0
Reaction 1 (Glutamic acid + NH₃ ➔ Glutamine): +14.2 kJ/mol (Endergonic)
Reaction 2 (ATP + H₂O ➔ ADP + Pi): -30.5 kJ/mol (Exergonic)
Net Coupled Reaction (Overall ΔG°'): -16.3 kJ/mol (Favorable & Spontaneous!)

As long as the total algebraic sum of ΔG for the coupled reactions is negative, the combined overall pathway is exergonic and proceeds spontaneously.

Section 05 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

5. Enzymes and Activation Energy

Enzymes are biological catalysts (predominantly globular proteins; some catalytic RNAs are ribozymes) that drastically accelerate biochemical reactions without being consumed in the process.

Activation Energy (Ea)

Activation Energy (Ea) is the minimum kinetic energy threshold that colliding reactant molecules must overcome to distort their chemical bonds and reach the unstable transition state.

Interactive Simulation

Interactive Activation Energy (Ea) Profile

Enzymes lower the activation energy barrier, allowing far more substrate molecules to attain the transition state per second. Notice that the initial free energy of reactants and final free energy of products (ΔG) are completely identical in both curves!

What Enzymes DO and DO NOT Do (MDCAT Checklist)

✔ What Enzymes DO:
  • Lower the Activation Energy (Ea) of the reaction.
  • Accelerate the rate of forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Form transient Enzyme-Substrate (ES) and Enzyme-Product (EP) complexes.
  • Provide exquisite substrate specificity via active site architecture.
✖ What Enzymes DO NOT Do:
  • Do NOT change overall standard free energy (ΔG).
  • Do NOT shift the final chemical equilibrium constant (Keq).
  • Do NOT get permanently consumed or chemically altered.
  • Do NOT make an energetically non-spontaneous reaction spontaneous.
Section 06

6. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Enzyme catalytic velocity is regulated by environmental parameters that influence protein conformation and active site charge distribution.

Factor 1

Temperature

As temperature rises, kinetic energy increases, increasing collisions between enzyme active sites and substrate molecules.

Optimum: ~37°C for human enzymes (up to 75°C for thermophiles like Thermus aquaticus).

Above optimum: Extreme thermal agitation disrupts hydrogen and hydrophobic bonds, causing irreversible denaturation and loss of tertiary structure.

Factor 2

pH & Ionization

Each enzyme has an optimal pH where the ionization state of acidic and basic amino acid R-groups at the active site is ideal.

Pepsin (Stomach): pH ~1.5–2.0
Salivary Amylase: pH ~6.8
Trypsin (Duodenum): pH ~7.8–8.5

Drastic pH shifts alter ionic bonds, leading to active site inactivation or denaturation.

Factor 3

Substrate Concentration

Increasing substrate concentration increases reaction velocity linearly at low concentrations (first-order kinetics).

Vmax (Maximum Velocity): Plateau occurs when all enzyme active sites are 100% saturated.

At saturation, reaction becomes zero-order with respect to substrate.

Factor 4

Enzyme Concentration

Assuming excess substrate is available, the reaction velocity is directly proportional to enzyme concentration.

Linear Relationship: Rate ∝ [Enzyme]

Adding more active sites directly increases catalytic turnover.

Section 07 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

7. Enzyme Inhibition: Competitive vs. Non-Competitive

Inhibitors are chemical agents that bind to enzymes and diminish their catalytic rate. They are clinically and physiologically vital.

Competitive Inhibition

Competitive Inhibitor

  • Structural Analogy: Closely resembles the natural substrate in 3D geometry.
  • Binding Target: Directly binds to the Active Site of the free enzyme.
  • Competition: Competes head-to-head with substrate for the active pocket.
  • Overcoming Inhibition: CAN be completely overcome by increasing substrate concentration [S].
  • Kinetic Impact: Apparent Km increases (lower binding affinity); Vmax remains unchanged.
  • MDCAT Classic Example: Malonate inhibiting Succinate Dehydrogenase (succinate is the substrate). Sulfonamides (sulfa drugs) inhibiting bacterial folate synthesis.
Non-Competitive (Allosteric)

Non-Competitive Inhibitor

  • Structural Analogy: Has no structural resemblance to the substrate.
  • Binding Target: Binds to an Allosteric Site (a distinct regulatory site away from the active site).
  • Mechanism: Induces an allosteric conformational shift in the active site, destroying catalytic capability.
  • Overcoming Inhibition: CANNOT be overcome simply by adding more substrate.
  • Kinetic Impact: Vmax is reduced; Km remains unchanged.
  • MDCAT Classic Example: Heavy metal poisoning (Ag⁺, Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺) binding cysteine -SH groups; Cyanide inhibiting Cytochrome Oxidase in the ETC.

Cellular Respiration is the catabolic, enzyme-catalyzed biochemical process whereby chemical bond energy stored in nutrient molecules (predominantly glucose) is released and coupled to ATP synthesis.

9. Glycolysis (The Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas Pathway)

Glycolysis (literally "sugar splitting") is the universal, evolutionarily ancient pathway occurring in the cytosol of almost all living cells.

Location Cytoplasm / Cytosol
Starting Substrate 1 Molecule of Glucose (6C)
End Products 2 Molecules of Pyruvate (3C)
Oxygen Requirement Does NOT require O₂ (Anaerobic)

The Two Distinct Phases of Glycolysis

Phase 1: Preparatory Phase
Energy Investment (2 ATP Consumed)

Glucose is phosphorylated and rearranged into Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, then cleaved into two triose phosphates (DHAP and G-3-P).

  • Step 1 (Hexokinase): Glucose ➔ Glucose-6-Phosphate (Uses 1 ATP).
  • Step 3 (Phosphofructokinase-1 / PFK-1): Fructose-6-P ➔ Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (Uses 1 ATP). (The primary rate-limiting, committed step of glycolysis!)
Phase 2: Payoff Phase
Energy Payoff (4 ATP + 2 NADH Formed)

The two triose phosphates are oxidized to 2 molecules of pyruvate, generating high-energy phosphate bonds.

  • Step 6 (G-3-P Dehydrogenase): 2 G-3-P ➔ 2 (1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate) + 2 NADH.
  • Steps 7 & 10 (Kinases): Substrate-level phosphorylation yields 4 ATP (2 ATP via Phosphoglycerate kinase, 2 ATP via Pyruvate kinase).
Glycolysis Net Balance Sheet (Per 1 Glucose Molecule)
Gross ATP Produced: 4 ATP (Substrate-level)
ATP Invested: -2 ATP (Hexokinase + PFK-1)
Net ATP Gain: +2 ATP
Reduced Coenzymes: +2 NADH (+ 2 H⁺)
End Carbon Products: 2 Pyruvate (3C each)
🔥 MDCAT Must-Remember!

Substrate-Level Phosphorylation: Direct synthesis of ATP from ADP by transfer of a high-energy phosphate group from a phosphorylated metabolic intermediate (e.g., PEP ➔ Pyruvate), occurring without an electron transport chain or oxygen.

Section 10 • Link Reaction

10. Pyruvate Oxidation (The Transition Step / Link Reaction)

Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate produced in the cytosol is actively transported across the mitochondrial membranes into the mitochondrial matrix by a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier.

Pyruvate (3C)
+ CoA-SH + NAD⁺ ➔
Acetyl-CoA (2C)
+
CO₂ (1C) + NADH

Key Events of the Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Complex

  • 1. Decarboxylation: The carboxyl group of pyruvate is cleaved off and released as carbon dioxide gas (CO₂). This is the first carbon dioxide released in aerobic respiration.
  • 2. Oxidation: The remaining two-carbon hydroxyethyl group is oxidized to an acetyl group, transferring electrons to NAD⁺ to form NADH.
  • 3. Coenzyme A Attachment: The acetyl group is attached to the sulfur atom of Coenzyme A (CoA) forming high-energy Acetyl-CoA.
Per 1 Glucose (2 Pyruvates) Yield: 2 Acetyl-CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH
Section 11 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

11. Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle / TCA Cycle)

Discovered by Sir Hans Krebs (Nobel Prize 1953), the Krebs Cycle (Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle) is the central metabolic cyclic hub operating in the mitochondrial matrix.

Entry

Acetyl-CoA (2C) condenses with Oxaloacetate (4C) ➔ Citrate (6C) (Catalyzed by Citrate Synthase; CoA-SH is released).

Isomerization

Citrate is isomerized to Isocitrate (6C) by the enzyme Aconitase.

1st Oxidation

Isocitrate ➔ α-Ketoglutarate (5C) + CO₂ + NADH (Catalyzed by Isocitrate Dehydrogenase).

2nd Oxidation

α-Ketoglutarate ➔ Succinyl-CoA (4C) + CO₂ + NADH (Catalyzed by α-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase).

ATP Generation

Succinyl-CoA ➔ Succinate (4C) + 1 ATP/GTP (Substrate-level phosphorylation by Succinyl-CoA Synthetase).

3rd Oxidation

Succinate ➔ Fumarate (4C) + 1 FADH₂ (Catalyzed by Succinate Dehydrogenase, an enzyme bound to the inner mitochondrial membrane!).

Hydration

Fumarate + H₂O ➔ Malate (4C) (Catalyzed by Fumarase).

Regeneration

Malate ➔ Oxaloacetate (4C) + NADH (Catalyzed by Malate Dehydrogenase, regenerating oxaloacetate for the next turn).

Yield Per 1 Acetyl-CoA (1 Turn)
  • 3 NADH (+ 3 H⁺)
  • 1 FADH₂
  • 1 ATP (or GTP)
  • 2 CO₂ molecules
Yield Per 1 Glucose Molecule (2 Turns)
  • 6 NADH (+ 6 H⁺)
  • 2 FADH₂
  • 2 ATP (or GTP)
  • 4 CO₂ molecules

Section 12 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

12. The Electron Transport Chain (ETC)

The Electron Transport Chain consists of a series of multi-protein electron complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded exclusively within the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae).

Complex I
NADH-Q Oxidoreductase

Accepts electrons from NADH (oxidizing it to NAD⁺). Pumps 4 H⁺ into intermembrane space.

Complex II
Succinate-Q Reductase

Accepts electrons from FADH₂ (via succinate oxidation). Does NOT pump any H⁺ ions!

Complex III
Cytochrome c Reductase

Transfers electrons from ubiquinone (CoQ) to Cytochrome c. Pumps 4 H⁺ into intermembrane space.

Complex IV
Cytochrome c Oxidase

Transfers 4 electrons to molecular oxygen (O₂) to form water (H₂O). Pumps 2 H⁺ into intermembrane space.

Mobile Electron Carriers in the Inner Membrane

  • Ubiquinone (Coenzyme Q / CoQ): A hydrophobic, lipid-soluble quinone that shuttles electrons through the hydrophobic core of the bilayer from Complexes I and II to Complex III.
  • Cytochrome c (Cyt c): A small water-soluble peripheral hemeprotein located on the outer intermembrane surface that shuttles electrons between Complex III and Complex IV.
Section 13 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

13. Chemiosmosis & Oxidative Phosphorylation

Proposed by Peter Mitchell (1961), Chemiosmosis is the mechanism coupling the flow of protons (H⁺) down an electrochemical gradient through ATP Synthase to drive the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP.

1
Proton Pumping

Exergonic electron transfer through Complexes I, III, and IV drives active pumping of H⁺ from the matrix into the narrow intermembrane space.

2
Electrochemical Gradient

Creates a steep concentration gradient (lower pH in intermembrane space) and voltage gradient across the membrane (the Proton Motive Force).

3
ATP Synthesis

Protons can only re-enter the matrix through the F₀ channel of ATP Synthase, causing the catalytic F₁ headpiece to rotate and synthesize ATP.

🔥 MDCAT Trap Alert! Role of Oxygen

Oxygen does NOT react directly with glucose or carbon atoms. Oxygen serves exclusively as the terminal (final) electron acceptor at Complex IV of the ETC. Without oxygen, the entire electron transport chain backs up, NADH cannot be reoxidized to NAD⁺, and aerobic respiration halts.

Section 14 • High-Yield ⭐⭐⭐

14. Fermentation (Anaerobic Respiration)

When oxygen is absent or depleted (hypoxic conditions), cells cannot use the ETC. Fermentation provides an alternate anaerobic mechanism to re-oxidize NADH back to NAD⁺, enabling glycolysis to keep running.

Lactic Acid Pathway

1. Lactic Acid Fermentation

Occurs in strenuously exercising human skeletal muscle cells and lactic acid bacteria (e.g. Lactobacillus in yogurt production).

Pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ ⇄ Lactate + NAD⁺
  • Enzyme: Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH).
  • Decarboxylation: NO CO₂ is produced! (Pyruvate is 3C, Lactate is 3C).
  • Fate: Lactate is exported via bloodstream to the liver where it is converted back to glucose via the Cori Cycle (Gluconeogenesis).
Alcoholic Pathway

2. Alcoholic Fermentation

Occurs in yeast (e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and certain plant tissues under anaerobic conditions.

Pyruvate ➔ Acetaldehyde + CO₂ ➔ Ethanol + NAD⁺
  • Enzymes: Pyruvate Decarboxylase (releases CO₂) + Alcohol Dehydrogenase.
  • Decarboxylation: CO₂ IS PRODUCED! (Causes dough to rise in baking & carbonation in brewing).
  • End Product: Ethanol (2-carbon alcohol).

15. Beta-Oxidation of Fatty Acids

Beta-Oxidation is the catabolic pathway by which fatty acid molecules are sequentially broken down in the mitochondrial matrix to generate high yields of Acetyl-CoA.

Mechanism of Beta-Oxidation

A fatty acyl-CoA chain undergoes iterative 4-step cycles (Oxidation ➔ Hydration ➔ Oxidation ➔ Thiolysis). In each cycle, the bond between the α-carbon (C2) and β-carbon (C3) is cleaved.

Per 2-Carbon Cleavage Cycle 1 Acetyl-CoA (2C) + 1 NADH + 1 FADH₂
Palmitic Acid (16C Fatty Acid) Net Yield ≈ 106 ATP!

Because fatty acids are in a much more chemically reduced state than carbohydrates, 1 gram of fat yields approximately 9 kcal/g (38 kJ/g) of energy, compared to only 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g) for carbohydrates or proteins.

17 High-Yield Bioenergetics Golden Rules

Direct high-frequency concepts frequently tested in national entrance exams. Click any card to copy or highlight.

01

ATP is the immediate chemical energy currency of the cell, not long-term storage.

02

Glycolysis takes place strictly in the cytoplasm / cytosol without direct O₂ requirement.

03

One molecule of 6C glucose yields two 3C pyruvate molecules during glycolysis.

04

Net gain of glycolysis is 2 ATP (4 produced − 2 invested) and 2 NADH.

05

Pyruvate Oxidation (Link Reaction) converts pyruvate into Acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH in the matrix.

06

Krebs Cycle takes place exclusively in the mitochondrial matrix.

07

Per acetyl-CoA, Krebs yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP (or GTP), and 2 CO₂.

08

The Electron Transport Chain (ETC) is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

09

NADH and FADH₂ act as mobile reduced coenzymes carrying high-energy electrons.

10

Oxygen (O₂) is the final electron acceptor in aerobic respiration, forming metabolic water (H₂O).

11

ATP Synthase utilizes the electrochemical H⁺ proton gradient via chemiosmosis to synthesize ATP.

12

Enzymes accelerate reaction rates by lowering activation energy (Ea) without altering ΔG.

13

Enzymes are biological catalysts that are not permanently consumed or altered in the reaction.

14

Catabolism is oxidative and releases energy; Anabolism is reductive and consumes energy.

15

Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue producing ATP under anaerobic conditions.

16

17

ATP Structure & Hydrolysis

Adenine + Ribose + 3 Phosphates. Hydrolysis ΔG ≈ -30.5 kJ/mol.
02
Metabolism (Anabolism vs Catabolism)

Anabolism = Assemble (Endergonic). Catabolism = Cut (Exergonic).
03
Enzymes & Activation Energy

Lowers Eₐ; does not alter ΔG or equilibrium Kₑq.
04
Enzyme Inhibition

Competitive = ↑Kₘ, same Vₘₐₓ. Non-competitive = ↓Vₘₐₓ, same Kₘ.
05
Glycolysis Pathway

Cytosol. 1 Glucose ➔ 2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH + net 2 ATP.
06
Pyruvate Oxidation (Link Step)

Matrix. 2 Pyruvate ➔ 2 Acetyl-CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH.
07
Krebs / Citric Acid Cycle

Matrix. Per glucose (2 turns): 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 ATP, 4 CO₂.
08
Electron Transport Chain (ETC)

Inner membrane. Complexes I-IV, CoQ & Cytochrome c pump H⁺.
09
Chemiosmosis & O₂ Role

Proton motive force drives ATP Synthase. O₂ is terminal acceptor.
10
Fermentation (Lactate vs Alcohol)

Regenerates NAD⁺. Lactic = Lactate (no CO₂). Alcoholic = Ethanol + CO₂.
11
Beta Oxidation

Fatty acids cleaved into Acetyl-CoA in matrix. High ATP yield.
