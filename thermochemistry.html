1. Thermochemistry Basics

What happens to heat during chemical changes?

WHAT IS IT?
Thermochemistry is the study of heat changes during chemical reactions.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
When chemical bonds break and new bonds form, energy is transferred. If heat goes out of the reaction into the surrounding air or water, the reaction is exothermic. If heat enters the reaction from the surroundings, the reaction is endothermic.
EXAMPLE
Burning natural gas on your kitchen stove releases heat (exothermic). Melting an ice cube absorbs heat from the air (endothermic).
MDCAT TIP
Thermochemistry only measures the heat change (ΔH) between reactants and products. It does not tell you how fast a reaction is (that is kinetics).
🌐

2. System and Surroundings

Defining the boundaries of a chemical reaction

WHAT IS A SYSTEM?
The system is the exact reaction mixture or substance under study.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
It consists of the reacting molecules, ions, and products. Everything that directly takes part in the reaction is inside the system.
EXAMPLE
Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide reacting inside a beaker.
MDCAT TIP
A closed system can exchange energy with surroundings, but cannot exchange matter.
WHAT ARE SURROUNDINGS?
The surroundings are everything outside the reaction system.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
The beaker glass, the water dissolving the ions, the thermometer, and the room air are all part of the surroundings.
EXAMPLE
When a thermometer shows temperature going UP, the surroundings gained heat from the system.
MDCAT TIP
System + Surroundings = Universe. Heat lost by the system equals heat gained by the surroundings!
🌡️

3. Heat (q)

Energy transfer due to temperature differences

WHAT IS HEAT?
Heat (q) is thermal energy flowing from a hotter body to a colder body.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
Heat is measured in Joules (J) or kiloJoules (kJ). By convention:
• If heat leaves the system into surroundings: q is negative (−)
• If heat enters the system from surroundings: q is positive (+)
EXAMPLE
If a reaction gives off 500 Joules of heat to the water around it, q(reaction) = −500 J, and q(water) = +500 J.
MDCAT TIP
Heat is a path function, not a state function. The amount of heat exchanged depends on the pathway followed.
⚖️

4 & 5. Exothermic vs Endothermic Reactions

Comparing heat flow, enthalpy change, and temperature shifts

🔥 Exothermic Reactions

ΔH is Negative (−)
WHAT IS IT?
A reaction that releases heat to its surroundings.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
Energy of reactants is higher than energy of products. Excess energy is released as heat. Temperature of surroundings rises.
EXAMPLE
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O    (ΔH = −890 kJ/mol)
MDCAT TIP
All combustion and all acid-base neutralization reactions are exothermic!

❄️ Endothermic Reactions

ΔH is Positive (+)
WHAT IS IT?
A reaction that absorbs heat from its surroundings.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
Energy of products is higher than energy of reactants. System takes thermal energy from surroundings. Temperature of surroundings drops.
EXAMPLE
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂    (ΔH = +178 kJ/mol)
MDCAT TIP
Thermal decomposition of metal carbonates and photosynthesis are endothermic!

6 & 7. Enthalpy (H) and Enthalpy Change (ΔH)

The total heat content of a chemical system

WHAT IS ENTHALPY (H)?
Enthalpy (H) is the total heat content of a system at constant pressure.
HOW DOES ENTHALPY CHANGE (ΔH) WORK?
We cannot measure absolute enthalpy H directly. We can only measure the change:
ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)
• If H(products) < H(reactants), then ΔH is negative (Exothermic)
• If H(products) > H(reactants), then ΔH is positive (Endothermic)
EXAMPLE
If reactants have 300 kJ and products have 100 kJ, then ΔH = 100 − 300 = −200 kJ (heat was released).
MDCAT TIP
Enthalpy (H) is a state function. Its change depends only on initial and final states, not the path taken.

9 & 10. Specific Heat Capacity vs Heat Capacity

Understanding heat requirements for substances

WHAT IS SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY (c)?
The heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 Kelvin).
FORMULA & UNITS
q = m × c × ΔT  →  c = q / (m × ΔT)
Unit: J / (g · °C) or J / (g · K)
EXAMPLE
Water has a high specific heat capacity of 4.184 J/g·°C. That is why water heats up and cools down slowly.
MDCAT TIP
Specific heat is an intensive property (does not depend on the amount of substance).
WHAT IS HEAT CAPACITY (C)?
The heat needed to raise the temperature of an entire object or body by 1 °C (or 1 Kelvin).
FORMULA & UNITS
C = q / ΔT  or  C = m × c
Unit: J / °C or J / K (Notice no mass in unit!)
EXAMPLE
A bomb calorimeter with a heat capacity of 5000 J/°C absorbs 5000 Joules for every 1 °C temperature rise.
MDCAT TIP
Heat capacity (capital C) is an extensive property (it depends on mass).

11 & 12. Enthalpies of Formation & Combustion

Standard conditions: 298 K (25 °C) and 1 atmosphere pressure

STANDARD ENTHALPY OF FORMATION (ΔHf°)
The heat change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states.
CRITICAL RULE
The standard enthalpy of formation of any pure element in its standard state is ZERO.
• ΔHf° of O₂(g) = 0
• ΔHf° of C(graphite) = 0
• ΔHf° of Na(s) = 0
REACTION FORMULA
ΔH°_rxn = Total ΔHf°(Products) − Total ΔHf°(Reactants)
MDCAT TIP
More negative ΔHf° means the compound is more thermodynamically stable!
STANDARD ENTHALPY OF COMBUSTION (ΔHc°)
The heat released when 1 mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen.
ALWAYS EXOTHERMIC
Combustion ALWAYS gives off heat. Therefore, ΔHc° is ALWAYS NEGATIVE.
EXAMPLE
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)    ΔHc° = −393.5 kJ/mol
MDCAT TIP
For carbon dioxide, the enthalpy of formation of CO₂ is identical to the enthalpy of combustion of carbon!

13. Enthalpy of Neutralization (ΔHn°)

Acid and base reaction forming one mole of water

WHAT IS IT?
The heat released when an acid and a base react to produce 1 mole of water (H₂O).
STRONG ACID + STRONG BASE VALUE
For ANY strong acid with ANY strong base, the reaction is simply:
H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H₂O(l)    ΔHn° = −57.3 kJ/mol
The value is constant because both acids and bases are completely ionized.
WEAK ACID / WEAK BASE EXCEPTION
For weak acids (like CH₃COOH) or weak bases, the heat released is LESS negative (e.g. −55 kJ/mol) because some heat is consumed to ionize the weak acid first.
MDCAT TIP
Neutralization is ALWAYS exothermic (ΔHn is always negative).

14. Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

Path independence of total enthalpy change

WHAT IS HESS'S LAW?
The overall enthalpy change of a chemical reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or in several steps.
TWO GOLDEN RULES FOR HESS'S LAW
1. Reverse the reaction → Reverse the sign of ΔH (+ becomes −, − becomes +)
2. Multiply reaction by n → Multiply ΔH by n
PATHWAY PRINCIPLE
Direct Path: A → C (ΔH_total)  =  Indirect Path: A → B (ΔH₁) + B → C (ΔH₂)
MDCAT TIP
Hess's Law is a direct consequence of the Law of Conservation of Energy (First Law of Thermodynamics).

15. Bond Energy & Enthalpy from Bonds

Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases energy

WHAT IS BOND ENERGY?
The average energy needed to break 1 mole of a specific chemical bond in gaseous state.
THE FUNDAMENTAL RULE
• Breaking bonds = NEEDS energy (Endothermic, +ΔH)
• Making bonds = RELEASES energy (Exothermic, −ΔH)
BOND ENERGY FORMULA
ΔH = Energy of Bonds Broken − Energy of Bonds Formed
(Notice: Reactants minus Products for bond energies!)
MDCAT TIP
If stronger bonds are formed than broken, the overall reaction is exothermic!

19. State Functions vs Path Functions

Properties determined only by current state

WHAT IS A STATE FUNCTION?
A macroscopic property whose value depends only on the present state of the system, not on how it got there.
EXAMPLES OF STATE FUNCTIONS
• Pressure (P)
• Volume (V)
• Temperature (T)
• Internal Energy (E or U)
• Enthalpy (H)
MDCAT TIP
For any state function, the change around a closed cycle is always ZERO (ΔH_cycle = 0).
WHAT IS A PATH FUNCTION?
A quantity whose value depends on the path taken between states.
EXAMPLES OF PATH FUNCTIONS
• Heat (q)
• Work (w)
MDCAT TIP
Heat (q) and Work (w) are NOT state functions! But at constant pressure, heat equals enthalpy change (q_p = ΔH), which is a state function.

20. Particle Kinetic Energy & Heating

Microscopic view: Temperature is average kinetic energy

🔥 Particle Motion Simulation

Medium Speed
WHAT IS TEMPERATURE?
Temperature is a direct measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.
HOW DOES IT WORK?
• Low Energy / Cold: Particles vibrate or move slowly with few effective collisions.
• High Energy / Hot: Particles move rapidly, collide with high energy, and easily cross activation barriers.
MDCAT TIP
At Absolute Zero (0 Kelvin or −273.15 °C), all molecular translational motion ceases.

21. Combustion Chamber Lab

Fuel + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + Heat

🔥 Flame Reaction Simulation

Always Exothermic
COMBUSTION BASICS
Combustion is the rapid oxidation of a fuel by oxygen, accompanied by heat and light.
WHY IS IT ALWAYS EXOTHERMIC?
The double bonds in C=O (in CO₂) and single bonds in O–H (in H₂O) are extremely strong. Forming these strong bonds releases far more energy than is required to break the weaker fuel and O=O bonds.
MDCAT TIP
Enthalpy of combustion (ΔHc) is measured using a Bomb Calorimeter at constant volume.

22. MDCAT Thermochemistry Formula Wall

All essential formulas rendered in clean HTML with usage instructions

q = m × c × ΔT
1. Calorimetry Heat Formula
m = mass (g), c = specific heat (J/g·°C), ΔT = Tf − Ti (°C)
When to use: To calculate heat gained or lost by a solution or water in a calorimeter.
💡 Tip: Convert q (Joules) to kJ by dividing by 1000!
C = q / ΔT = m × c
2. Heat Capacity Formula
C = heat capacity (J/°C or J/K)
When to use: When given an entire calorimeter without individual mass.
💡 Tip: Heat capacity (C) does not have grams in its unit!
ΔH = q_p / n
3. Molar Enthalpy Change
q_p = heat at constant pressure (kJ), n = moles reacted
When to use: To find heat released per mole of limiting reactant.
💡 Tip: Remember to assign the negative sign if exothermic!
ΔH°_rxn = Σ ΔHf°(Products) − Σ ΔHf°(Reactants)
4. Formation Enthalpy Summation
Sum of products' formation enthalpies minus reactants' formation enthalpies.
When to use: When standard enthalpies of formation (ΔHf°) are given in the problem.
💡 Tip: Pure elements in standard state have ΔHf° = 0!
ΔH = Σ E(Bonds Broken) − Σ E(Bonds Formed)
5. Bond Energy Enthalpy
Energy of broken reactant bonds minus energy of formed product bonds.
When to use: When individual bond dissociation energies are provided.
💡 Tip: Notice this is Reactants minus Products!
ΔH_total = ΔH₁ + ΔH₂ + ΔH₃ + ...
6. Hess's Law Summation
Total enthalpy change equals sum of stepwise reactions.
When to use: When adding multiple intermediate chemical reactions.
💡 Tip: Reversing a reaction flips the sign of ΔH!
ΔH = ΔE + PΔV
7. First Law Enthalpy Relation
ΔE = internal energy change, PΔV = expansion work
When to use: Relating internal energy change with enthalpy for gaseous reactions.
💡 Tip: For reactions with no gas volume change, ΔH ≈ ΔE!
Ea(rev) = Ea(fwd) − ΔH
8. Reverse Activation Energy
Relationship between forward Ea, reverse Ea, and reaction enthalpy.
When to use: Calculating reverse reaction activation barrier.
💡 Tip: For exothermic reactions, Ea(rev) is always greater than Ea(fwd)!

23. MDCAT High-Frequency Traps

Common examiner tricks and misunderstandings to avoid

⚠️ Trap 1: Temperature Rise vs ΔH Sign
RULE: When thermometer temperature rises, ΔH is NEGATIVE!
The thermometer is part of the surroundings. Temperature rises because the system lost heat. Therefore ΔH(reaction) < 0.
⚠️ Trap 2: Bond Breaking is Never Exothermic
RULE: Breaking a chemical bond ALWAYS absorbs energy (+ΔH).
No bond breaks spontaneously to release heat. Energy is released only when new bonds form!
⚠️ Trap 3: Catalyst and ΔH
RULE: A catalyst has ZERO effect on ΔH of a reaction.
A catalyst lowers activation energy (Ea) equally in both forward and reverse directions, leaving the net enthalpy change untouched.
⚠️ Trap 4: Pure Elements Formation Enthalpy
RULE: ΔHf° is zero ONLY in the element's standard state!
ΔHf° of C(graphite) = 0, but ΔHf° of C(diamond) = +1.9 kJ/mol. ΔHf° of O₂(g) = 0, but ΔHf° of O₃(g) = +142.7 kJ/mol!
⚠️ Trap 5: Formation vs Bond Energy Formula
RULE: Formation is (Products − Reactants), but Bond Energy is (Reactants − Products)!
Never mix up these two directions in enthalpy calculations.
⚠️ Trap 6: Specific Heat vs Heat Capacity Units
RULE: Specific heat = J/(g·°C); Heat capacity = J/°C.
If the question gives J/°C, you do NOT multiply by mass in q = C × ΔT!
⚠️ Trap 7: Reversing Reactions in Hess's Law
RULE: When an equation is flipped, change the sign of ΔH.
Forgetting to flip the negative sign to positive is the #1 math error in MDCAT Hess's Law questions.
⚠️ Trap 8: Water Mass in Calorimetry
RULE: In aqueous calorimetry, 1 mL of solution = 1 gram of water.
Mixing 50 mL acid + 50 mL base gives a total mass of 100 grams for the m in q = mcΔT.
