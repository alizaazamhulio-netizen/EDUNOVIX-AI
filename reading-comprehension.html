Strategic Methodology

Smart Reading Strategy

Follow the battle-tested 10-step analytical methodology used by top scorers in competitive and academic reading exams.

Before diving into detailed line-by-line reading, quickly scan the first sentence of each paragraph. This primes your working memory with the author's intellectual roadmap, allowing you to anticipate arguments rather than reacting passively.
The main idea is the unifying message that justifies the entire text. Details (examples, statistics, anecdotes) merely support that claim. Beware of trap choices that accurately restate a paragraph detail but fail to summarize the holistic text.
Transitions are the compass needles of academic prose. Words like however, nevertheless, conversely signal crucial pivots where the author's genuine viewpoint often diverges from conventional consensus.
Ask: Why did the writer include paragraph 2? Does it introduce a counterargument, provide empirical validation, offer a historical comparison, or propose a policy remedy? Understanding function prevents factual confusion.
Note the valence of adjectives and adverbs. Is the author alarmed, cautiously optimistic, skeptical, or strictly objective? Notice modal words like might, may, likely, conclusively.
Identify the precise objective of the question stem (e.g. Inference vs Explicit Detail vs NOT/EXCEPT). Formulate a preliminary answer in your mind before reading the four choices to prevent being anchored by attractive distractors.
Never answer from hazy memory. Locate the exact paragraph and lines referenced in the question stem. The correct answer will be an airtight paraphrase of the passage's explicit words or inescapable logical implications.
Watch for absolute words: always, never, all, completely, solely, impossible. In academic reading, nuanced and moderate claims (often, tend to, can lead to) are overwhelmingly more likely to be correct.
A statement may be true in the real world, but if the passage does not support it, it is 100% incorrect on a reading comprehension test. Strict validity exists only within the universe of the author's words.
When split between two plausible choices, contrast them word-for-word against the passage. One of the options will contain a subtle fatal flaw (e.g., swapping a cause for an effect, or misattributing a speaker's opinion).

Spot the Trap Distractors

Test writers construct predictable incorrect answer patterns. Learn to identify and neutralize the 6 most common trap designs.

Trap #1

The Extreme Statement

Takes a moderate authorial point and exaggerates it with unyielding absolutes (always, completely, never, every, impossible).

Detection Clue: Look for unqualified categorical terms that go beyond the author's measured tone.
Trap #2

The Partially Correct Halftruth

Contains 80% accurate phrasing lifted from the text, but inserts one false, inverted, or unsupported word at the very end.

Detection Clue: Read the entire option to the final word; traps often lurk in the trailing clause.
Trap #3

The Opposite Interpretation

Inverts cause and effect, confuses two contrasting subjects, or assigns the critic's view to the author.

Detection Clue: Check subject-verb attribution: who argued what in the original text?
Trap #4

The Outside Knowledge Bait

A statement that is factually accurate in general science or everyday life, but has zero textual evidence in the passage.

Detection Clue: Ask: "Did the author specifically write this here, or am I importing my own knowledge?"
Trap #5

The Right Fact, Wrong Question

Accurately quotes an authentic fact from paragraph 4 when the question stem explicitly asked about the main cause in paragraph 1.

Detection Clue: Re-read the question stem to verify relevance to the exact question asked.
Trap #6

The Unsupported Leap

Makes a plausible speculation that sounds reasonable, but cannot be strictly verified through direct deduction.

Detection Clue: Valid inferences require direct proof, not speculative likelihood.
