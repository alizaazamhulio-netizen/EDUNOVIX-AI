01

Introduction to Statistics

Statistics is the branch of applied mathematics that deals with the collection, organization, presentation, analysis, and interpretation of numerical and categorical data. In high-stakes university entry tests (such as FAST-NUCES, NUST, GIKI, and competitive merit scholarships), statistics questions test your quantitative reasoning, speed in computing averages, and ability to extract rapid insights from distributions, tables, and graphs.

💡 Why Statistics Matters for Entry Tests

Unlike calculus or trigonometry which require extensive symbolic manipulation, statistics problems in FAST tests can often be solved in under 30 seconds if you understand mental sum recovery, shift-invariance of spread, and empirical approximations.

Real-Life Statistical Contexts in FAST Problems:

  • Student Marks & GPAs: Weighted averages of credit hours across semesters.
  • Test Scores & Percentile Rankings: Normalizing entrance exam results.
  • Heights & Physical Measurements: Modeling continuous variations across candidate batches.
  • Daily Temperatures & Climate Records: Calculating mean deviations and seasonal ranges.
  • Survey & Polling Results: Inferring national preferences from representative samples.
  • Industrial Quality Control: Measuring batch variance and defect rates.

Data, Observations, and Datasets

To solve statistical questions without confusion, distinguish between these core terms:

1. Data

The raw factual information (numbers, words, or measurements) gathered together for reference or quantitative analysis.

2. Observation (Data Point)

A single individual measurement or record within the collected data. Denoted typically by xi.

3. Dataset

The entire structured collection of all n individual observations under consideration: {x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ}.

Population vs. Sample

Population (N)

The entire universe or complete collection of all possible items, individuals, or measurements possessing the specific characteristic being investigated.

  • Notation for population size: N
  • Population Mean: μ (mu)
  • Population Variance: σ² (sigma squared)
  • Population Standard Deviation: σ (sigma)

Sample (n)

A smaller, representative subset drawn directly from the population to estimate or infer properties about the whole population when testing all items is impractical.

  • Notation for sample size: n
  • Sample Mean: x̄ (x-bar)
  • Sample Variance: s² (divides by n - 1)
  • Sample Standard Deviation: s
Key Illustration: Concept Check

If FAST-NUCES has 12,000 enrolled students across all campuses, and researchers survey 400 randomly selected students to study average daily study hours:

• Population (N) = All 12,000 FAST students.
• Sample (n) = The 400 surveyed students.
• Why sample? Surveying all 12,000 takes excessive time, cost, and logistics, while 400 unbiased samples yield high statistical precision.

8 Conceptual Practice Questions:

1. Testing light bulbs in a factory of 50,000 units by inspecting 200:

Pop = 50,000 bulbs; Sample = 200 inspected bulbs.

2. National blood pressure study of 1,500 citizens in Pakistan:

Pop = All Pakistani citizens; Sample = 1,500 examined.

3. Exit poll asking 5,000 voters after an election:

Pop = All registered voters who cast ballots; Sample = 5,000 surveyed.

4. Average battery life of a batch of 10,000 laptops tested on 50:

Pop = 10,000 laptops in batch; Sample = 50 laptops tested.

5. Checking milk purity in a 5,000-liter milk tanker using 100 mL:

Pop = All 5,000 L of milk; Sample = 100 mL test beaker.

6. Measuring heights of 30 students in a section of 50:

Pop = All 50 students in section; Sample = 30 measured students.

7. Quality audit of 100 code commits out of 10,000 in a repository:

Pop = 10,000 commits; Sample = 100 audited commits.

Pop = 2,000 applicants; Sample = 50 reviewed candidates.

04

Types of Data: Qualitative vs. Quantitative

Data Category Sub-type Definition Examples
Qualitative (Categorical) Nominal Categories without natural mathematical ordering. Gender, blood group, city of birth, program (CS vs EE).
Ordinal Categories with an inherent ranked hierarchy. Letter grades (A, B, C, F), difficulty rating (Easy, Med, Hard), satisfaction levels.
Quantitative (Numerical) Discrete Countable values; gaps exist between possible values (usually integers). Number of books, family size, test questions correct, cars sold.
Continuous Measurable values on an unbroken continuum; can take infinite fractional values within an interval. Height, weight, temperature, exam time elapsed, vehicle speed.
⚠️ FAST Trap: Counting vs. Measuring

Whenever data results from counting, it is Discrete Quantitative. Whenever data results from measuring with an instrument (stopwatch, scale, ruler, thermometer), it is Continuous Quantitative.

Frequency & Cumulative Frequency

Frequency (f): The number of times a particular observation occurs in a dataset.
Total Frequency (Σf or N): The sum of all individual frequencies, which equals total sample size n.
Cumulative Frequency (cf): The running total of frequencies up to and including the current observation value.

07

Arithmetic Mean (x̄)

Arithmetic Mean Formula
x̄ = (Σx) / n = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / n
Where Σx is the sum of all individual observations and n is the total number of observations.

Properties of the Arithmetic Mean:

  1. Algebraic Balance Point: The sum of deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is always exactly zero: Σ(x - x̄) = 0.
  2. Sum Recovery: The total sum of observations can always be recovered as: Total (Σx) = Mean (x̄) × n.
  3. Uniqueness: For every dataset, there is one and only one arithmetic mean.
  4. Sensitivity: Every single observation enters into the computation. Hence, an extreme value (outlier) dramatically shifts the mean.

6 Typical Solved Mean Problems:

Ex 1: Standard Mean

Find the mean of: 12, 16, 20, 24, 28.

Sum = 100, n = 5. Mean = 100 / 5 = 20.

Ex 2: Mean with Decimals

Find the mean of: 3.5, 4.5, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0.

Sum = 32.0, n = 5. Mean = 32.0 / 5 = 6.4.

Ex 3: Negative Numbers

Find the mean of: -10, -5, 0, 15, 20.

Sum = 20, n = 5. Mean = 20 / 5 = 4.

Ex 4: Combined Mean

Batch A (10 students): mean 60. Batch B (20 students): mean 75.

Combined = (10×60 + 20×75)/30 = (600 + 1500)/30 = 2100/30 = 70.

FAST Shortcut: Missing Value Using Mean

🚀 The Universal Total-Sum Method

Never write long algebraic fractions. Follow these two mental steps:
Step 1: Total Required = Target Mean × n
Step 2: Missing Value = Total Required - Known Sum

Worked FAST Example: High-Frequency Question

The mean of five numbers 7, 11, 14, 18, x is 15. Find the value of x.

Step 1: Compute Total Required

Total Required = 15 × 5 = 75.

Step 2: Add Known Numbers

Known Sum = 7 + 11 + 14 + 18 = 50.

Step 3: Subtract

x = 75 - 50 = 25.

Linear Transformations & Effect on Mean

If original dataset {x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ} has mean x̄, then applying linear transformation y = ax + b transforms the mean identically:

Transformation on Every Observation Mathematical Effect on Mean Concrete Example (Original Mean = 50)
Add constant c (+c) x̄_new = x̄ + c Add 5 to each: New Mean = 50 + 5 = 55
Subtract constant c (-c) x̄_new = x̄ - c Subtract 8 from each: New Mean = 50 - 8 = 42
Multiply by factor k (×k) x̄_new = k · x̄ Double each (×2): New Mean = 2 × 50 = 100
Divide by factor k (÷k) x̄_new = x̄ / k Divide each by 5: New Mean = 50 / 5 = 10
General Linear: y = ax + b ȳ = a · x̄ + b If y = 3x - 10: ȳ = 3(50) - 10 = 140

Weighted Mean (x̄_w)

When different observations contribute unequally to the final average (such as university course credit hours or exam components with varying weightages), the simple mean is inaccurate and we must use the Weighted Arithmetic Mean.

Weighted Mean Formula
x̄_w = Σ(w · x) / Σw = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ... + wₖxₖ) / (w₁ + w₂ + ... + wₖ)
Where x represents the value and w represents its relative assigned weight.
FAST Exam Question: GPA Semester Calculation Solved Example

A FAST student achieved the following grades in Semester 1:

Subject Credit Hours (Weight, w) Grade Points (x) Product (w · x)
Programming Fundamentals43.6714.68
Calculus & Analytical Geometry34.0012.00
Applied Physics33.339.99
English Composition23.006.00
Total Σw = 12 — Σ(w·x) = 42.67
Calculation:

SGPA = Σ(w·x) / Σw = 42.67 / 12 = 3.5558 ≈ 3.56 GPA.

Median (Middle Value)

The Median is the positional middle value of a dataset that divides an ordered array into two equal halves (50% of values lie below the median, and 50% lie above).

⚠️ CRITICAL GOLDEN RULE: ALWAYS SORT FIRST

Never find the median directly from unsorted data! You MUST arrange observations in ascending (or descending) order first.

Case A: Odd Number of Items (n is Odd)

Position of median is (n + 1) / 2.

Data: 15, 3, 9, 21, 7
1. Sort: [3, 7, 9, 15, 21] (n = 5)
2. Pos: (5+1)/2 = 3rd item.
Median = 9

Case B: Even Number of Items (n is Even)

Median is the average of the two middle items at positions n/2 and (n/2 + 1).

Data: 18, 4, 12, 9, 25, 7
1. Sort: [4, 7, 9, 12, 18, 25] (n = 6)
2. Middle items: 9 and 12.
Median = (9 + 12) / 2 = 10.5

12

Mode (Most Frequent Observation)

The Mode is the observation that appears with the highest frequency in a dataset. It is the only measure of central tendency that can be used directly for qualitative/categorical data (e.g. most popular shoe size or programming language).

Classification Description Example Dataset Mode
Unimodal Exactly one unique value has the maximum frequency. 2, 4, 4, 4, 7, 9 Mode = 4 (freq = 3)
Bimodal Two distinct values tie for the highest frequency. 1, 3, 3, 3, 5, 8, 8, 8, 10 Modes = 3 and 8
Multimodal Three or more values share the highest frequency. 2, 2, 4, 4, 6, 6, 9 Modes = 2, 4, and 6
No Mode All values occur with the exact same frequency. 4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 20 No Mode
13

Mean vs. Median vs. Mode Comparison

Measure Best Used When Major Advantage Major Vulnerability
Arithmetic Mean Data is symmetric with no extreme outliers (e.g. test scores). Uses every data value mathematically; great for algebraic proofs. Extremely sensitive to extreme outliers.
Median Data is skewed or has severe outliers (e.g. salaries, house prices). Completely immune to extreme outliers at the boundaries. Ignores the actual numerical magnitude of non-median values.
Mode Identifying the most popular category, item, or common size. Works on non-numeric qualitative data (e.g. shirt sizes). May not exist or may have multiple values.
14

Empirical Relationship: Karl Pearson's Law

Empirical Approximation for Moderately Skewed Distributions
Mode ≈ 3(Median) - 2(Mean)
Rearranged Forms:
• Mean - Mode ≈ 3(Mean - Median)
• Median ≈ (Mode + 2·Mean) / 3
• Mean ≈ (3·Median - Mode) / 2
⚠️ Mandatory Note on Statistical Validity

This formula is an empirical approximation developed for unimodal, moderately skewed continuous distributions. It is NOT an exact mathematical identity for all arbitrary datasets. In FAST questions, use it whenever two measures are given and you are asked to estimate the third.

15

Range (Simplest Measure of Dispersion)

Range Formula
Range = Maximum Observation - Minimum Observation

Key Insights on Range:
• If every value in a dataset is increased by constant c, the range remains unchanged: Range_new = (Max + c) - (Min + c) = Max - Min.
• If every value is multiplied by constant k, the range is multiplied by |k|.

16

Variance (Population vs. Sample)

Variance measures the average of the squared deviations from the arithmetic mean.

Population Variance (σ²)
σ² = Σ(x - μ)² / N
Computational Shortcut:
σ² = (Σx² / N) - μ²
Sample Variance (s²)
s² = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n - 1)
Bessel's correction (n - 1) provides an unbiased estimator for sample data.
17

Standard Deviation (σ and s)

Standard Deviation Formula
Standard Deviation = √(Variance)
Population SD: σ = √(σ²) | Sample SD: s = √(s²)

Quartiles and Interquartile Range (IQR)

Quartiles divide an ordered dataset into 4 equal quarters (each containing 25% of data):

  • Q₁ (First / Lower Quartile): 25th percentile (median of lower half).
  • Q₂ (Second Quartile): 50th percentile = Median.
  • Q₃ (Third / Upper Quartile): 75th percentile (median of upper half).
Interquartile Range & Outlier Fences
IQR = Q₃ - Q₁
• Lower Outlier Boundary: Q₁ - 1.5 × IQR
• Upper Outlier Boundary: Q₃ + 1.5 × IQR
20

Percentiles vs. Percentages

Percentage (%)

An absolute score representing parts per hundred.

Example: Scoring 85 marks out of 100 on an entry test means you scored 85%.

Percentile

A relative measure of ranking within a candidate cohort.

Example: Scoring in the 85th percentile means your score is higher than or equal to 85% of all candidates who took the exam.

🚀 FAST Speed Techniques (10 High-Yield Shortcuts)

Shortcut 1: Total Sum Recovery

Always calculate Total = Mean × n before attempting missing value or modified average questions.

Shortcut 2: Shift-Invariance of Spread

Adding or subtracting any number to all values leaves Range, Variance, Standard Deviation, and IQR completely unchanged.

Shortcut 3: Arithmetic Progression Mean

For any evenly spaced sequence (AP), Mean = Median = (First + Last) / 2.

Shortcut 4: Adding Mean Leaves Mean Constant

If a new observation equal to the existing mean is introduced, the new mean is unchanged, while standard deviation decreases.

Shortcut 5: Replacing Elements in Average

New Element = Replaced Element + n × (New Mean - Old Mean).

Shortcut 6: Consecutive Odd/Even Numbers

The average of n consecutive numbers is simply the central number (for odd n) or the midpoint of the two central numbers (for even n).

Shortcut 7: Pie Chart 1% = 3.6°

To convert percentage to angle rapidly, multiply by 3.6 (e.g. 15% = 15 × 3.6 = 54°). To convert angle to %, divide by 3.6.

Shortcut 8: Equal Distance Average Speed

Average speed for equal forward/backward distance is the Harmonic Mean: 2v₁v₂ / (v₁ + v₂).

Shortcut 9: Sum of Deviations is Zero

Σ(x - x̄) = 0. If asked for Σ(x - 20) when mean is 25, it is simply n × (25 - 20) = 5n.

Shortcut 10: Standard Deviation of First n Integers

σ = √((n² - 1) / 12). For first 7 numbers: √((49-1)/12) = √4 = 2.

15 Detailed Solved Word Problems

Problem 1: Class Combined Average FAST Standard

In a class of 40 students, 24 boys averaged 70 marks, and 16 girls averaged 80 marks. Find the overall class average.

Understand & Given Data:

n₁ = 24, x̄₁ = 70; n₂ = 16, x̄₂ = 80; Total n = 40.

Formula:

Combined Mean = (n₁·x̄₁ + n₂·x̄₂) / (n₁ + n₂)

Substitute & Calculate:

Combined = (24×70 + 16×80) / 40 = (1680 + 1280) / 40 = 2960 / 40 = 74 marks.

Final Answer:

Overall Class Average = 74 marks.

Problem 2: Correction of Misread Observation Scholarship Level

The mean of 50 values was found to be 38. Later, it was discovered that 64 was misread as 44. Find the corrected mean.

Given Data:

n = 50, Old Mean = 38, Wrong Val = 44, Correct Val = 64.

Formula & Steps:

Initial Total = 50 × 38 = 1900.
Corrected Total = 1900 - 44 + 64 = 1920.
Corrected Mean = 1920 / 50 = 38.4.

Final Answer:

Correct Mean = 38.4.

Problem 3: Replacement Average Shift FAST Speed Problem

The average weight of 6 persons increases by 1.5 kg when a new person replaces someone weighing 50 kg. Find the weight of the new person.

Formula:

New Weight = Replaced Weight + (n × Average Increase)

Calculation:

New Weight = 50 + (6 × 1.5) = 50 + 9 = 59 kg.

Final Answer:

Weight of new person = 59 kg.

20 Common Mistakes & Traps to Avoid

❌ Wrong: Finding median of [15, 3, 9, 21, 7] as 9 by picking the 3rd item directly from raw input.
✅ Correct: Sort first [3, 7, 9, 15, 21], then pick middle term = 9.
💡 Explanation: Even if raw positions accidentally match in some examples, picking unsorted middle values fails on 95% of test questions. Always sort first!
❌ Wrong: Assuming adding 5 to each value increases Standard Deviation by 5.
✅ Correct: Standard Deviation remains completely unchanged.
💡 Explanation: Adding a constant shifts the entire distribution along the number line without altering the spread or distance between observations.
❌ Wrong: Multiplying data by -3 multiplies Standard Deviation by -3.
✅ Correct: SD is multiplied by |-3| = +3.
💡 Explanation: Standard deviation is a measure of distance from the mean and is strictly non-negative (SD ≥ 0).
❌ Wrong: Using sample variance formula dividing by n.
✅ Correct: Sample variance s² divides by (n - 1). Population variance σ² divides by N.
💡 Explanation: Dividing sample data by (n - 1) removes downward bias (Bessel's correction).
❌ Wrong: Treating Mode ≈ 3 Median - 2 Mean as an exact universal law.
✅ Correct: Treat it as an approximate empirical rule for moderately skewed continuous data.
💡 Explanation: Highly skewed or bimodal datasets will not satisfy Pearson's empirical approximation.

⚡ 5-Minute Quick Revision (High-Yield Summary)

  • Mean: Sum divided by count Σx / n. Sum of deviations is always Σ(x - x̄) = 0.
  • Median: Middle element of sorted array. (n+1)/2 for odd n; average of n/2 & n/2 + 1 for even n.
  • Mode: Most frequent data point. Can be unimodal, bimodal, or non-existent.
  • Empirical Rule: Mode ≈ 3(Median) - 2(Mean).
  • Range: Max - Min.
  • Population Variance: σ² = (Σx²/N) - μ².
  • Sample Variance: s² = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n - 1).
  • Standard Deviation: σ = √(Variance). Unaffected by addition/subtraction of constants; scaled by |k| when multiplied.
  • IQR: Q₃ - Q₁. Upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5·IQR; Lower fence = Q₁ - 1.5·IQR.
  • Pie Charts: Sector Angle = (Category / Total) × 360°. 1% corresponds to 3.6°.
