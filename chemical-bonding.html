Chemical Bonds, Valence Electrons & Octet Rule

1. What is a Chemical Bond? Core Law

A chemical bond is the attractive force that holds atoms or ions together in a chemical substance.

Why do atoms form bonds? Isolated atoms have higher potential energy. By interacting and redistributing electrons, atoms achieve a lower potential energy state with maximum electrostatic stability.

2. Valence Electrons ⭐ Outer Shell

Valence electrons are the electrons present in the outermost occupied shell (highest principal quantum number n) of an atom.

They determine: Chemical valency, ion formation capability, coordination number, and reactivity.

Sodium (Na: 2, 8, 1)  ⟶  Na⁺ (2, 8) + 1e⁻ (Valence e⁻ lost)
👑 3. The Octet Rule & Noble Gas Stability

Main-group atoms tend to lose, gain, or share electrons until they are surrounded by 8 valence electrons (noble gas configuration, ns² np⁶, or helium duet 1s²).

⚠️ Critical MDCAT Note: Exceptions to the Octet Rule
  • Incomplete Octet (Electron-Deficient molecules): BeCl₂ (4e⁻ on Be), BF₃ / BCl₃ (6e⁻ on B), AlCl₃ (monomer).
  • Expanded Octet (Hypervalent molecules with empty d-orbitals): PCl₅ (10e⁻ on P), SF₆ (12e⁻ on S), IF₇ (14e⁻ on I), H₂SO₄.
  • Odd-Electron Molecules: NO (11 valence e⁻), NO₂ (17 valence e⁻) — contain unpaired radical electrons.

Ionic Bonding & Crystal Lattice Properties

An ionic bond is the non-directional electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by the complete transfer of one or more valence electrons from an electropositive metal to an electronegative non-metal.

Covalent Bonding & Electron Sharing

A covalent bond is formed when two non-metal atoms mutually share one or more pairs of valence electrons to achieve stable noble-gas electron configurations.

06

Coordinate Covalent (Dative) Bond

A coordinate covalent bond (dative bond) is a type of covalent bond in which both shared electrons are supplied by one of the participating atoms (the donor / Lewis base) into the empty orbital of an electron-deficient species (the acceptor / Lewis acid).

🔄 Formation of Ammonium Ion [NH₄]⁺ Step-by-Step
Step 1: Isolated Reactants: NH₃ has an unshared lone pair of electrons on Nitrogen. H⁺ (proton) has an empty 1s orbital with zero electrons.
⭐ High-Yield MDCAT Rule: Coordinate Bond Behavior

Once formed, a coordinate covalent bond is completely indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond in terms of bond length, bond energy, and chemical reactivity.

Hybridization Theory (sp, sp², sp³)

Hybridization is the theoretical mixing of non-equivalent atomic orbitals (such as s and p orbitals of similar energy in the same atom) to generate a new set of identical, degenerate hybrid orbitals having specific directional geometry suitable for stable chemical bonding.

🧪 Select Hybridization State
sp Hybridization
  • Orbitals Mixed: 1 s + 1 p orbital mix → 2 sp hybrid orbitals
  • Spatial Geometry: Linear
  • Bond Angle: 180°
  • Character %: 50% s, 50% p character
  • Common Examples: BeCl₂, Ethyne (H–C≡C–H), CO₂, HgCl₂
Directional & Bonding Mechanism

The two sp hybrid orbitals orient in opposite directions along a straight line at 180° to minimize electrostatic repulsion.

Sigma (σ) and Pi (π) Bonds

Sigma (σ) Bond
Head-on / Coaxial Overlap
  • Formed by direct end-to-end overlap along the internuclear axis.
  • Stronger bond with cylindrical charge symmetry.
  • Allows free rotation around the bond axis.
  • Present in every single bond.
Pi (π) Bond
Sideways / Lateral Overlap
  • Formed by parallel lateral overlap of unhybridized p-orbitals.
  • Weaker bond (electron lobes lie above and below internuclear axis).
  • Restricts rotation (creates cis-trans geometric isomerism).
  • Double = 1σ + 1π; Triple = 1σ + 2π.

Intermolecular Forces & Hydrogen Bonding

⚖️ Fundamental Distinction: Intramolecular Bonds vs. Intermolecular Forces

Intramolecular Bonds: Ionic, covalent, and metallic bonds holding atoms/ions together inside a substance (~100 – 1000 kJ/mol).
Intermolecular Forces (IMF): Non-covalent electrostatic attractions between separate molecules (~1 – 50 kJ/mol), controlling boiling points, melting points, and physical states.

1. London Dispersion (LDF)

Present in all atoms and molecules. Caused by momentary fluctuations in electron distribution creating instantaneous and induced dipoles. Strength scales with molar mass, polarizability, and surface area.

2. Dipole-Dipole Forces

Electrostatic attractions between permanent dipoles of polar molecules (e.g., HCl···HCl). Stronger than LDF for molecules of comparable size.

3. Hydrogen Bonding ⭐

An extraordinarily strong dipole attraction occurring when Hydrogen is covalently bonded to small, highly electronegative atoms: Nitrogen (N), Oxygen (O), or Fluorine (F).

🌊 Compare Substances & Boiling Points
Water (H₂O)
Boiling Point: 100.0 °C
  • Predominant Intermolecular Force: Hydrogen Bonding (Extensive 3D Network)
  • Molecular Polarity: Highly Polar
  • MDCAT Mechanism: Each H₂O can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds; high enthalpy of vaporization.

A metallic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal cations arrayed in a regular crystal lattice and the surrounding cloud of delocalized, freely mobile valence electrons (the "Electron Sea Model").

Feature Sigma (σ) Bond Pi (π) Bond

Free Rotation Permitted around bond axis Restricted (causes geometric isomerism)
