Foundation

What is Error Detection?

Understanding the crucial distinction between finding an error and properly repairing sentence architecture.

Error Detection

Locating the violation

Error Detection is the analytical skill of scanning a sentence to pinpoint precisely which word, phrase, punctuation mark, or grammatical construction violates standard English syntax.

It requires testing grammatical dependencies: subject-verb alignment, tense consistency, modifier attachments, and correct idiomatic prepositions.

Sentence Correction

Restoring syntactic precision

Sentence Correction goes a step further by replacing the flawed segment with the most accurate, concise, and contextually appropriate construction without altering original meaning.

It demands structural mastery to ensure the revised sentence flows naturally while maintaining standard formal conventions.

Level 1

Beginner Error Categories

Master core structural principles: agreement, basic tenses, articles, pronouns, prepositions, and word order.

Subject-Verb Agreement

Singular vs. Plural Subjects

A singular subject requires a singular verb (-s/-es in present tense); a plural subject requires a base verb. Prepositional phrases between subject and verb never alter the verb.
❌ The list of new items are on the desk.
✓ The list of new items is on the desk.
Why: The true subject is the singular noun 'list'. The phrase 'of new items' is an intervening prepositional distractor.
Agreement Traps

Indefinite Singular Pronouns

Words like everyone, everybody, each, neither, either, someone, nobody are grammatically singular and require singular verbs.
❌ Each of the students have a textbook.
✓ Each of the students has a textbook.
Why: 'Each' refers to individuals one at a time, requiring the singular verb 'has'.
Tense Forms

Past Simple vs. Present Perfect

Do not mix the simple past form (went) with auxiliary verbs (has/have/had). Perfect tenses strictly require the past participle (gone).
❌ She has went home already.
✓ She has gone home already.
Why: After auxiliary 'has', the past participle 'gone' is required, never simple past 'went'.
Article Usage

Phonetic Sound & Non-Count Nouns

Use 'a' before consonant sounds (e.g., university starts with /j/ sound) and 'an' before vowel sounds (e.g., an hour starts with /aʊ/). Uncountable nouns do not take a/an.
❌ He is an university student and gave me an advice.
✓ He is a university student and gave me some advice.
Why: 'University' starts with the consonant /juː/ sound; 'advice' is uncountable and cannot take the indefinite article 'an'.
Pronoun Case

Compound Subjects & Objects

Use subjective pronouns (I, he, she, they, we) for subject positions. Use objective pronouns (me, him, her, them, us) after verbs and prepositions.
❌ Me and Ali went to the library, and she gave the book to he.
✓ Ali and I went to the library, and she gave the book to him.
Why: Test by removing 'Ali': 'I went' (not 'Me went'). After preposition 'to', objective pronoun 'him' is required.
Prepositions

Fixed Preposition Collocations

Certain adjectives and verbs pair with specific prepositions: good at (skill), interested in, depend on, afraid of, different from.
❌ She is good in English and depends of her parents.
✓ She is good at English and depends on her parents.
Why: English requires 'good at' for abilities and 'depend on' for reliance.
Word Form

Adjectives Modifying Nouns vs. Adverbs Modifying Verbs

Adjectives modify nouns (successful person). Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs (sings beautifully).
❌ She sings beautiful because she is a success singer.
✓ She sings beautifully because she is a successful singer.
Why: 'Sings' is an action verb needing adverb 'beautifully'; 'singer' is a noun needing adjective 'successful'.
Word Order

Frequency & Time Adverb Placement

Standard English follows Subject-Verb-Object (SVO). Adverbs of frequency (always, often, usually) go before main verbs, while specific time expressions (every day, yesterday) go at the end or start.
❌ I every day study English.
✓ I study English every day.
Why: The time adverbial phrase 'every day' naturally occupies sentence-final position, not wedged between Subject and Verb.
Nouns & Plurals

Uncountable Nouns with No Plural -s

Nouns such as furniture, luggage, baggage, equipment, information, scenery have no plural form and never take -s.
❌ All the furnitures and equipments were sold.
✓ All the furniture and equipment was sold.
Why: These are mass nouns that remain grammatically singular in form.
Negatives

Avoiding Double Negation

In standard English, two negative words in the same clause cancel each other out or create a nonstandard error. Do not pair not/never with no, nothing, nobody, hardly, scarcely.
❌ I don't know nothing about it.
✓ I don't know anything about it.
Why: Combine negative auxiliary 'don't' with non-assertive 'anything', not negative 'nothing'.

Parallelism

Balanced Grammatical Series

Items joined by coordinating conjunctions (and, or, but) or correlatives must match in grammatical form (all gerunds, all infinitives, or all clauses).
❌ Flawed: reading [gerund], swimming [gerund], and to dance [infinitive]
✓ Balanced: reading [gerund], swimming [gerund], and dancing [gerund]
❌ She likes reading, swimming and to dance.
✓ She likes reading, swimming and dancing.
Why: All elements in a series must maintain parallel syntactic weight.
Modifiers

Dangling & Misplaced Modifiers

An introductory participial phrase must modify the immediate subject of the following main clause. If the subject did not perform the action, the modifier dangles.
Logical Question: Who was walking?

In 'Walking to school, the rain started', the grammar implies the rain was walking.

❌ Walking to school, the rain started to fall.
✓ While I was walking to school, the rain started to fall.
Why: The rain cannot walk. Rephrase with a time clause or place the true subject ('I') immediately after the comma.
Verb Complements

Gerunds (-ing) vs. Infinitives (to + verb)

Verbs like enjoy, avoid, admit, suggest require gerunds. Verbs like decide, want, hope, agree, refuse require infinitives. Causative verbs (make, let) take bare infinitives.
❌ I enjoy to read, but she decided going home.
✓ I enjoy reading, but she decided to go home.
Why: 'Enjoy' takes a gerund object; 'decide' takes an infinitive.
Conditionals

Condition Clause Tense Restrictions

Never use will or would in the if-clause in standard conditionals. Use present simple for 1st conditional, past simple for 2nd, and past perfect for 3rd.
❌ If I will study hard, I will pass the exam.
✓ If I study hard, I will pass the exam.
Why: The condition clause in a First Conditional requires the simple present tense.
Voice & Verbs

Passive Structure: Be + Past Participle

Passive voice strictly consists of a form of be + past participle (V3). Watch out for missing auxiliaries or using simple past/present base forms.
❌ The critical report was write and submit yesterday.
✓ The critical report was written and submitted yesterday.
Why: The past participle forms are 'written' and 'submitted'.
Reported Speech

Said vs. Told vs. Asked

'Told' requires an indirect personal object (told me/him/us). 'Said' does not take a personal object directly (use said that or said to me).
❌ He told that he was late, and she said me the truth.
✓ He said that he was late, and she told me the truth.
Why: 'Told' needs an object; 'said' introduces the noun clause directly.
Relative Pronouns

Who (Subject) vs. Whom (Object) vs. Which (Things)

Use who for people doing the action, whom for people receiving the action (or after prepositions), and which/that for things and animals.
❌ The doctor which treated me is the man who I spoke to.
✓ The doctor who treated me is the man whom I spoke to.
Why: Doctors are persons (requiring who); in the second clause, 'I' is subject and the man is object of preposition 'to' (requiring whom).
Comparisons

Double Comparatives & Illogical Comparisons

Never combine more/-er (e.g., more taller) or most/-est (e.g., most fastest). Ensure comparisons compare like things (e.g., comparing scores to scores, not scores to people).
❌ She is more taller than her sister, and his score is higher than Ali.
✓ She is taller than her sister, and his score is higher than Ali's.
Why: 'Taller' already conveys the comparative. The score must be compared to Ali's score (Ali's), not Ali the human.
Conjunctions

Double Conjunctions & Preposition Errors

Do not use both a subordinating conjunction (Although, Since, Because) and a coordinating conjunction (but, so) in the same sentence. Despite takes a noun phrase (not despite of).
❌ Although he was exhausted, but he continued working.
✓ Although he was exhausted, he continued working.
Why: 'Although' already subordinates the first clause; adding 'but' breaks the grammatical connection.
Quantifiers

Few vs. A Few vs. Little vs. A Little

Few/a few modify countable plural nouns. Little/a little modify uncountable nouns. 'Few/little' have negative connotations (almost none); 'a few/a little' have positive connotations (some).
❌ I have little books and much questions to ask.
✓ I have a few books and many questions to ask.
Why: 'Books' and 'questions' are countable nouns requiring few/many.
Modals

Modal Auxiliaries & 'Could Have' vs. 'Could Of'

Modals (can, could, must, should, would) are followed by the bare infinitive (base verb). Never write could of / should of—the correct contraction is could have / should have.
❌ You must to attend the meeting and could of notified us.
✓ You must attend the meeting and could have notified us.
Why: 'Must' takes a bare verb without 'to'. The phonetically slurred 'could of' is a spelling mistake for 'could have'.
Linking Verbs

Sense & Linking Verbs

Linking verbs (taste, smell, feel, sound, look, seem, become) describe a state and take predicate adjectives, not adverbs.
❌ The soup tastes deliciously and feels warmly.
✓ The soup tastes delicious and feels warm.
Why: The soup is not performing the physical action of tasting; it possesses the quality of being delicious (adjective).

Advanced Error Categories

Tackle difficult patterns: inversion, subjunctive mood, mixed conditionals, redundancy, and collocations.

Inversion

Negative Adverb Inversion

When negative or restrictive adverbs (Never, Rarely, Seldom, Barely, Scarcely, Only then) begin a clause, the auxiliary verb must precede the subject (question order).
❌ Rarely he visits us, and never I have seen such beauty.
✓ Rarely does he visit us, and never have I seen such beauty.
Why: Fronting the negative adverb demands auxiliary inversion: 'does he visit' and 'have I seen'.
Advanced Agreement

Correlative Conjunction Proximity (Neither...nor / Either...or)

When subjects are connected by either...or, neither...nor, or not only...but also, the verb agrees in person and number with the subject closer to the verb.
❌ Neither the teacher nor the students was present.
✓ Neither the teacher nor the students were present.
Why: The noun closest to the verb is the plural 'students', so the verb must be 'were'.
Subjunctive Mood

Mandative Subjunctive (Base Form)

Clauses following verbs of demand, recommendation, or necessity (insist, recommend, demand, essential that, imperative that) require the bare base verb (no -s and no was/is).
❌ I recommend that he is present and that she submits the form.
✓ I recommend that he be present and that she submit the form.
Why: The mandative subjunctive uses uninflected base forms (be, submit).
Mixed Conditionals

Time Relationship in Mixed Conditions

When a past hypothetical action influences a present result (Past Condition → Present Result), pair had + V3 in the if-clause with would + base verb in the main clause.
❌ If I took the medication yesterday, I would have been fine today.
✓ If I had taken the medication yesterday, I would be fine today.
Why: The condition occurred in the past (had taken), but the outcome is present today (would be).
Reduced Clauses

Perfect Participle Subject Consistency

In reduced clauses (Having finished..., When completed...), the implied subject of the participle must be the exact grammatical subject of the main clause.
❌ Having completed the test, the bell rang.
✓ Having completed the test, the student left the hall.
Why: The bell did not complete the test; the student did.
Conciseness

Pleonasm & Tautology (Unnecessary Repetition)

Avoid pairing words that repeat the same semantic meaning: return back, repeat again, completely finished, the reason is because.
❌ Please repeat it again and return back the document.
✓ Please repeat it and return the document.
Why: 'Repeat' inherently means to say again; 'return' inherently means to give back.
Collocations

Natural Verb-Noun Collocations

Use standard English pairings: make a decision (not do), do homework/research (not make), pay attention, make progress, take an exam.
❌ make homework ✓ do homework
❌ do a mistake ✓ make a mistake
❌ give an exam ✓ take an exam
❌ cope up with ✓ cope with
❌ He did a decision to cope up with the pressure.
✓ He made a decision to cope with the pressure.
Why: English uses 'make' with decisions, and the idiom is 'cope with' (no 'up').
Noun Clauses

Embedded Question Word Order

When a question is embedded inside a larger statement (I wonder where..., Could you tell me what...), use standard statement word order (Subject + Verb), never inverted question order.
❌ I do not know where is the manager and what did he say.
✓ I do not know where the manager is and what he said.
Why: The noun clause acts as an object and must follow Subject-Verb order.
