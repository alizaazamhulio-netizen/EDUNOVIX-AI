Fundamental Concept

1. Coordination & Control

Coordination is the process by which different parts of the body work together harmoniously to produce a suitable, coordinated response to internal and external stimuli.

Human Body Coordination Mechanisms:
Nervous Coordination: Rapid electrical signals via neurons, highly specific, short-duration effect.
Chemical Coordination: Hormones via bloodstream, broader distribution, generally slower but longer-lasting.

2. Receptors & Sensory Detection

A receptor is a specialized cell, nerve ending, or sensory organ structure designed to detect a specific type of stimulus and transduce it into an electrical nerve signal.

Interactive Receptor Explorer

Click any receptor below to reveal its specific stimulus, location, and MDCAT high-yield examples:

Active

Photoreceptors

Detects: Light energy

Example: The retina of the eye contains Rods (dim light/black & white) and Cones (bright light/color vision).

Active

Mechanoreceptors

Detects: Mechanical stimuli, pressure, touch, sound, balance

Example: Pacinian corpuscles & Meissner's corpuscles in skin; hair cells in cochlea (hearing) and semicircular canals (balance).

Active

Chemoreceptors

Detects: Chemical substances, pH, blood CO₂/O₂

Example: Taste buds on tongue, olfactory receptors in nasal cavity, carotid & aortic bodies sensing blood pH/gas concentrations.

Active

Thermoreceptors

Detects: Temperature variations (Cold & Warmth)

Example: Free nerve endings in the skin and central thermoreceptors located in the Hypothalamus for core body temperature.

Active

Pain Receptors

Detects: Nociceptive & tissue-damaging stimuli

Example: Nociceptors (unspecialized naked nerve endings) widely distributed in skin, joints, and organs that warn of mechanical, thermal, or chemical harm.

👁️ Visual Guide: Photoreceptors in Action Retina of the Human Eye

MDCAT Exam Note: Retina & Photoreceptors

The retina is the sensory inner coat of the eye. Photoreceptor cells (Rods & Cones) absorb light photons, generating electrochemical impulses routed via the optic nerve to the cerebrum (occipital lobe) for image processing.

⭐ Key Fact: Rods = Dim light/Scotopic vision; Cones = Color/Photopic vision.

Structural Unit & Classifications

3. The Neuron & Types of Neurons

A neuron (nerve cell) is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Highly specialized for excitability and conductivity, it generates and carries electrochemical impulses.

🧠 Structural Anatomy Interactive Labeled Neuron Diagram

Dendrites

Branched projections of the neuron that receive incoming chemical/electrical signals from receptors or preceding neurons and conduct them towards the cell body.

Core Parts of a Neuron Breakdown

01

Dendrites

Short, highly branched cytoplasmic extensions that receive signals/stimuli and conduct electrical impulses towards the cell body.

02

Cell Body (Soma)

Contains the nucleus, Nissl's granules, mitochondria, and cytoplasm; maintains cell life, synthesizes neurotransmitters.

03

Axon

A long, single cylinder-like fiber that carries nerve impulses away from the cell body toward target effectors or other neurons.

04

Myelin Sheath

Lipid-rich insulating sheath produced by Schwann cells/Oligodendrocytes. Prevents charge loss and dramatically increases impulse speed via saltatory conduction.

05

Axon Terminals

End branches with synaptic knobs containing neurotransmitter vesicles; transmits signals across synapses to next neurons, muscles, or glands.

"SAME DAVE" Rule: Sensory = Afferent, Motor = Efferent | Dorsal = Afferent, Ventral = Efferent.

Electrochemical Transmission

5. Nerve Impulse & Action Potential

A nerve impulse is an electrical signal (wave of electrochemical changes) that propagates rapidly along the axon membrane of a neuron due to localized ion flow across gated channels.

Phase 1 -70 mV

Resting Potential

Neuron membrane is polarized. Active Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump maintains high Na⁺ outside and high K⁺ inside (3 Na⁺ pumped out for every 2 K⁺ in). Inside is negatively charged relative to outside.

Polarized State
Phase 2 +30 to +40 mV

Depolarization (Action Potential)

Threshold stimulus opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Rapid influx of sodium ions reverses membrane polarity (inside becomes electropositive).

Na⁺ Rapid Influx
Phase 3 Falling to -70 mV

Repolarization

Na⁺ channels close; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open. Efflux of potassium ions restores the negative interior charge of the resting state.

K⁺ Rapid Efflux
MDCAT High-Yield Cycle:
Resting state (-70mV) → Depolarization (+35mV) → Repolarization → Resting state restored

6. The Synapse & Neurotransmitters

A synapse is the microscopic physiological junction between two successive neurons or between a neuron and an effector cell (muscle/gland). The minute fluid-filled gap is called the synaptic cleft (~20 nm).

🔬 Chemical Synapse Transmission Step-by-step Neurotransmitter Diffusion
1
Electrical impulse arrives at pre-synaptic axon terminal knob.
2
Calcium (Ca²⁺) influx causes vesicles to fuse with pre-synaptic membrane.
3
Neurotransmitter release: Acetylcholine (ACh) diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
4
Receptor binding on post-synaptic membrane opens Na⁺ channels, triggering an action potential in next cell.
⭐ Primary MDCAT Neurotransmitter: Acetylcholine (broken down by enzyme Acetylcholinesterase to prevent continuous stimulation).

7. Reflex Action & 8. Reflex Arc

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, and involuntary response to a specific stimulus without requiring prior conscious decision by the brain.

🔥 Classic Example

Accidental touch of a red-hot object: The hand is pulled back immediately before you even consciously perceive the sensation of pain. This rapid mechanism protects bodily tissues from severe burns.

Hormonal Control

11. Chemical Coordination & Master Comparison

Chemical coordination is carried out primarily by the Endocrine System consisting of ductless glands that secrete chemical messengers called hormones directly into the bloodstream.

🧪

What is a Hormone?

A hormone is a specific chemical messenger synthesized by an endocrine gland, secreted directly into blood circulation, and transported to distant target cells where it alters metabolic or physiological activity.

⚡ Nervous vs. Chemical Coordination

⚡

Nervous Coordination

  • Nature of Signal: Electrical impulses + Neurotransmitters
  • Speed: Extremely fast (Milliseconds to seconds)
  • Duration: Short-lasting (Immediate cessation)
  • Transmission Route: Specific nerve fibers / Axons
  • Target Specificity: Highly localized (Specific muscle/gland)
🧪

Chemical Coordination

  • Nature of Signal: Hormones (Peptides, Steroids, Amines)
  • Speed: Generally slower (Minutes, hours, or days)
  • Duration: Often longer-lasting & sustained
  • Transmission Route: Bloodstream / Circulatory System
  • Target Specificity: Widespread or selective (Target receptors)

Homeostatic Mechanism

20. Negative Feedback Control

Negative feedback is a corrective homeostatic mechanism whereby an increase in the output of a system results in a decrease in the initial stimulus, maintaining physiological equilibrium within narrow limits.

Example Feedback Loop: Thyroid Regulation
1
Low Blood Thyroxine triggers Hypothalamus (TRH) & Anterior Pituitary (TSH).
↓
2
TSH stimulates Thyroid to synthesize and secrete more Thyroxine into blood.
↓
3
High Thyroxine levels inhibit TRH and TSH secretion (Negative Feedback loop closed).

22. ⚡ Quick Revision Q&A

Click on any question below to reveal the precise MDCAT-ready answer and verify your concept retention:

A neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system, specialized for generating, conducting, and transmitting electrical nerve impulses.

The cerebellum controls body balance, posture, equilibrium, and the precise coordination of voluntary muscular activities.

A reflex arc is the nerve pathway followed by an impulse during a reflex action: Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory Neuron → Relay Neuron → Motor Neuron → Effector → Response.

Insulin (secreted by beta cells of the pancreas) decreases blood glucose levels by promoting cellular glucose uptake, glycogenesis (glycogen storage in liver and muscles), and lipogenesis.

Glucagon (secreted by alpha cells of the pancreas) increases blood glucose levels by stimulating glycogenolysis (breakdown of glycogen to glucose) and gluconeogenesis in the liver.

Thyroxine (T4 and T3), produced by the thyroid gland, requires mineral iodine for its chemical synthesis. Deficiency causes enlargement of the thyroid (simple goiter).

PTH increases blood calcium (Ca²⁺) levels by stimulating bone demineralization (osteoclasts), increasing renal calcium reabsorption, and activating vitamin D for enhanced intestinal absorption.

Adrenaline (Epinephrine), secreted by the adrenal medulla during acute physical or emotional stress, prepares the body for fighting or fleeing by increasing heart rate, ventilation, and circulating glucose.

Melatonin, secreted by the pineal gland in response to darkness, regulates circadian biological rhythms and the daily sleep-wake cycle.

1. Sensory (Afferent) neurons: Receptors → CNS.
2. Relay (Interneurons): Within CNS connecting neurons.
3. Motor (Efferent) neurons: CNS → Effectors (muscles/glands).
