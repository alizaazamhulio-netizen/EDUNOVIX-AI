01
Foundation • Nominal Class

Noun (Naming Word)

A Noun is a fundamental part of speech that names a person, place, thing, animal, idea, quality, feeling, or event. Nouns form the subject and object framework of virtually every English sentence.

Person: teacher, doctor, student Place: school, Pakistan, laboratory Thing: book, computer, table Animal: horse, eagle, cat Idea: freedom, knowledge, justice Quality: honesty, bravery, patience

7 Major Types of Nouns

1. Proper Noun Capitalized

Specific, unique name of an individual person, place, or entity.

Ali, Pakistan, Monday, Microsoft
2. Common Noun General

General, non-specific name for a class of person, place, or item.

boy, country, university, teacher
3. Concrete Noun 5 Senses

Something physical that can be perceived via sight, touch, smell, hearing, or taste.

book, apple, chair, music
4. Abstract Noun Intangible

Concepts, states, emotions, philosophical ideas, or moral qualities.

honesty, courage, happiness, knowledge
5. Collective Noun Group

A single word referring to a collection or group of individuals or items.

team, family, committee, audience
6. Countable Noun Individual

Items that can be counted individually and have distinct plural forms.

one book → two books, student → students
7. Uncountable Noun Mass

Treated as a unified mass rather than individual countable units.

information, advice, furniture, knowledge

Crucial Rule: Uncountable Noun Traps

Uncountable nouns cannot take plural suffixes (-s / -es) or indefinite articles (a/an) directly:

❌ She gave me many informations. ✅ She gave me much information. (or pieces of advice)
❌ I bought new furnitures. ✅ I bought new furniture.

02
Foundation • Reference Class

Pronoun (Replacement Word)

A Pronoun replaces or refers to a noun or noun phrase. Its primary purpose is to keep communication elegant, concise, and free of redundant repetition.

Repetitive & Clunky:

"Sara went to Sara's room because Sara wanted Sara's book."

Elegant with Pronouns:

"Sara went to her room because she wanted her book."

Personal Pronoun Transformation Matrix

Person / Number Subject Object Possessive Adj Possessive Pronoun Reflexive
1st Person Sing. I me my mine myself
2nd Person Sing./Plural you you your yours yourself / yourselves
3rd Person Masc. he him his his himself
3rd Person Fem. she her her hers herself
1st Person Plural we us our ours ourselves
3rd Person Plural they them their theirs themselves

Other Pronoun Types

Demonstrative Pointing

Points directly to specific things near or far in space/time.

this, that, these, those ("This is interesting.")
Interrogative Questioning

Used to introduce inquiries and questions.

who, whom, whose, which, what ("Who called you?")
Relative Connecting

Relates a subordinate clause to a previous noun or antecedent.

who, whom, whose, which, that ("The student who won...")
Indefinite Non-specific

Refers to people or things in a general, unspecified manner.

someone, anyone, everyone, nobody, many ("Everyone enjoyed it.")
03
Foundation • Predicate Engine

Verb (Action & State Word)

A Verb is the heart and engine of every sentence. It expresses an action (physical or mental), an occurrence, or a state/condition of being. Without a finite verb, a complete sentence cannot exist.

Action Verbs (Dynamic)

Physical movement or observable activity:

run write study jump

Example: "She studies linguistics every day."

State Verbs (Stative)

Condition, perception, knowledge, or possession:

know believe understand seem

Example: "He knows the theoretical answer."

Core Verb Classifications

Main Verb Primary Meaning

Carries the core lexical meaning of the predicate.

She writes exceptionally well.
Helping (Auxiliary) Tense/Aspect

Works with main verbs to form complex tenses, voices, or questions.

be (is/are), have (has/had), do (does/did)
Modal Auxiliaries Mood & Capability

Expresses obligation, capability, probability, or permission.

can, could, may, might, must, should, will, would
Transitive vs Intransitive Object Dependency

Transitive requires a direct object; Intransitive does not.

Transitive: "She opened [the door]." / Intransitive: "The baby slept."

04
Intermediate • Modifier Class

Adjective (Describing Word)

An Adjective modifies, qualifies, or describes a noun or pronoun. It provides essential descriptive clarity answering questions like: What kind? Which one? How many? How much?

What kind?

a beautiful garden, an interesting novel

Which one?

this question, that auditorium

How many?

three students, several candidates

How much?

sufficient time, much patience

The 3 Degrees of Comparison

Positive Degree
Basic quality of one item without direct comparison.
tall | intelligent | good
Comparative Degree
Compares two entities (often with -er or more).
taller | more intelligent | better
Superlative Degree
Compares three or more entities (with -est or most).
tallest | most intelligent | best
05
Intermediate • Modifier Class

Adverb (Modifying Word)

An Adverb modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or even an entire clause. It answers fundamental questions such as: How? When? Where? How often? To what extent?

Types of Adverbs

Adverb of Manner How?

Describes the manner in which an action happens.

carefully, slowly, quickly ("She solved it swiftly.")
Adverb of Time When?

Specifies the timing of an event.

today, yesterday, soon, later ("He will arrive soon.")
Adverb of Place Where?

Indicates the physical or relative location of the occurrence.

here, there, everywhere, inside ("She looked everywhere.")
Adverb of Frequency How Often?

States how frequently or regularly something occurs.

always, often, sometimes, rarely, never ("They always revise.")
Adverb of Degree To What Extent?

Modifies intensity of adjectives, verbs, or other adverbs.

very, extremely, quite, almost ("He is extremely diligent.")
Sentence Adverb Full Clause

Comments on the attitude, certainty, or truth of the entire sentence.

Fortunately, obviously ("Fortunately, nobody was injured.")

07
Advanced • Connector Class

Conjunction (Connecting Word)

A Conjunction acts as a bridge that links words, phrases, independent clauses, or subordinate clauses together into unified syntactic structures.

1. Coordinating Conjunctions: The FANBOYS System

Connects words, phrases, or independent clauses of equal grammatical rank.

F
For
Shows reason ("He studied, for he loved learning.")
A
And
Adds information ("She sings and plays piano.")
N
Nor
Negative addition ("He doesn't smoke, nor drinks.")
B
But
Shows contrast ("He was tired, but he worked.")
O
Or
Shows alternative ("Study now or revise later.")
Y
Yet
Unexpected contrast ("It was cold, yet they swam.")
S
So
Shows consequence ("It rained, so we stayed in.")

Subordinating Conjunctions

Introduces a dependent (subordinate) clause and connects it to a main clause:

because although while since unless if when before after
"Although it was raining, they continued the match."

Correlative Conjunctions

Paired conjunctions that work in tandem to balance grammatical structures:

either ... or
neither ... nor
both ... and
not only ... but also
"He is not only diligent but also creative."
08
Advanced • Emotive Class

Interjection (Expressive Word)

An Interjection is an expressive exclamation that communicates a sudden burst of human emotion, reaction, surprise, pain, or enthusiasm. It is grammatically independent from the rest of the sentence.

Wonder / Awe
"Wow! That was an impressive performance."
Realization
"Oh! I forgot my notebook at home."
Pain
"Ouch! That stepped right on my toe."
Attention
"Hey! Check out this sentence structure."
Celebration
"Bravo! You mastered all eight parts of speech."

Context & Formality Rule

Interjections are natural and powerful in expressive narrative dialogue, creative literature, and conversational speech, but should be used sparingly in formal academic essays or technical research papers.

ADVANCED SYNTACTIC PRINCIPLE

One Word, Different Parts of Speech

A word's category is NEVER determined by its spelling alone, but by the functional job it performs in the active sentence.

Word Form vs. Syntactic Function

Deeper Look

Don't get tricked by suffixes like -ing. Examine what the word is actually doing:

Functioning as a NOUN (Subject/Gerund)

"Swimming is good cardiovascular exercise."

Here, "swimming" acts as the nominal subject answering what is good exercise.

Functioning as a VERB (Participle)

"She is swimming in the regional tournament."

Here, "swimming" is the main action verb combined with the auxiliary "is".

TRICKY PAIRS RESOLVED

Don't Confuse These

Clear explanations of the most frequently confused grammatical pairs and homophones in English.

Pair 01

Good vs. Well

Good is an adjective modifying nouns. Well is an adverb modifying verbs (or an adjective meaning healthy).

Adj She is a good student. (describes student)
Adv She performed well. (describes performed)
Pair 02

Adjective vs. Adverb

Adjectives describe people/things. Adverbs describe how, when, where actions occur.

Adj She is a careful driver.
Adv She drives carefully.
Pair 03

Noun vs. Verb

The same spelling can refer to an action or the object/concept itself.

Verb I answer the question. (Action)
Noun I know the answer. (Thing/Concept)
Pair 04

Its vs. It's

Its is a possessive determiner. It's is a contraction of it is or it has.

Possessive The cat licked its paw.
Contraction It's raining heavily today.
Pair 05

Then vs. Than

Then is an adverb relating to time/sequence. Than is a conjunction/preposition of comparison.

Time We studied, then we rested.
Comparison She is faster than him.
Pair 06

Their vs. They're vs. There

Their (Possession), They're (They are), There (Place/Pronoun).

Possessive That is their house.
Contraction They're arriving soon.
Place Look over there.
