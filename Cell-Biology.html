Section 01 Foundations

Cell: Basic Unit of Life

Definition: A cell is the basic structural, functional, and biological unit of all known living organisms. The activities and metabolism of any complex organism represent the sum of the coordinated activities of its constituent cells.

Classical Cell Theory

The formulation of the cell theory is one of the pillars of modern biology, establishing three fundamental tenets:

  • All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the basic structural and physiological unit of life.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells through cellular division (Omnis cellula e cellula).

Pioneering Scientists & Historical Discoveries

Robert Hooke (1665)

First observed dead cork cells under a compound microscope and coined the word "cell" (derived from Latin cellula meaning little rooms).

Hooke → Term Cell
Matthias Schleiden (1838)

German botanist who concluded that all plant tissues are composed of individualized cells and cell aggregates.

Schleiden → Plants
Theodor Schwann (1839)

German zoologist who concluded that all animal tissues consist of cells, uniting botany and zoology under cell theory.

Schwann → Animals
Rudolf Virchow (1855)

Formulated the third tenet: emphasized that new cells arise exclusively from pre-existing cells by cell division.

Virchow → Pre-existing
★
MDCAT Rapid Recall Formula

Hooke → Cell terminology | Schleiden → Plant cells | Schwann → Animal cells | Virchow → Pre-existing cells

Section 02 Cellular Classification

Types of Cells: Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

Living organisms are divided into two fundamental architectural patterns based on internal compartmentalization:

Prokaryotic Cells

Examples: Bacteria, Cyanobacteria, Archaea.

  • No membrane-bound true nucleus.
  • Genetic material is a single circular DNA lying in an irregular nucleoid region.
  • Completely lack membrane-bound organelles (no ER, Golgi, mitochondria, or lysosomes).
  • Contain smaller 70S ribosomes (50S + 30S subunits).
  • Cell wall is usually present (composed of peptidoglycan/murein in true bacteria).
  • Typically smaller in diameter (0.5 – 5.0 µm).

Eukaryotic Cells

Examples: Animals, Plants, Fungi, Protists.

  • True nucleus surrounded by a double-membraned nuclear envelope with nuclear pores.
  • Linear DNA organized with histone proteins into chromosomes.
  • Abundant membrane-bound specialized organelles.
  • Contain larger 80S ribosomes in cytoplasm and 70S inside mitochondria/chloroplasts.
  • Cell wall present in plants (cellulose) and fungi (chitin); absent in animals.
  • Typically larger and compartmentalized (10 – 100 µm).

Comprehensive MDCAT Comparison Table

Feature Prokaryotic Cell Eukaryotic Cell
True Nucleus ✗ Absent (No envelope) ✓ Present (Double envelope)
Membrane-Bound Organelles ✗ Absent ✓ Present (ER, Golgi, Mito, etc.)
DNA Architecture & Location Circular, naked (no histones), in nucleoid Linear, complexed with histones, inside nucleus
Ribosome Size 70S (30S + 50S) 80S Cytoplasmic (40S + 60S)
Cell Division Binary fission (No spindle apparatus) Mitosis and Meiosis (Spindle fibers form)
Typical Size 0.5 – 5 µm (Simpler) 10 – 100 µm (Complex)
⚠
Examiner Trap: Prokaryotic Organelles & DNA

Prokaryotes DO NOT lack DNA or Ribosomes! Examiners often phrase questions to trick students into thinking prokaryotes have no ribosomes or genetic code. Prokaryotes have both DNA (in the nucleoid) and functional 70S ribosomes for protein synthesis.

Section 03 Comparative Anatomy

Plant Cell vs Animal Cell

Both plant and animal cells are eukaryotic and share fundamental metabolic machinery:

✓
Universal Shared Eukaryotic Structures

Both contain: Plasma membrane, Cytoplasm, Ribosomes, Nucleus, Mitochondria, Endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus.

Key Distinctive Features

Structural Feature Plant Cell Animal Cell
Cell Wall ✓ Present (Cellulose, outer to membrane) ✗ Completely Absent
Chloroplasts / Plastids ✓ Present (Photosynthetic machinery) ✗ Absent
Vacuolar System One large central vacuole (maintains turgor) Multiple small, temporary vacuoles
Centrosomes & Centrioles ✗ Absent in higher plants ✓ Present (Near nucleus, 9 triplets)
Cytokinesis Mode Cell plate formation (Phragmoplast) Cleavage furrow (Microfilament constriction)
⚠
Critical Distinction: Cell Wall ≠ Cell Membrane

The cell wall is an outer, non-living, rigid, and completely permeable layer giving mechanical shape. The cell membrane (plasma membrane) is living, dynamic, and selectively permeable. Plant cells possess BOTH.

Section 04 Membrane Physiology

Plasma Membrane & Fluid Mosaic Model

The plasma membrane encapsulates the living protoplasm, functioning as a selectively permeable barrier that strictly regulates ionic and molecular flux.

The Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972)

This model describes the cell membrane as a two-dimensional liquid in which phospholipid and protein molecules diffuse easily:

  • Phospholipid Bilayer: Amphipathic lipids arranged with polar hydrophilic heads (phosphate group, glycerol) pointing outward to the aqueous environment, and nonpolar hydrophobic tails (fatty acid chains) pointing inward to avoid water.
  • Integral (Transmembrane) Proteins: Firmly embedded in the bilayer, acting as transport channels, carriers, and signal transducers.
  • Peripheral Proteins: Located on the inner or outer surfaces, frequently serving enzymatic and cytoskeletal anchoring functions.
  • Cholesterol (in Animals): Intercalated between phospholipids; functions as a fluidity buffer (prevents membrane from becoming too fluid at high temperatures and prevents crystallization at low temperatures).
  • Carbohydrates (Glycolipids & Glycoproteins): Project from the external surface, forming the glycocalyx for cell recognition and immune identification.
★
Hydrophilic vs Hydrophobic Rule

Hydrophilic: Water-loving / Polar / Phosphate Head • Hydrophobic: Water-fearing / Nonpolar / Hydrocarbon Fatty Acid Tails

Section 05 Physiology & Kinetics

Transport Across the Plasma Membrane

Membrane transport is divided into energy-independent (Passive) and energy-dependent (Active) pathways:

1. Passive Transport (No ATP)

Molecules move down their concentration gradient (High → Low):

  • Simple Diffusion: Non-polar, uncharged small molecules pass directly through lipid bilayer (e.g., O2, CO2, fatty acids).
  • Facilitated Diffusion: Charged ions or polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) move down gradient via channel or carrier proteins without ATP.
  • Osmosis: Net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane toward higher effective solute concentration.

2. Active Transport (Requires ATP)

Molecules move against their concentration gradient (Low → High):

  • Requires cellular energy in the form of ATP.
  • Utilizes specialized transmembrane carrier pumps.
  • Na+/K+ ATPase Pump: Pumps 3 Na+ OUT and 2 K+ IN per hydrolyzed ATP molecule.
  • Maintains resting membrane potential in nerve and muscle cells.

Bulk Transport: Endocytosis & Exocytosis

Endocytosis (Material IN)

Cell invaginates its membrane to engulf bulk materials into vesicles:

  • Phagocytosis: "Cell eating" — uptake of solid particles (e.g., macrophage engulfing bacteria).
  • Pinocytosis: "Cell drinking" — non-specific uptake of extracellular fluids.
  • Receptor-Mediated: Highly specific uptake involving clathrin-coated pits (e.g., LDL cholesterol).

Exocytosis (Material OUT)

Intracellular secretory vesicles migrate to, fuse with plasma membrane, and discharge contents:

  • Secretion of hormones (e.g., Insulin from beta cells).
  • Release of digestive enzymes by acinar cells.
  • Release of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.
★
Golden MDCAT Memory Rule: Na+/K+ Pump Stoichiometry

3 Na+ OUT • 2 K+ IN • 1 ATP CONSUMED — This electrogenic ratio creates net negative charge inside the resting cell.

Section 06 Genomics & Control

The Nucleus: Master Control Center

Discovered by Robert Brown (1831), the nucleus contains the vast majority of the eukaryotic genome and directs all cellular metabolic activities.

  • Nuclear Envelope: Double-membrane boundary. Outer membrane is continuous with the Rough ER. Perinuclear space separates both membranes.
  • Nuclear Pores: Multiprotein complexes (nuclear pore complexes) that regulate selective nucleocytoplasmic exchange (mRNA, ribosomal subunits out; polymerases, histones in).
  • Nucleolus: Dense, non-membrane-bound subnuclear structure responsible for the transcription of rRNA and assembly of ribosomal subunits.
  • Chromatin: Thread-like relaxed network of double-stranded DNA wrapped around octamers of basic histone proteins (forming nucleosomes). Condenses into visible chromosomes during cell division.
Section 07 Protein Machinery

Ribosomes: Protein Synthesis Factories

Ribosomes are granular ribonucleoprotein particles composed of rRNA (ribosomal RNA) and proteins. They are non-membrane-bound structures present in all cellular life.

Free Ribosomes

Suspended in the cytosol; synthesize water-soluble proteins that function within the cytoplasm (e.g., glycolytic enzymes, actin, tubulin).

Bound Ribosomes

Attached to the cytosolic face of the Rough ER; synthesize proteins destined for insertion into membranes, lysosomal packaging, or extracellular secretion.

★
MDCAT Rule

Ribosome → Universal Site of Protein Synthesis / Translation

Section 08 Endomembrane System

Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER & SER)

An extensive network of interconnected tubular cisternae and vesicles extending from the nuclear envelope throughout the cytoplasm.

Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER)

  • Studded with 80S ribosomes on outer surface.
  • Primary site for protein synthesis, folding, and initial glycosylation.
  • Packages nascent polypeptides into transport vesicles for Golgi processing.

Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)

  • Devoid of attached ribosomes.
  • Lipid and steroid synthesis (phospholipids, cholesterol, steroid hormones).
  • Detoxification of drugs and poisons (prominent in hepatocytes).
  • Calcium ion storage (sarcoplasmic reticulum in muscle fibers).
💡
Memory Trick

Rough = Ribosomes (Proteins) • Smooth = Steroids & Soft Lipids (No Ribosomes)

Section 09 Endomembrane System

Golgi Apparatus (Golgi Complex)

Discovered by Camillo Golgi (1898), consisting of stacks of flattened membrane-bound sacs called cisternae. Operates with strict polarity:

  • Cis Face (Forming Face): Convex surface facing ER, receives transport vesicles carrying immature proteins.
  • Medial Cisternae: Biochemical modification zone (glycosylation, phosphorylation, sulfation).
  • Trans Face (Maturing Face): Concave surface facing plasma membrane; sorts and buds off secretory vesicles and primary lysosomes.
⚠
Examiner Trap: Golgi Does NOT Make Ribosomes

The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts, and packages macromolecules; it is NOT the site of primary protein synthesis. Synthesis occurs on ribosomes.

Section 10 Cellular Digestion

Lysosomes: Intracellular Digestion

Single-membrane vesicles discovered by Christian de Duve (1949) containing over 40 types of acid hydrolases (proteases, lipases, nucleases) requiring an acidic lumen (pH ~5.0 maintained by H+ ATPase pumps).

  • Heterophagy: Digestion of foreign food particles or phagocytosed pathogens.
  • Autophagy: Degradation and recycling of damaged cellular organelles (cellular self-renewal).
  • Autolysis: Complete self-destruction of aging/damaged cells by synchronous rupture of lysosomes ("suicide bags").
Section 11 Bioenergetics

Mitochondria: Powerhouses of the Cell

Mitochondria are double-membraned organelles that serve as the primary site of aerobic cellular respiration and ATP generation through oxidative phosphorylation.

Membrane Compartments

  • Outer Membrane: Smooth, containing porin proteins permeable to small metabolites.
  • Intermembrane Space: High H+ proton concentration zone driving ATP synthase.
  • Inner Membrane: Thrown into deep convolutions called cristae to maximize surface area for Electron Transport Chain (ETC) complexes and ATP synthase F0-F1 complexes.
  • Mitochondrial Matrix: Contains Krebs cycle enzymes, circular mtDNA, and 70S ribosomes.

Semi-Autonomous Organelle

Mitochondria reproduce independently by binary fission and synthesize some of their own proteins because they possess:

  • Their own circular double-stranded DNA (mtDNA).
  • Their own 70S ribosomes and tRNAs.
  • Originated via endosymbiosis of an aerobic proteobacterium.
★
MDCAT Focus

Cristae = Inner membrane folds • Matrix = Krebs cycle • ATP Synthase = Inner membrane

Section 12 Photosynthesis

Chloroplasts & Plastids

Double-membraned photosynthetic plastids found in green plant cells and algae. Key architectural zones:

  • Thylakoids: Flattened disc-shaped sacs containing chlorophyll pigments, Photosystems I & II, and electron carriers for light-dependent reactions.
  • Granum (plural: Grana): Dense vertical stacks of thylakoids interconnected by stroma lamellae.
  • Stroma: Fluid-filled ground matrix containing Calvin cycle enzymes (Rubisco), circular DNA, and 70S ribosomes for light-independent (dark) reactions.
Section 13 Osmoregulation

Vacuoles & Osmotic Balance

Membrane-bound fluid compartments. In mature plant cells, the large central vacuole occupies up to 90% of cellular volume.

  • Tonoplast: The semi-permeable limiting membrane surrounding the central vacuole.
  • Cell Sap: Stored water, inorganic ions, amino acids, and pigments (e.g., anthocyanins).
  • Turgor Pressure: Generates outward hydrostatic pressure against the cell wall, providing mechanical stiffness to non-woody plant stems and leaves.
Section 14 Structural Biology

Plant Cell Wall Architecture

A non-living extracellular protective envelope secreted external to the plasma membrane in plants, fungi, and bacteria:

  • Middle Lamella: Outermost cementing layer between adjacent plant cells, composed of sticky calcium and magnesium pectates.
  • Primary Cell Wall: True wall formed in developing cells; composed of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin. Thin, flexible, extensible.
  • Secondary Cell Wall: Deposited inside the primary wall in non-growing sclerenchyma and xylem cells; heavily reinforced with lignin for compressive strength.
Section 15 Structural Framework

Cytoskeleton: Filaments & Tubules

A dynamic three-dimensional polymeric meshwork maintaining cell morphology and driving motility:

Filament Type Protein Subunit Diameter Primary Biological Role
Microfilaments Actin (G-actin polymers) ~7 nm (Thinnest) Muscle contraction, amoeboid movement, cleavage furrow during cytokinesis.
Intermediate Filaments Keratin, Vimentin, Neurofilaments 8 – 12 nm High tensile mechanical strength, stabilizing nuclear lamina and cell junctions.
Microtubules α and β Tubulin dimers ~25 nm (Hollow cylinders) Mitotic spindle fibers, intracellular organelle transport, 9+2 axoneme of cilia/flagella.
Section 16 Microtubule Organization

Centrosome & Centrioles

The centrosome is the principal Microtubule Organizing Center (MTOC) of animal cells.

  • Contains a pair of cylindrical structures called centrioles situated perpendicular (90°) to each other.
  • Each centriole consists of 9 triplets of microtubules arranged in a cylindrical circle (9+0 pattern) without central tubules.
  • Duplicates during the S phase of interphase and migrates to opposite cell poles to organize the mitotic spindle during mitosis.
Section 17 Genetics & Structure

Chromosomes & Genomic Organization

Chromatin fibers condense into distinct chromosomes during early cell division.

  • Centromere (Primary Constriction): The specialized constricted region holding two sister chromatids together.
  • Kinetochores: Disc-shaped protein complexes assembled on centromeric DNA where spindle microtubules attach.
  • Sister Chromatids: Two identical duplicated copies of a chromosome joined at the centromere, separated during mitotic anaphase.
  • Telomeres: Repetitive non-coding nucleotide sequences capping chromosome ends to prevent degradation.
Section 18 Cell Cycle Kinetics

The Cell Cycle & Interphase

The ordered sequence of events from one cell division to the next, consisting of Interphase (~90% duration) and M Phase (Division):

G1
Gap 1 Phase

Rapid metabolic growth, organelle duplication, synthesis of RNA and replication enzymes.

S
Synthesis (S) Phase

DNA Replication (2C → 4C DNA content; chromosome number remains 2n) + Centrosome duplication.

G2
Gap 2 Phase

Final growth check, synthesis of tubulin proteins and ATP reserves for spindle assembly.

M
Mitotic Phase

Nuclear division (Karyokinesis) followed by cytoplasmic division (Cytokinesis).

★
Major MDCAT Trap: S Phase vs G1 Phase

DNA replication occurs strictly in the S Phase, NEVER in G1 or G2. In human cells (2n=46), chromosome number remains 46 while DNA amount doubles from 2C to 4C.

Section 19 Equational Division

Mitosis: Stages & Cytokinesis

Equational nuclear division maintaining diploid chromosome number (2n → 2n). Stages follow the sequence P → M → A → T:

P
Prophase

Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes; nucleolus disappears; mitotic spindle forms; nuclear envelope disintegrates.

M
Metaphase

Chromosomes align singly along the equatorial metaphase plate; kinetochores attach to spindle fibers.

A
Anaphase

Centromeres split; sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles as daughter chromosomes.

T
Telophase

Chromosomes decondense back into chromatin; nuclear envelopes reform around both sets; nucleoli reappear.

Cytokinesis: Cytoplasmic division. Animal cells form a contractile ring of actin microfilaments causing a cleavage furrow. Plant cells form a cell plate (phragmoplast) originating from Golgi vesicles.

Section 20 Reduction Division

Meiosis: Reduction & Genetic Diversity

Specialized two-step division (Meiosis I and II) reducing chromosome count from diploid (2n) to haploid (n), producing 4 genetically diverse gametes:

Meiosis I (Reductional Division)

  • Prophase I: Longest phase with 5 substages (Leptotene, Zygotene, Pachytene, Diplotene, Diakinesis). Synapsis of homologous chromosomes forms bivalents/tetrads.
  • Crossing Over: Physical exchange of genetic material between non-sister chromatids at chiasmata during Pachytene → primary source of genetic variation!
  • Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate toward opposite poles while sister chromatids remain attached at centromeres.

Meiosis II (Equational Division)

  • Resembles standard mitosis.
  • Occurs without intervening DNA replication (Interkinesis).
  • Anaphase II: Sister chromatids finally separate at the centromeres.
  • Result: 4 unique haploid (n) daughter cells.
Section 21 Master Summary

Mitosis vs Meiosis: High-Yield Comparison

Feature Mitosis Meiosis
Number of Divisions 1 nuclear division 2 successive nuclear divisions (I & II)
Daughter Cells Produced 2 daughter cells 4 daughter cells (Gametes/Spores)
Ploidy / Chromosome Count Conserved (2n → 2n) Halved (2n → n)
Homologous Synapsis ✗ Absent ✓ Present (Prophase I)
Crossing Over (Recombination) ✗ Absent ✓ Present (Pachytene of Prophase I)

⚠
The #1 MDCAT Anaphase Trap

Homologous chromosomes separate in Anaphase I • Sister chromatids separate in Anaphase of Mitosis AND Anaphase II of Meiosis.

Section 22 Intercellular Connections

Cell Junctions & Tissue Integrity

Animal Cell Junctions

  • Tight Junctions (Occluding): Form continuous leak-proof seals preventing paracellular fluid movement (e.g., intestinal epithelium).
  • Desmosomes (Anchoring): Mechanical rivet-like junctions bound to intermediate keratin filaments providing sheer resistance (e.g., skin, cardiac muscle).
  • Gap Junctions (Communicating): Transmembrane connexon protein channels allowing rapid direct transfer of ions and small signaling molecules (essential for intercalated discs in cardiac muscle syncytium).

Plant Cell Junctions

  • Plasmodesmata: Microscopic trans-wall cytoplasmic channels traversing adjacent plant cell walls.
  • Allows symplastic transport of water, nutrients, sugars, and RNA between cells without crossing plasma membranes.
Section 23 Signal Transduction

Cell Signaling & Communication

The universal triad of cellular communication:

1
Reception

Extracellular ligand (hormone, neurotransmitter) binds specifically to cell surface or intracellular receptor protein.

2
Transduction

Receptor activation triggers second messenger cascades (cAMP, Ca2+, phosphorylation cascades) amplifying the signal.

3
Response

Specific activation of cellular response: enzyme activation, gene transcription, or cytoskeletal rearrangement.

⚠ Examiner Traps

10 MDCAT Common Traps & Pitfalls

These recurring misconceptions are intentionally tested in PM&DC entrance papers. Study both the wrong instinct and the correct medical truth.

Trap 01 Organelle Classification
✗ Wrong: Assuming ribosomes are membrane-bound organelles.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound ribonucleoprotein complexes.
Ribosomes lack a lipid bilayer membrane, allowing them to exist in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
Trap 02 Prokaryotic Structure
✗ Wrong: Thinking prokaryotes lack DNA or ribosomes.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Prokaryotes have circular DNA in the nucleoid and 70S ribosomes.
They lack a nuclear envelope and membrane-bound organelles, not genetic code or ribosomes.
Trap 03 Plant Cell Envelope
✗ Wrong: Believing plant cells have only a cell wall and no cell membrane.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Plant cells have BOTH an outer cell wall and an inner plasma membrane.
The wall is permeable and structural; the plasma membrane performs selective permeability.
Trap 04 Mitochondrial Autonomy
✗ Wrong: Assuming mitochondria are fully independent cells inside us.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Mitochondria are semi-autonomous, requiring nuclear genes.
They encode some proteins, but most mitochondrial enzymes are imported from nuclear genes.
Trap 05 Golgi Function
✗ Wrong: Selecting Golgi as the primary organelle synthesizing proteins.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Golgi modifies, sorts, and packages proteins synthesized on ribosomes.
Ribosomes perform translation; Golgi performs post-translational glycosylation and packaging.
Trap 06 Cell Cycle Timing
✗ Wrong: Assuming DNA replication occurs during G1 or Prophase.
✓ MDCAT Fact: DNA replication strictly occurs in the S (Synthesis) Phase.
G1 prepares nucleotides and enzymes, but DNA synthesis is strictly confined to S phase.
Trap 07 Meiosis Recombination
✗ Wrong: Selecting Metaphase I as the stage for crossing over.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Crossing over occurs in Pachytene of Prophase I.
Metaphase I is alignment of bivalents; recombination has already concluded by Diakinesis.
Trap 08 Meiosis I Segregation
✗ Wrong: Stating that sister chromatids separate during Anaphase I.
✓ MDCAT Fact: In Anaphase I, homologous chromosomes separate.
Sister chromatids remain joined at their centromeres until Anaphase II and Mitotic Anaphase.
Trap 09 Meiosis II Segregation
✗ Wrong: Confusing Anaphase I and Anaphase II mechanisms.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Sister chromatids separate in Anaphase II.
Centromeres split for the first time during Meiosis in Anaphase II, pulling sister chromatids apart.
Trap 10 Permeability Physiology
✗ Wrong: Believing cell wall and cell membrane share identical permeability.
✓ MDCAT Fact: Cell wall is fully permeable; membrane is selectively permeable.
The wall allows almost all dissolved solutes through; the lipid bilayer strictly regulates entry.

Active Recall • Flashcard Self-Test

Test your active memory before attempting the MCQ examination.

1. What is the fundamental biological role of ribosomes?
Answer: Protein synthesis (translation of mRNA into polypeptide chains).
2. During which precise stage of the cell cycle is nuclear DNA replicated?
Answer: The S (Synthesis) phase of interphase.
3. Where and when does genetic crossing over occur during meiosis?
Answer: During the Pachytene substage of Prophase I of Meiosis I between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
4. What is the fundamental difference between passive and active transport?
Answer: Passive transport moves molecules down their concentration gradient without direct cellular ATP expenditure; Active transport requires ATP energy to pump molecules against their concentration gradient.
5. Which cellular organelle is responsible for post-translational modification, sorting, and packaging?
Answer: The Golgi apparatus (Golgi complex).

Master Synoptic Review

Cell Biology • Final Comprehensive Summary

The definitive syllabus distillation for your final revision hours.

1. Cellular Foundation

Cell Biology studies the organization and function of cells. Cells are categorized into prokaryotes (nucleoid, 70S ribosomes) and eukaryotes (true nucleus, 80S ribosomes, compartmentalized organelles).

2. Membrane & Transport

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer. Molecules pass via simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport (Na+/K+ pump), endocytosis, and exocytosis.

3. Genetic Control

The nucleus stores genomic DNA wrapped around histones as chromatin. The nucleolus assembles ribosomal subunits.

4. Protein & Lipid Synthesis

Ribosomes translate proteins. Rough ER folds proteins for transport; Smooth ER synthesizes lipids and detoxifies chemicals.

5. Processing & Recycling

The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts, and packages macromolecules. Lysosomes contain acidic hydrolases for digestion and autophagy.

6. Energy Transduction

Mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration and ATP generation on cristae. Chloroplasts drive photosynthetic carbon fixation in thylakoids and stroma.

7. Cytoskeleton & Cell Cycle

The cytoskeleton supports cell morphology. The cell cycle progresses through G1, S (DNA replication), G2, and M phase.

8. Mitosis vs Meiosis

Mitosis yields 2 identical diploid daughter cells. Meiosis involves 2 divisions producing 4 diverse haploid gametes with crossing over in Prophase I.
