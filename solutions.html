🚨 MDCAT TRAPS & HIGH-YIELD POINTS
Examine these 12 critical traps before test day — questions are frequently set on these exact distinctions!
⚠️ Trap 1: Molarity vs Molality Denominator
Molarity uses litres of SOLUTION.
Molality uses kilograms of SOLVENT.
Never mix up solution and solvent in calculations!
⚠️ Trap 2: Temperature Dependence
Molarity changes with temperature (because liquid volume expands or contracts with heat).
Molality is temperature independent (mass does not change with temperature).
⚠️ Trap 3: Gas Solubility vs Temperature
While solid solutes usually dissolve more at high temperature, gas solubility DECREASES with temperature.
Warm soda goes flat faster because dissolved CO₂ escapes!
⚠️ Trap 4: Gas Solubility vs Pressure (Henry's Law)
Gas solubility INCREASES as pressure increases.
When you open a carbonated drink bottle, pressure drops and gas bubbles out.
⚠️ Trap 5: Non-Volatile Solute & Vapor Pressure
Adding a non-volatile solute (e.g. glucose, NaCl) LOWERS the vapour pressure of the solvent because solute particles occupy surface spots.
⚠️ Trap 6: Boiling Point Elevation
Adding a non-volatile solute RAISES the boiling point.
Because vapour pressure is lowered, more thermal energy is needed to reach atmospheric pressure.
⚠️ Trap 7: Freezing Point Depression
Adding a solute LOWERS the freezing point.
Salt melts ice on cold roads because it lowers the freezing point of water below 0°C.
⚠️ Trap 8: Colligative Particle Count
Colligative properties depend on the number of dissolved particles, not their chemical nature.
1M NaCl produces 2 moles of particles (Na⁺ + Cl⁻), so it has double the colligative effect of 1M glucose!
⚠️ Trap 9: Osmosis Direction
In osmosis, SOLVENT molecules move through a semipermeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution ("Water moves toward more solute").
⚠️ Trap 10: Tyndall Effect
Tyndall effect is exhibited by Colloids and Suspensions, but NOT by true solutions (true solution particles are too small to scatter light).
⚠️ Trap 11: Settling on Standing
Suspension particles settle down under gravity when left undisturbed.
True solutions and colloids do NOT settle.
⚠️ Trap 12: Dilution Equation M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
During dilution, the number of moles of solute stays CONSTANT.
Only solvent volume increases, which decreases the molar concentration.

1. Solution

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.

It has the same composition and properties throughout the entire sample.

Example: Salt dissolved in water.
Salt = solute, Water = solvent
⭐
MDCAT Tip: A solution is strictly homogeneous at the molecular level.
2

2. Solute

The substance that is dissolved in a solution is called the solute.

It is generally the component present in lesser quantity.

Example: In salt water, salt is the solute.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: A solution can contain multiple solutes dissolved in a single solvent.
3

3. Solvent

The substance that dissolves the solute is called the solvent.

It provides the continuous medium in which solute particles disperse.

Example: In salt water, water is the solvent.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: The component present in greater amount is usually designated as the solvent.
4

4. Types of Solutions

Solutions can exist in different physical states depending on the state of the solute and solvent.

Key Types & Examples:
• Solid in Liquid: Salt in water
• Liquid in Liquid: Alcohol in water
• Gas in Liquid: CO₂ in soft drink
• Gas in Gas: Air (N₂, O₂, etc.)
• Solid in Solid: Brass (Cu + Zn alloy)
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Brass and bronze are solid solutions (alloys). Air is a gaseous solution.
5

5. Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a particular temperature.

Simple idea: Solubility tells us how much solute can dissolve.

Example: ~36g of NaCl dissolves in 100g of water at 20°C.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Solubility is temperature-dependent and usually measured in g of solute per 100g of solvent.
6

6. Unsaturated Solution

An unsaturated solution contains less than the maximum amount of dissolved solute.

More solute can still dissolve at that temperature without changing external conditions.

Example: Adding 10g of salt into 100g water (it can hold 36g, so it remains unsaturated).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Adding more solute to an unsaturated solution results in complete dissolution.
7

7. Saturated Solution

A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at a particular temperature.

Adding more solute will leave some undissolved at the bottom (dynamic equilibrium between dissolved and undissolved solute).

Example: Adding 40g NaCl to 100g water at 20°C (36g dissolves, 4g stays at the bottom).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Saturated = maximum dissolved solute in equilibrium with undissolved solute.
8

8. Supersaturated Solution

A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than normally possible at that temperature.

It is prepared by heating, saturating, and cooling slowly without disturbance.

Key property: It is highly unstable. Adding a tiny seed crystal or scratching the glass causes rapid crystallization!
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Supersaturated solutions are metastable and easily precipitate excess solute upon disturbance.
9

9. Concentration

Concentration tells us how much solute is present in a certain amount of solution or solvent.

• Dilute: Relatively small amount of solute.
• Concentrated: Relatively large amount of solute.

Units: Molarity, Molality, Mole Fraction, %, and PPM.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Dilute and concentrated are qualitative terms; Molarity and Molality are quantitative.
10

10. Molarity

Molarity (M) is the number of moles of solute present in one litre of solution.

Molarity = moles of solute / volume of solution in litres

Unit: mol/L, mol/dm³, or M

Formula expansion: M = (Mass in g / Molar Mass) × (1000 / Volume in mL)
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Remember: Molarity uses volume of solution and changes with temperature.
11

11. Molality

Molality (m) is the number of moles of solute present in one kilogram of solvent.

Molality = moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg

Unit: mol/kg or m

Formula expansion: m = (Mass of solute in g / Molar Mass) × (1000 / Mass of solvent in g)
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Remember: Molality uses mass of solvent and is independent of temperature.
12

12. Molarity vs Molality

A very frequent comparison in MDCAT entrance exams:

• Molarity: Moles / Litre of solution | Uses volume | Changes with temperature | Unit: mol/L
• Molality: Moles / kg of solvent | Uses mass | Independent of temperature | Unit: mol/kg
🚨
MDCAT Trap: Do not confuse solution and solvent. For dilute aqueous solutions at 4°C, Molarity ≈ Molality because 1L water ≈ 1kg.
13

13. Mole Fraction

Mole fraction (X) is the ratio of the moles of one component to the total moles of all components in the mixture.

Mole fraction = moles of component / total moles

It has no unit (dimensionless quantity).

Binary solution: X₁ + X₂ = 1
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Sum of mole fractions of all components is always equal to 1. Temperature independent!
14

14. Percentage Concentration

Three common ways to express percentage:

• Mass % (m/m): (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 100
• Volume % (v/v): (volume of solute / volume of solution) × 100
• Mass/Volume % (m/v): (mass of solute in g / volume of solution in mL) × 100

Example: 5% (w/w) NaCl solution contains 5g NaCl in 100g of solution (95g water).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: m/m is temperature independent; m/v and v/v depend on temperature.
15

15. Parts Per Million (ppm)

ppm is used for expressing very small (trace) amounts of solute.

ppm = (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 10⁶

For dilute aqueous solutions: 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg of solute / litre of solution

Applications: Water pollution, heavy metals, fluoride levels in drinking water.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: 1 ppm = 1 mg/L = 1 μg/mL in dilute water solutions.
16

16. Dilution

Dilution means making a solution less concentrated by adding more solvent.

• Amount of solute stays the same.
• Total volume increases.
• Molar concentration decreases.

Important Formula: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Number of millimoles before dilution = number of millimoles after dilution.
17

17. Solvation & Hydration

Solvation is the process in which solvent molecules surround and interact with solute particles.

When the solvent is water, this process is specifically called hydration.

Example: In NaCl solution, water's partial negative O atoms surround Na⁺, and partial positive H atoms surround Cl⁻.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Hydration energy must overcome lattice energy for an ionic solid to dissolve spontaneously.
18

18. Electrolytes

Electrolytes produce mobile ions in solution and allow the solution to conduct electricity.

• Strong Electrolytes: Almost completely ionize (HCl, NaOH, NaCl, H₂SO₄).
• Weak Electrolytes: Only partially ionize (CH₃COOH, NH₄OH, HF).

Example: NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) (100% dissociation)
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Strong electrolytes cause larger colligative effects due to higher ion counts.
19

19. Non-Electrolytes

Non-electrolytes do not produce ions in solution and do not conduct electricity.

They dissolve as neutral intact molecules.

Examples: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), Sucrose, Urea, Ethyl alcohol.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: For non-electrolytes, the van 't Hoff factor i = 1 (1 mole dissolves into 1 mole of particles).
20

20. Solubility of Gases

Gas solubility in liquids is strongly influenced by temperature and pressure:

• Pressure ↑: Gas solubility increases.
• Temperature ↑: Gas solubility generally decreases.

Example: A warm soft drink loses dissolved CO₂ much more quickly than a cold one.
🚨
MDCAT Trap: Do not confuse solid solute temp effect (usually increases) with gas solute temp effect (decreases).
21

21. Henry's Law

At constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid.

C = k × P  (or Solubility ∝ Pressure)

Real-world: CO₂ remains dissolved in sealed bottles under high pressure. Opening the cap releases pressure, causing effervescence.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Henry's law applies to gases that do not chemically react with the solvent.
22

22. Vapour Pressure

Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid phase in a closed container at a given temperature.

Liquids with stronger intermolecular forces have lower vapour pressure.

Comparison: Diethyl ether (weak forces) has higher vapour pressure than water (strong hydrogen bonding).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Vapour pressure increases exponentially with temperature. Boiling occurs when VP = atmospheric pressure.
23

23. Non-Volatile Solute

A non-volatile solute is one that does not evaporate easily (has negligible vapour pressure).

When added to a solvent, solute particles occupy surface area, so fewer solvent molecules can escape into the vapour phase.

Result: Adding non-volatile solute → Vapour pressure decreases (Lowering of vapour pressure).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Lowering of vapour pressure is the primary driver for boiling point elevation and freezing point depression.
24

24. Raoult's Law

For an ideal solution, the partial vapour pressure of a component is equal to the product of its mole fraction and the vapour pressure of the pure component.

P = X × P°

Key idea: Higher mole fraction → greater vapour pressure contribution.

Relative lowering of VP: (P° - P) / P° = X_solute
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Raoult's Law applies strictly to ideal solutions with similar intermolecular forces.
25

25. Colligative Properties

Colligative properties depend only on the number of dissolved solute particles, not on their chemical identity.

Four major colligative properties:

1. Lowering of vapour pressure
2. Elevation of boiling point
3. Depression of freezing point
4. Osmotic pressure

⭐
High Yield: More dissolved particles → stronger colligative effect! 1M AlCl₃ (4 ions) > 1M CaCl₂ (3 ions) > 1M NaCl (2 ions) > 1M Glucose (1 particle).
26

26. Elevation of Boiling Point

Adding a non-volatile solute raises the boiling point of a solution.

Why? The solute lowers the vapour pressure, so the solution must be heated to a higher temperature to make its vapour pressure equal atmospheric pressure.

ΔTb = Kb × m × i

Rule: Boiling point of Solution > Boiling point of pure solvent (e.g. Salt water boils > 100°C).
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Kb is the ebullioscopic constant; it depends strictly on the solvent.
27

27. Depression of Freezing Point

Adding a solute lowers the freezing point of a solution.

Solute particles disrupt the orderly formation of the solvent crystal lattice, requiring a lower temperature to freeze.

ΔTf = Kf × m × i

Applications: Rock salt spread on icy roads melts ice; ethylene glycol in car radiators acts as antifreeze.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Freezing point of Solution < Freezing point of pure solvent (Freezing point decreases!).
28

28. Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from a dilute solution (high solvent potential) toward a more concentrated solution (low solvent potential).

Easy way to remember: Water moves toward more solute.

Biological role: Water absorption by plant roots and cell turgidity.
🚨
MDCAT Trap: In osmosis, SOLVENT moves, not solute! The membrane blocks solute particles.
29

29. Osmotic Pressure

Osmotic pressure (π) is the minimum hydrostatic pressure that must be applied to the solution to prevent the inward flow of solvent across the semipermeable membrane.

π = i × C × R × T

Higher concentration of dissolved particles → Higher osmotic pressure.

⭐
MDCAT Tip: Osmotic pressure is best suited for determining the molar mass of high-molecular-weight polymers and proteins.
30

30. Reverse Osmosis (RO)

In reverse osmosis, an external pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the concentrated solution.

This forces pure solvent molecules to pass through the semipermeable membrane in the opposite direction (from concentrated to dilute).

Application: Desalination of seawater to produce fresh drinking water.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Applied Pressure > Osmotic Pressure (P_ext > π) is required for RO.
31

31. Colloid

A colloid is a heterogeneous mixture containing particles larger than ordinary solution molecules (1 nm to 1000 nm) but small enough to remain dispersed without settling.

Consists of a dispersed phase and a dispersion medium.

Examples: Milk, Fog, Smoke, Blood, Gelatin, Mayonnaise, Paints.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Colloidal particles pass through normal filter paper but are retained by animal membranes / ultra-filters.
32

32. Tyndall Effect

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by colloidal particles when a beam of light passes through the mixture.

The illuminated path of the beam becomes clearly visible.

Examples: Headlight beams visible in fog; sunlight streaming through dusty room air or forest canopy.
⭐
MDCAT: Tyndall effect → Colloid & Suspension (True solutions show NO Tyndall effect).
33

33. Suspension

A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture containing relatively large particles (> 1000 nm) that do not dissolve and settle down when left undisturbed.

Particles are visible to the naked eye or under a standard light microscope.

Examples: Muddy water, chalk in water, milk of magnesia, flour in water.
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Suspension particles can be easily separated by ordinary filter paper.
34

34. Solution vs Colloid vs Suspension

Complete comparison matrix for MDCAT classification questions:

• Solution: < 1 nm | Homogeneous | Never settles | No Tyndall effect | Salt in water
• Colloid: 1 - 1000 nm | Heterogeneous | Does not settle | Strong Tyndall effect | Milk, Fog
• Suspension: > 1000 nm | Heterogeneous | Settles on standing | Scatters / blocks light | Muddy water
⭐
MDCAT Tip: Memorize particle sizes: True Solution < 1 nm < Colloid < 1000 nm < Suspension.
