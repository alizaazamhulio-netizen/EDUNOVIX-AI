Section 01 Atomic Structure

1. Atom and Atomic Nucleus

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical properties.

Particle Symbol / Charge Location Approx. Mass MDCAT Key Note
Proton +1e (+1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) Inside Nucleus 1 u (1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) Determines element identity ($Z$)
Neutron 0 (Neutral) Inside Nucleus 1 u (1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) Slightly heavier than proton; acts as nuclear glue
Electron −1e (−1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) Outside Nucleus (Orbitals) 1/1836 u (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg) Equal to protons in a neutral atom

The Nucleus

The nucleus is the tiny, extremely dense, positively charged central core of the atom. It contains protons and neutrons, collectively termed nucleons.

⭐
MDCAT Trap Alert: Electrons are NOT present inside the nucleus under normal conditions! In $\beta^-$ decay, the emitted electron is produced at the moment of decay by neutron disintegration ($n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}$), not ejected from a nuclear orbital.
Section 02 Nuclear Constants

2. Atomic Number $Z$

The atomic number ($Z$) is the total number of protons present in the nucleus.

$$Z = \text{Number of Protons}$$

For any neutral atom:

$$\text{Number of Protons} = \text{Number of Electrons} = Z$$
💡 Concrete Example: Carbon ($Z = 6$)

Carbon has $Z = 6$. Therefore, in a neutral carbon atom: 6 Protons in nucleus and 6 Electrons revolving around the nucleus.

⭐
MDCAT Trap: The atomic number $Z$ is the unique fingerprint that identifies the chemical element. If $Z$ changes, the element transforms into a different element (transmutation).
Section 03 Mass Calculations

3. Mass Number $A$

The mass number ($A$) is the total sum of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus.

$$A = Z + N \implies N = A - Z$$
$A$ = Mass Number (Nucleons) $Z$ = Proton Number $N$ = Neutron Number
💡 MDCAT Calculation Example: Sodium Nucleus $_{11}^{23}\text{Na}$
• Protons: $Z = 11$
• Mass number: $A = 23$
• Neutrons: $N = A - Z = 23 - 11 = \mathbf{12\text{ neutrons}}$
• Electrons in neutral sodium atom: $\mathbf{11\text{ electrons}}$
Section 04 Isotopes

4. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same atomic number ($Z$) but different mass numbers ($A$) due to differing numbers of neutrons ($N$).

Protium ($^1\text{H}$)
1 Proton, 0 Neutrons
Ordinary Hydrogen ($A=1, Z=1$)
Deuterium ($^2\text{H}$)
1 Proton, 1 Neutron
Heavy Hydrogen ($A=2, Z=1$)
Tritium ($^3\text{H}$)
1 Proton, 2 Neutrons
Radioactive Hydrogen ($A=3, Z=1$)
📌
Remember: Isotopes have identical chemical properties (same electron configuration / $Z$) but different physical and nuclear properties (different masses, different nuclear stability).
Section 05 Forces in Nucleus

5. Nuclear Stability

A nucleus is stable when internal binding forces overcome destabilizing electrostatic repulsion.

1. Strong Nuclear Force (Attractive)

Extremely strong, attractive force operating at short range ($\approx 10^{-15}\text{ m}$). Acts equally between $p-p$, $n-n$, and $p-n$. Holds nucleons tightly together.

2. Electrostatic Repulsion (Disruptive)

Coulomb repulsive force between positively charged protons ($+1e$). Long-range force that grows rapidly with larger $Z$ ($F \propto Z^2$).

For heavy nuclei ($Z > 82$, e.g. Lead, Bismuth, Uranium), electrostatic repulsion overpowers the strong nuclear force, leading to spontaneous decay (radioactivity).

Section 07 Nature of Decay

7. Spontaneous and Random Decay

⚡ Spontaneous

Radioactive decay occurs purely by internal nuclear instability. It is completely independent of external conditions such as temperature, pressure, chemical state, or electric/magnetic fields.

🎲 Random

We cannot predict the exact moment when any individual nucleus will disintegrate. However, for a large population of nuclei ($N$), the statistical rate of decay is strictly predictable.

💡
Key MDCAT Distinction: An individual radioactive nucleus has a constant probability of decaying per unit time, regardless of how long it has already existed (it does not "age").
Section 08 Decay Rate

8. Activity / Rate of Decay ($A$)

The activity ($A$) of a sample is the number of radioactive disintegrations occurring per unit time.

$$A = -\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t} = \lambda N$$
$A$ = Activity $\lambda$ = Decay Constant $N$ = Number of Undecayed Nuclei

SI Unit of Activity

The SI unit is the Becquerel ($\text{Bq}$).

$$1\text{ Bq} = 1\text{ disintegration (decay) per second}$$

Historic Unit: $1\text{ Curie (Ci)} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\text{ Bq}$.

Section 09 Decay Constant

9. Decay Constant ($\lambda$)

The decay constant ($\lambda$) is the constant fraction or probability per unit time for a nucleus to decay. Unit: $\text{s}^{-1}$ or $\text{day}^{-1}$ or $\text{yr}^{-1}$.

$$\lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} \iff T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda}$$
⭐
High-Yield MDCAT Trap: $\lambda$ and $T_{1/2}$ are inversely proportional!
  • • Large $\lambda$ $\implies$ rapid decay $\implies$ Short Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$)
  • • Small $\lambda$ $\implies$ slow decay $\implies$ Long Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$)
Section 10 Half-Life Concept

10. Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$)

The half-life is the time required for exactly half ($50\%$) of the radioactive nuclei present in a sample to disintegrate.

$$N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \quad \text{where } n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}}$$
Elapsed Half-Lives ($n$) Fraction Remaining Percentage Undecayed Percentage Decayed
0 (Initial $t=0$) $N_0$ 100% 0%
1 ($t = T_{1/2}$) $\frac{1}{2} N_0$ 50% 50%
2 ($t = 2T_{1/2}$) $\frac{1}{4} N_0$ 25% 75%
3 ($t = 3T_{1/2}$) $\frac{1}{8} N_0$ 12.5% 87.5%
4 ($t = 4T_{1/2}$) $\frac{1}{16} N_0$ 6.25% 93.75%
5 ($t = 5T_{1/2}$) $\frac{1}{32} N_0$ 3.125% 96.875%
Section 11 Standard Problem

11. Half-Life Numerical Walkthrough

📌 MDCAT Past Pattern Question

A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 4 hours. If the initial count is 800 nuclei, how many nuclei remain undecayed after 12 hours?

Step 1 Calculate number of elapsed half-lives: $$n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{12\text{ hours}}{4\text{ hours}} = 3\text{ half-lives}$$
Step 2 Apply fraction formula: $$N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 800 \times \frac{1}{8} = 100\text{ nuclei}$$
Answer Remaining Nuclei ($N$) = 100 (Decayed = $800 - 100 = 700$ nuclei)
Section 12 Exponential Law

12. Radioactive Decay Law

Radioactive nuclei and overall activity decrease strictly according to an exponential decay curve.

$$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t} \quad \text{and} \quad A(t) = A_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}$$
$N_0, A_0$ = Initial nuclei and activity at $t = 0$ $N, A$ = Value after time $t$ $\lambda$ = Decay constant

Because the curve is asymptotic, the sample theoretically takes infinite time to reach zero atoms ($N \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$).

Section 13 Balancing Equations

13. Nuclear Equations & Transformation Rules

In every nuclear reaction, both Mass Number ($A$) and Atomic Number (Charge $Z$) must be conserved.

1. Alpha ($\alpha$) Decay $A \rightarrow A - 4, \quad Z \rightarrow Z - 2$
$$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z-2}^{A-4}\text{Y} + _{2}^{4}\text{He}$$

Example: $_{92}^{238}\text{U} \longrightarrow _{90}^{234}\text{Th} + _{2}^{4}\text{He}$

2. Beta-Minus ($\beta^-$) Decay $A \rightarrow \text{Same}, \quad Z \rightarrow Z + 1$
$$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z+1}^{A}\text{Y} + _{-1}^{0}e + \bar{\nu}$$

Example: $_{6}^{14}\text{C} \longrightarrow _{7}^{14}\text{N} + _{-1}^{0}e + \bar{\nu}$ (Antineutrino)

3. Gamma ($\gamma$) Emission $A \rightarrow \text{Same}, \quad Z \rightarrow \text{Same}$
$$_{Z}^{A}\text{X}^* \longrightarrow _{Z}^{A}\text{X} + \gamma$$

Example: Excited $^{60m}_{27}\text{Co} \longrightarrow _{27}^{60}\text{Co} + \gamma$ (De-excitation)

⭐
SUPER MDCAT TRAP SUMMARY:
Alpha: $A-4$, $Z-2$ Beta-minus: $A$ same, $Z+1$ Gamma: $A$ same, $Z$ same
Section 14 Biological Effects

14. Biological Effects of Radiation

Ionizing radiation strips electrons from atoms, creating free radicals and damaging biological tissue and nucleic acids.

Cellular Damage

Breaks DNA chemical bonds, leading to cell death or faulty gene mutations causing cancer or genetic hereditary abnormalities.

Determinants of Severity

  • Radiation Type: High ionizing ($\alpha$) is dangerous internally.
  • Absorbed Dose: Measured in Gray (Gy) and Sievert (Sv).
  • Exposure Duration & Tissue Sensitivity: Rapidly dividing cells (bone marrow, GI tract) are most vulnerable.
Section 15 Medical Physics

15. Medical Uses of Radiation

Controlled radiation is one of the most powerful diagnostic and therapeutic tools in modern medicine.

Diagnostic Imaging

X-Rays & CT Scans

Differential absorption through bone vs soft tissue produces sharp skeletal and internal organ imaging.

Oncology

Radiotherapy (Cobalt-60)

Targeted beams of high-energy gamma rays or protons destroy cancerous tumor cells while sparing healthy tissue.

Radioisotope Tracers

Technetium-99m & Iodine-131

Tc-99m ($T_{1/2} = 6\text{h}$) is used for organ scans; I-131 is used for thyroid imaging and treating hyperthyroidism/thyroid cancer.

Sterilization

Medical Equipment Sterilization

Gamma irradiation destroys bacteria, viruses, and spores on pre-packaged surgical tools and syringes without heat.

📐 Nuclear Physics MDCAT Formula Vault

Every formula, conversion factor, constant, and rule required for 100% accuracy in MDCAT physics numericals.

1. Mass & Nucleon Formulas
Mass Number ($A$): $$A = Z + N$$
Neutron Number ($N$): $$N = A - Z$$
Nucleon Fraction: $$\text{Protons} = Z, \quad \text{Electrons} = Z \text{ (neutral)}$$
⭐ Note: Mass number $A$ is an integer; atomic mass $m$ has units in atomic mass units ($\text{u}$).
2. Activity & Decay Constant
Rate of Decay (Activity): $$A = \left|-\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}\right| = \lambda N$$
Decay Constant ($\lambda$): $$\lambda = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{\ln(2)}{T_{1/2}}$$
Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$): $$T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$
⭐ SI Unit: $1\text{ Becquerel (Bq)} = 1\text{ decay/s}$. $1\text{ Curie (Ci)} = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\text{ Bq}$.
3. Half-Life & Fractional Decay
Number of Half-Lives ($n$): $$n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}}$$
Remaining Nuclei ($N$): $$N = N_0 \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{N_0}{2^n}$$
Decayed Fraction ($\Delta N$): $$\Delta N = N_0 - N = N_0 \left[1 - \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\right]$$
Exponential Law: $$N = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}, \quad A = A_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}$$
4. Nuclear Decay Shift Rules
Alpha ($\alpha$) Decay: $$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z-2}^{A-4}\text{Y} + _{2}^{4}\text{He}$$
Beta-Minus ($\beta^-$) Decay: $$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \longrightarrow _{Z+1}^{A}\text{Y} + _{-1}^{0}e + \bar{\nu}$$
Gamma ($\gamma$) De-excitation: $$_{Z}^{A}\text{X}^* \longrightarrow _{Z}^{A}\text{X} + \gamma$$
⭐ Alpha reduces $A$ by 4, $Z$ by 2. Beta preserves $A$, increments $Z$ by 1. Gamma leaves both unchanged.
5. Physical & Fundamental Constants
Elementary Charge ($e$): $$1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$$
Atomic Mass Unit ($1\text{ u}$): $$1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} \approx 931.5\text{ MeV}$$
Proton Rest Mass ($m_p$): $$1.6726 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} \approx 1.007276\text{ u}$$
Neutron Rest Mass ($m_n$): $$1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} \approx 1.008665\text{ u}$$
Electron Rest Mass ($m_e$): $$9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg} \approx 0.000548\text{ u}$$
